# Chez Colette — how your script machine works

Plain English. Read once, then keep it for reference.

---

## The two files, and what each one does

You have **one Claude Project** with two pieces in it.

| File | Where it goes | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| `PROJECT-INSTRUCTIONS.txt` | Project **instructions** box | The rules. *How* to write. |
| `COLETTE-REFERENCE.txt` | Project **knowledge** | The facts. *What is true.* |

Plus your **transcripts** in knowledge — the sample channels whose structure Colette borrows.

The split matters: if a script ever gets a fact wrong, you fix the reference file.
If a script ever gets the *shape* wrong, you fix the instructions.

---

## How to use it

Start a new chat in the project and give it three things:

```
TITLE:      The exact title of the video you're making
TRANSCRIPT: Paste the reference video's transcript
LENGTH:     e.g. "14 minutes" or "2,450 words"
```

That's it. It writes the script and nothing else — no preamble, no notes,
straight into your teleprompter.

**One tip:** if you tell it what the last video covered and which
"who profits" target you used, it won't repeat either.

## The length tiers — the thing that changed most

Every Colette script carries a fixed set of pieces: the cold open, the identity
block, the pitch, the honesty block, the "who profits" section, the close.
Together these cost **1,100 to 1,600 words before a single tip is written.**

That's why length isn't just a word count — it decides how much room is left
for actual content, and therefore how many items you can have.

| Tier | Runtime | Words | Format | Items | Callbacks | Comment bait |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **S** | 8–11 min | 1,500–1,925 | single method | 3–4 steps | 2 | 1 question |
| **M** | 12–15 min | 2,100–2,625 | method or short list | 5–7 | 3 | 2 questions |
| **L** | 16–22 min | 2,800–3,850 | numbered list | 8–12 | 4 | 2 questions |
| **XL** | 23–30 min | 4,025–5,250 | numbered list | 12–18 | 5 | 2 questions |

**The pitch is always exactly one**, at 20–25% of the way in. Never at the end.

The callback numbers aren't arbitrary. Together with the pitch they space the
book mentions **one every 3 to 5 minutes** in every tier — often enough to sell,
never often enough to feel like an advert.

**Below 1,500 words the format cannot be delivered honestly.** It will still
write you something and tell you in one line that the runtime was too short.

## Turning a long video into a short one

You paste a 25-minute reference and ask for 12 minutes. It no longer just
lops off the end. It runs seven steps:

1. **Inventory** — lists every distinct idea in the reference.
2. **Discard** — drops anything Colette can't honestly stand behind, and
   anything that doesn't serve your title.
3. **Merge** — *this is the important one.* Ideas that share one underlying
   mechanism become **one** item built on the strongest example. Three separate
   points about a room quietly demanding effort become one item about a room
   that makes your nervous system work. You lose a heading, you keep the value.
4. **Rank** — what survives is ordered by how surprising it is, then by how
   fast the viewer can act on it tonight.
5. **Relocate the offcuts** — nothing good is binned. The best leftover becomes
   the bonus item; two or three become one-liners in the closing laws; the
   strongest dropped theme becomes your next-video tease.
6. **Re-pace from zero** — it rebuilds timing against your tier, rather than
   shrinking the original's proportions.
7. **Check what was lost** — if a viewer of the original would miss something,
   it goes back in at step 5.

It also strips the padding: restated points, second and third examples, long
analogies, the other channel's anecdotes, and "and that's why" recaps.

## Titles that promise science

If your title says **neuroscience**, **psychology** or **the science of**, the
script has to deliver it — but Colette is a decorator, not a scientist.

So the reference file now has **Part 8: five named effects she may cite**, with
accurate attributions:

- Prospect and refuge — Jay Appleton, 1975
- The Diderot effect — Diderot, 1769; named by Grant McCracken, 1988
- Sensory gating — described only, no researcher attached
- The mere exposure effect — Robert Zajonc, 1968
- Attention restoration theory — Rachel and Stephen Kaplan, 1989

**The rule:** the craft explanation comes first and carries the weight. The
effect is named afterwards, as an aside — *"the name they gave it later."*
One per script, two at the very most. If a title implies research that isn't
on that list, it writes the craft version and tells you in one line.

## The one thing to protect

Page numbers in the book callbacks. The reference file has a **complete map of
every real page** in the 204-page book. Nothing else may be cited. A buyer who
turns to a page that doesn't exist is the most expensive mistake this channel
can make.

If you ever edit the book, update that map in the same hour.
