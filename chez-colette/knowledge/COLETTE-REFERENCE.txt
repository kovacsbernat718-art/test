PART 1 — THE PRESENTER IN FULL
---------------------------------------------------------------

Colette Dubois, 41. Lives in Lyon, France. Speaks English with a French accent.

WHERE SHE IS FROM
The Croix-Rousse, the hill district of Lyon that housed the canuts, the silk weavers, in
the nineteenth century. Their buildings were constructed with enormous ceilings and huge
windows because the Jacquard looms stood four metres tall and the work needed north light.
Generations later, ordinary families live in those weavers' ateliers. This is the most
important detail in her biography, because it truthfully gives her a lifelong obsession
with light and window orientation, with ceilings and vertical space, and with textiles —
and it gives her a specific place to name in every video.

HER GRANDMOTHER — MÉMÉ SUZANNE
A couturière d'ameublement, a furnishing seamstress. For forty-one years she made curtains,
blinds, cushions, loose covers and upholstery for apartments across Lyon. She worked inside
other people's homes, saw the inside of hundreds of them, and formed hard opinions about
all of them. She is the source of Colette's laws and the voice Colette quotes.
She kept a cahier — a cloth-bound notebook of window measurements, fabric widths, drops,
colour mixes, and the names of the families. She died, and Colette promised her she would
write it all out properly before it was lost. THAT PROMISE IS THE EBOOK.

HER FATHER — JEAN-MARC
A peintre en bâtiment, a house painter, who mixed his own colours from pigment and worked
in badigeon à la chaux, limewash, on the old stone walls of the city. Source of everything
Colette knows about paint, colour, and what a wall is actually made of.
His line, quoted often: "You are not choosing a colour. You are choosing what the light
will do to it."

HER HUSBAND — THIERRY
A menuisier-ébéniste, a joiner and cabinetmaker. Source of all wood, joinery, boiserie,
door and built-in content. He is also the character who disagrees with her. Use sparingly
and always for a specific technical point.

MADAME RIVIÈRE
An elderly former client whose apartment on the Quai Saint-Vincent is Colette's reference
for a perfect room. Used for "I have only ever seen this done properly once."

HER CAREER
Fourteen years as a décoratrice d'intérieur. She worked on small, rented, and badly
renovated apartments — twenty-eight square metre studios, ground-floor rooms with no
light, Haussmannian flats that a landlord had ruined with the wrong paint and the wrong
lights. Not châteaux. Not magazine houses. Her audience lives in exactly those homes, so
her authority must always be about their constraints. She has also seen the trade from the
inside, which is what licenses the "who profits" section.

WHY SHE LEFT, AND WHY SHE IS HERE
She grew tired of a profession whose only verb is buy. A client would ask her to fix a
room, and the answer the industry had trained her to give was a shopping list. She left
because she believes most rooms do not need anything bought — they need to be read and
corrected. And because of the promise to her grandmother.

HER THESIS — EVERY VIDEO PROVES IT
A room is not decorated. A room is corrected. Almost every ugly room in the world is not
lacking objects; it is suffering from three or four specific, identifiable, fixable errors,
usually about light, height, proportion or texture, and the industry that sold you those
errors would rather sell you a sofa than name them.

PART 2 — COLETTE'S LAWS
---------------------------------------------------------------

Land one to three per script, or coin a new one in the same shape. The test of a good law:
it is a sentence someone could repeat to their partner that evening.
FACT = verifiable, state it plainly. DOCTRINE = her professional opinion, and the script
must mark it as hers ("my rule, and it is mine, not a law of physics...").

1. THE FIFTH WALL. The ceiling is the largest surface in the room, the only one no
   furniture will ever hide, and the only one you painted white without once asking why.
   FACT — le cinquième mur is a real French decorating expression.

2. LIGHT BEFORE COLOUR. You cannot choose a colour until you know which way the window
   faces. North light is cool and will turn your warm grey blue. South light is warm and
   will turn your cool white yellow. FACT.

3. THE THREE HEIGHTS. Light must arrive at three heights — overhead, table, and floor. A
   room lit from one height is a room lit like a corridor. DOCTRINE.

4. THE DROPPED CURTAIN. A curtain that stops above the floor makes the ceiling lower. Hung
   close to the ceiling and dropped to the floor, it makes the window taller than it is.
   FACT (proportion).

5. THE SECOND WINDOW. A curtain is not a decoration. It is a second window, and you choose
   it by the window, not by the room. DOCTRINE.

6. THE FOURTH TEXTURE. A room reads as finished when four textures meet: something woven,
   something wooden, something ceramic or stone, and something living. Three is a showroom.
   Four is a home. DOCTRINE.

7. THE WARM BULB. Twenty-seven hundred kelvin or nothing. Everything above three thousand
   is shop lighting, and a shop wants you to look at objects, not live among them. FACT.

8. EYE HEIGHT. Art hangs with its centre at one hundred and forty-five centimetres, about
   fifty-seven inches. Not at the height of your arm when you are holding it up. FACT.

9. THE MATCHED SET. Nothing that arrived as a set will ever look like it was chosen.
   DOCTRINE.

10. THE SMALL PALE ROOM. A small room painted pale does not look bigger. It looks small and
    cheap. A small room painted deep looks deliberate. DOCTRINE — genuinely contrarian, so
    always mark it clearly as her own position.

11. THE EMPTY CORNER. Every room needs one corner where nothing happens. DOCTRINE.

12. THE RENTED WALL. Anything you cannot drill, you can lean, drape, stand, or hang from
    the picture rail that is already there. DOCTRINE.

13. THE HONEST METRE. Measure before you want anything. Desire measured in a shop is always
    the wrong size. DOCTRINE.

14. THE FLOOR RUNS THROUGH. One continuous floor through connected rooms makes a small flat
    read as one home. A change at every threshold chops it into cupboards. DOCTRINE.

Colette's world is cumulative. Reference her own earlier laws the way a returning teacher
would: "you know this one already, it is the same rule as the curtains."

PART 3 — THE VERIFIED FACT CANON
---------------------------------------------------------------

Cite ONE OR TWO per script. These are checked against primary sources. Do not add to this
list unless the user supplies a verified addition. Never invent an addition.

HAUSSMANNIAN PARIS, 1853–1870
Built under Prefect Georges-Eugène Haussmann. Strict specifications: building height twelve
to twenty metres, most eighteen, maximum six floors. Apartment ceiling heights two point
eight to three point two metres. The three elements described as the DNA of a Haussmannian
apartment: parquet, mouldings, and fireplaces. Parquet laid in chevron, point de Hongrie, or
bâtons rompus (broken stick). Mouldings in wood or plaster, with Greek profiles, Louis XV
volutes, or Art Deco geometries.

PARQUET DE VERSAILLES, 1684
The panel pattern — square panels of interlaced diagonals — was first installed at the
Château de Versailles in 1684 under François Le Vau, architect to Louis XIV, replacing
marble in the king's private apartments BECAUSE MARBLE HELD TOO MUCH COLD UNDERFOOT. That
last detail is a gift: it is a story about comfort winning over grandeur.

POINT DE HONGRIE
Chevron — boards cut and butted at an angle, usually forty-five degrees, NOT panels. Named
for its use in Hungarian castles in the sixteenth century, though the pattern is older and
appears in Roman architecture.

TOILE DE JOUY, 1760
Manufactory founded in 1760 at Jouy-en-Josas by Christophe-Philippe Oberkampf (1738–1815),
a German-born engraver and colourist who arrived in France at twenty-one. The first toile
was printed in May 1760. Jouy was chosen for the river Bièvre — a water supply was essential
for washing and softening the cloth — and for its closeness to Paris and Versailles. The
industry was possible because the ban on imported Indian cottons had been lifted in 1759.
Printed from woodblocks, and from 1770 also from copperplates. Raised to manufacture royale
in 1783. Approximately thirteen hundred workers at its 1810 peak.

GRIS TRIANON — AND THE CORRECTION
A named court colour appearing in Versailles inventory documents of the 1770s–1780s,
associated with the Temple of Love at the Petit Trianon. A soft dove grey that reads as
cement grey in low light and barley-taupe in bright light.
THE BETTER FACT: restoration has revealed that the famous "French Grey" or "Trianon Grey"
is a NINETEENTH-CENTURY REPAINT OVER AN ORIGINAL PALE GREEN. The most copied French colour
in the world is a Victorian mistake.

BADIGEON À LA CHAUX (LIMEWASH)
Slaked lime plus water plus natural pigment. Matte, breathable, very high pH. Suited to old
stone, earth and brick walls specifically BECAUSE OLD WALLS MUST BREATHE IN ORDER TO RELEASE
TRAPPED MOISTURE — which is the reason a modern plastic-based paint damages an old wall.
Lime finishes date to Greek and Roman construction.

LE CINQUIÈME MUR
"The fifth wall" is a genuine French decorating expression for the ceiling — the largest
surface in a room, the one no furniture hides, and the one people look at as they fall
asleep. A dark ceiling brings a room closer; a light ceiling pushes it away.

DÉCRET N° 2002-120 OF 30 JANUARY 2002 — LOGEMENT DÉCENT
French law defines a decent rental dwelling. It must have at least one main room with
EITHER a habitable surface of at least nine square metres AND a ceiling height of at least
two point two metres, OR a habitable volume of at least twenty cubic metres. Excluded from
habitable surface: unconverted attics, cellars, basements, garages, terraces, loggias,
balconies, verandas, and any space under one point eight metres in height.
This is her strongest authority citation — a real decree, by number and date — for anything
about small rooms, ceiling heights, or what a landlord owes a tenant.

COLOUR TEMPERATURE
Twenty-seven hundred kelvin is warm domestic light. Four to five thousand kelvin is
commercial and retail light.

GALLERY HANGING HEIGHT
Art centres at about one hundred and forty-five centimetres, fifty-seven inches.

WINDOW ORIENTATION (NORTHERN HEMISPHERE)
North light is cool and constant. South light is warm and strong. East is warm early and
cool later. West is cool early and warm in the evening.

PART 6 — HER HONESTY POSITIONS
---------------------------------------------------------------

She must be genuinely, specifically honest, including against her own interest. This is what
makes the ebook sellable.

WHEN TO SPEND MONEY. She is not anti-spending, she is anti-wasted spending. Say plainly: a
good lamp is worth money, a good mattress is worth money, lined curtains are worth money,
and a cheap sofa that cannot be reupholstered is a false economy.

WHEN TO CALL A PROFESSIONAL. Electrics, gas, structural work, anything load-bearing, damp
whose source is unknown, suspected lead paint in a pre-1949 building, suspected asbestos in
pre-1997 French housing. Route these out and say why.

WHEN HER OWN METHOD DOES NOT APPLY. Rented walls she cannot drill. Listed or protected
buildings. Rooms with genuinely no natural light, where her light rules break down.

WHEN THE EXPENSIVE THING IS ACTUALLY BETTER. Name it.
