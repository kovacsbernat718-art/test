You carve the pumpkin, string the lights, tape bats to the door, and like everyone, learn Halloween’s eleven decorating lessons one night too late. Most of what goes wrong shows at the one hour that counts, as the first child comes up your path. Lesson four is that bat on your door. And the last lesson explains why everything orange on your porch turns grey at dusk.

Lesson one: big shadows from small props. The scariest thing on your house can be a shadow, and a toy that fits in a shoebox throws one taller than your front door. A shadow’s size is set by two distances, light to wall and light to object, so whatever sits close to the bulb grows. Put a spotlight on the ground three metres, about ten feet, from the house, stand a plastic spider twenty-five centimetres across, ten inches, a foot in front of it, and a spider two and a half metres wide, eight feet, climbs your wall. Low light adds menace: sun, moon and ceiling all light faces from above, so a beam rising from the ground throws every shadow upward, the trick behind a flashlight under the chin. One LED spot gives a crisper edge than a floodlight, and a stake-in one is usually under twenty dollars and ten minutes to aim. Tonight, set a flashlight on the floor with a small toy just in front. And the pumpkin on your step, carved on the wrong day, can cave in before a single child sees it.

First, if tonight’s shadow climbs your wall, like this video and subscribe: November’s long evenings get lists of their own.

Lesson two: buy early, carve late. A whole pumpkin keeps for weeks, a carved one often less than one. The rind is a waterproof seal, and the first cut opens wet flesh to the air, so every edge dries and every mould spore that lands has a meal. The stem is part of that seal, so a pumpkin lifted by it can snap and rot from the wound. Before you buy, push a thumbnail against the rind: if it dents, the skin never hardened and it won’t keep. Line them up whole from early October, five minutes that cost nothing, and keep the knife away until two or three days before the thirty-first. Where October stays warm, across much of the South and Southwest, a carved pumpkin can slump within days whatever you do, and that’s where I’d spend: a carvable foam pumpkin costs more on the day and pays for itself within a few Halloweens. Before bed, set a phone reminder to carve on the twenty-ninth. When you carve, the layer blocking your light isn’t the one you’d think.

Light runs through this list, and it has a part of its own in Colette’s Home Bible: lamps at floor level, the height most rooms leave empty, and the warmth of a bulb, which shifts every colour it falls on. It’s one tap away in the description, or one scan of the QR code on the screen.

Lesson three: peel the skin for a glowing face. In a pumpkin, the skin stops the light, not the flesh. The flesh is translucent, like thick wax, while the rind is nearly opaque, so wherever you peel off the skin and leave the flesh, the face glows soft amber beside its bright holes, cheekbones without one extra cut. Scrape the inside of the face wall down to about two and a half centimetres, an inch. Then hunt your drawing for islands: any patch of rind not joined to the rest falls out, so an O’s middle or an eye’s pupil needs a bridge of skin. Cut the smallest details first and the big openings last, because each large hole weakens the wall. A small serrated carving saw, a few dollars, pushed straight in and out, beats a wedging kitchen knife and makes a face an hour’s work. Before you carve, mark every island on your drawing with a cross. That paper bat taped to your front door vanishes every night at sunset, and a window explains why.

Lesson four: black decorations need light behind them. As promised, that’s the bat: the eye finds a shape by its edge, and after sunset the door, the porch and the bat merge into one dark with no edge at all. Taped inside a lit window, the same bat becomes a crisp silhouette from across the street. A pale blind lowered behind the cutouts, with a lamp behind it, makes the pane glow evenly like a shadow-puppet screen. Choose shapes in profile, a cat side-on, a witch with hat and nose, since face-on most figures blur into a blob, which is why silhouette portraits were cut that way. Black card works, or a cereal box painted black: fait maison, the words a French menu prints beside a dish made in its own kitchen. A plug-in timer under ten dollars lights the lamp at dusk; the cutting and taping take one evening. After dark, move one bat from the door to a lit window. And the bale on your porch may be feeding next spring’s weeds and every mouse on the street.

Lesson five: buy straw bales, not hay. Most porches call it hay, but hay is grass cut as animal feed, seed heads and all, so by your door it draws mice and drops seed into every flowerbed; straw is the hollow stalk left once a grain crop is harvested, most of its seed already gone. Either kind soaks up rain and holds it. Set straight on wooden boards, a wet bale keeps them damp for weeks and leaves a dark rectangle; on a lawn, a few weeks without light leave a yellow one. Ask the garden centre for straw by name, and stand the bale on two bricks or a scrap of pallet so air moves underneath, ten minutes with spares from the garage. In November, straw pulled apart over a vegetable bed makes clean mulch, where hay would sow grass. Tomorrow morning, tip the bale onto its side and check the ground beneath. A tea light’s flame burns near a thousand degrees Celsius, eighteen hundred Fahrenheit, and it sits inside your pumpkin.

Lesson six: light pumpkins with battery candles. That flame cooks a jack-o’-lantern from the inside, lid first. Its heat rises straight into the lid, so the flesh there browns, softens and smells of baking, and cooked flesh sags and spoils faster than raw. It’s also a naked flame at knee height, on the one night children in loose costumes brush past every porch. A battery tea light runs almost cold, and the better ones carry a timer: press once at dusk and it shines for six hours, rests for eighteen, then lights itself again the next evening. A big pumpkin wants two. If you keep a real candle anyway, cut a chimney hole in the lid right above the flame, and stand the pumpkin well back from the path. Swap in timer tea lights: a box costs around fifteen dollars, and setting them takes a minute. Before supper, drop one battery candle into your pumpkin and judge its glow. One letter printed along your outdoor extension cord decides whether it belongs outside at all.

Lesson seven: weatherproof cords and a safe socket. On an American extension cord, that letter is the W in the code along the jacket, as in S J T W, marking a cord made for outdoors, its covering built for rain and sun where an indoor one can crack and let water in. Then check your outdoor socket for two small buttons marked test and reset: that’s a ground-fault outlet, which cuts the power in a fraction of a second when current escapes through rain or a wet hand. If yours has none, the protection may sit upstream or nowhere at all, and an electrician can tell you which. Never run a cord under a closed door or window, whose edge pinches it and hides the damage. Swap any indoor cord for an outdoor one, a five-minute job for barely more money, and lift every outdoor joint onto a brick or into a snap-shut cover. Later tonight, read the letters along every cord that runs outside. An old white sheet in your linen cupboard makes a fine ghost, as long as no scissors touch it.

Lesson eight: tear your ghost, don’t cut it. A sheet is woven from threads crossing at right angles, so once you snip the hem and pull, it rips along a single thread, leaving a straight, soft fringe that stirs in any breeze, where a scissor line looks machine-made from the street. The sound I remember best from my grandmother’s workroom is linen tearing. Mémé Suzanne nicked the edge with her scissors, gripped both sides and ripped a whole width in one pull, ma chère, because a tear follows one thread where scissors wander. Tie it loosely over a ball for a head and tear the lower half into uneven strips. Hang it from clear fishing line, which vanishes against the night sky so the ghost floats; ten-pound line, about four and a half kilos, holds it easily. A spool costs less than a coffee, and the ghost takes twenty minutes. Right now, nick the hem of an old sheet and tear one strip. Whatever lights up and goes back in its bin with the batteries still in may be dead by next October.

Lesson nine: take batteries out before storing anything. Do it on the first of November, before the lids go on, because a cell left inside for eleven months can ruin the decoration around it. An alkaline cell can slowly vent its electrolyte, which meets the air as the white, furry crust that eats springs and contacts. Anything with a timer or push-button switch keeps sipping current while it looks off, and a cell run completely flat is the likeliest to leak; mixing old and new cells makes it worse, because the fresh ones drive the weakest past empty. Empty a whole bin in five minutes, writing each battery size on tape so next October you buy the right ones. Where crust has formed, a cotton bud dipped in white vinegar or lemon juice dissolves it, gloves on; dry the contacts, then rub them bright with a pencil eraser. Open one battery door on last year’s decorations tonight and look for white. Your glow-in-the-dark skeleton charges better on a grey windowsill than under your warmest lamp.

Lesson ten: glow-in-the-dark pieces need blue light. They charge on blue and violet light, which warm living-room bulbs give out comparatively little of. The pigment stores energy from light, and only the short-wavelength end of the spectrum carries enough per particle to fill that store; yellow and orange light, however bright, barely registers. Even a grey sky is rich in blue, so a few minutes by a window charges a skeleton far better than a warm lamp ever will, and so does a phone flashlight, because a white LED is a blue chip under a yellow coating. Look for strontium aluminate on the pack: it glows faintly for hours, while the older, cheaper zinc sulphide fades within an hour or so. The glow is brightest in its first minutes, so give each piece a free minute under your phone just before the first knock. This evening, charge one piece under your phone and one under a lamp. Find the most orange thing within reach: outdoors at dusk, it’s the first colour your eyes would lose.

Lesson eleven, the last: pair orange with white. Everything orange on your porch turns grey at dusk because your eyes switch to a different set of cells. In daylight you see with cones, which need plenty of light and read colour. As the light fails, rods take over, and rods see no colour and barely respond to red, so reds and oranges sink into dark grey first, while blues, greens and whites hold their shape longest. Trick-or-treating usually starts in that light, l’heure bleue, when the sun is down and the sky hasn’t let go of its glow, so the pumpkins chosen for their colour turn into dull lumps just as the first child reaches your path. The Czech physiologist Jan Purkyně described it about two centuries ago, hence its name, the Purkinje shift. Summer evenings on the Croix-Rousse, at our tall weavers’ windows, I watched the red geraniums on the balcony opposite go almost black, ma chère, while the white ones in the same box kept glowing. Half this list quietly obeys the same rule: the shadow, the glowing skin, the lit window, the cold candle, the charged skeleton and the torn ghost either make light or stay pale enough to hold the last of it. So give every unlit orange decoration a white neighbour, a white pumpkin, a skull or a ghost, and light orange from inside or with a spotlight, ten minutes and the price of one white pumpkin. Tomorrow, fifteen minutes after sunset, walk to the street and see which colour goes first. All eleven boil down to three questions, and dusk is the time to ask them.

Stand at the end of your path and ask them in order. What on my porch makes its own light, and what is only a shape waiting for some? What have I cut, lit or set down wet, and how many days have I given it to spoil? And what will go back in its box on the first of November with the batteries still inside? The shadow is the place to begin: two minutes and a flashlight. In the comments, give me one number: how many days did your last carved pumpkin stand before its face caved in? Then switch off the lights, lay your phone face down on the floor with its flashlight on, and hold one hand a little above it.
