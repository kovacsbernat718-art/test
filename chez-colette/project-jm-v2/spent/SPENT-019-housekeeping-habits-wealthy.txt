Eleven old French housekeeping habits make guests think you’re wealthy, and your table and front door are probably skipping several of them right now. Plenty of the French homes that keep them have ordinary money and plates older than their owners. Number five is the fork beside your plate, and which way up it lies. Number eleven is in your linen cupboard: a stain nobody can see yet.

The first habit is taking the grey lines off your white plates, because very often they aren’t scratches. They’re metal. Glaze is harder than the steel in your knives, so a knife sawing across a plate can leave a smear of itself behind, worst on matte glazes. Run a fingernail across a line: a scratch catches the nail, a metal mark lets it glide. Rub it with a paste of baking soda and a few drops of water on a damp cloth, about a minute a plate for pennies, keeping it off gold rims and painted borders. The lines come back fastest under serrated steak knives, and wherever a plate rests against the base of an aluminium pan in the sink or the dishwasher, so wash your good plates apart from the pans. Tonight, rub one grey line with a fingertip of paste. Now hold your cloudiest glass to a window: one haze washes off, the other never will.

A pinch of baking soda, a minute a plate. Second, learn which cloud is on your glasses, because only one kind ever comes off. Stand the glass in white vinegar for fifteen minutes. If it comes out clear, the haze was hard-water minerals left as the dishwasher dried, and rinse aid keeps them away. If it stays cloudy, the glass itself has been eaten: that’s etching, and nothing brings it back. Etching usually comes from soft water with too much detergent and too much heat; with few minerals to work on, the detergent attacks the glass, and a faint rainbow sheen is the first sign. So halve the detergent if your water is soft, choose a cooler cycle and wash fine crystal by hand, a one-minute change that saves the whole set. And dry a wine glass turning bowl and foot together, because twisting one against the other is how stems snap. Before bed, lift one glass to the lamp and look for a rainbow. The candles beside those glasses were decided, for better or worse, the first evening you lit them.

If yours have sunk into a hollow, the third habit is a long first burn, because a candle remembers it. Wax rarely melts wider than its first pool, so a candle blown out after twenty minutes tunnels down that narrow well ever after, until the wick drowns in wax you paid for. Candle makers allow about an hour for every inch across, two and a half centimetres, until the pool reaches the rim, so a three-inch pillar wants three hours. Before each lighting, snip the wick to about a quarter of an inch, six millimetres, ten seconds with nail scissors; a longer one throws a tall flame that smokes and blackens the glass. A tunnelled candle can often be saved with foil wrapped round the top, middle left open, while you stay in the room. And a white, unburnt wick on a laid table reads as a prop, so light new candles once before guests arrive. This evening, give one new candle its full first burn. Set one plate down on that same table and listen to how it lands.

A knock like that is what the fourth habit silences: a padded cloth under the tablecloth, the layer of a good table no guest sees. Hotels call it a silence cloth: plates and cutlery land on felt instead of wood, so a full table murmurs instead of clattering. It also stops the cloth sliding, keeps heat off the wood, and makes a thin tablecloth hang thick, like damask. A lot of French dining tables keep one on year-round, under whichever cloth is on top. At Madame Rivière’s table on the Quai Saint-Vincent, a full casserole went down without a sound, ma chère, and she lifted her cloth to show me cream felt cut exactly to the wood. Cut yours to the tabletop so only the tablecloth hangs over, ideally twenty to thirty centimetres, eight to twelve inches; a folded flannel sheet takes ten minutes, and a made-to-measure pad, tens of dollars. After supper, fold something soft under the tablecloth and set a plate down again. And the fork you lay on top is, by French rules, the wrong way up.

Number five, as promised, is that fork: on a French table it lies tines down, with the spoon bowl down beside it. Old French silver usually carried the family’s initials or crest on the back of the handle, so forks were laid face down to show them; English silver was engraved on the front and laid face up. Les arts de la table, literally the arts of the table, the French term for everything that goes on a laid table, adds two more rules. Handles line up about an inch, two and a half centimetres, from the edge, and the knife’s blade faces the plate. To my eye, a fork turned over is the quickest sign of a table laid by someone who knew how, because the row of little spikes disappears. So turn yours over and square the handles to the edge, a minute’s work for a whole table. At the next meal, lay one place the French way beside one laid yours. Every dinner plate in your cupboard is cold right now, and it will take the heat from tonight’s food.

Warming the plates is the sixth habit, and it comes from old dining rooms where plates waited in a warmer by the fire. A ceramic plate is dense and soaks up heat, so food on a cold one hands its warmth straight over, and the sauce sets before anyone reaches for seconds. Restaurant kitchens keep a stack warming at around sixty degrees Celsius, about a hundred and forty Fahrenheit, and at home warm to the touch is plenty. Ten minutes in the oven on its lowest setting does it, or the hot tap while the food finishes, or a dishwasher fresh from its cycle, all for next to nothing. Most china takes that heat happily; check the stamp underneath, and keep gold or platinum rims out of the microwave, where the metal sparks. A guest feels a warm plate without ever knowing what it was. Before you serve tonight, run the hot tap into two plates. There’s probably a streak on the window across your table, and you can’t say which side it’s on.

Which side it’s on is what the seventh habit tells you: wipe the two faces of a window in different directions. Finish the inside with up-and-down strokes and the outside side to side, and a vertical streak is then in the room, a horizontal one outdoors. Many streaks come from the hour you chose: sunshine warms the glass, the water dries before you wipe it, and what it carried stays as a film, so wait for an overcast morning. Clean the frame and the sill before the glass, or their grime runs down onto a finished pane, and overlap each squeegee pull by a couple of centimetres, an inch. Use the trade’s kit: warm water with a few drops of washing-up liquid, a squeegee, and a lint-free cloth to wipe its blade after each pull, about ten minutes a window and a few dollars. Never lean out of an upper window; a long-handled squeegee does the outside. Tonight, check the forecast and save one window for the next grey morning. Your front-door brass has its own trouble: if it’s spotted, the polish in your cupboard is the wrong tool.

Those spots are the eighth habit’s business: find out whether your brass is wearing a coat. A great deal of new brass leaves the factory under a clear lacquer, meant to stay bright for years with only a damp cloth. Once that lacquer cracks or wears thin, air reaches the metal in patches, and the brown freckles creep underneath its edges where polish can’t follow. Test a hidden spot: a dab of brass polish on a white cloth turns black on bare brass and stays clean on lacquer. If a fridge magnet sticks, it’s plated steel with only a thin skin of brass, so go gently. For freckled lacquer, take it all off with acetone nail-polish remover, gloves on and a window open, then polish once, an evening and a few dollars; after that, wax it, or let it darken evenly to a soft brown. After dark, dab polish on a hidden corner of one handle and check the cloth. Before you shut that front door, take one breath of the air coming out of your home.

That breath is the ninth habit: a home’s smell is held in its soft things, so that’s where you clean it. Frying, smoke and damp drift through a room as a fine, oily vapour that settles into the fibres of curtains, cushions and the coats by the door, then seeps back out for weeks, long after the pan is scrubbed. You won’t catch it from your own armchair, because a nose dulls to whatever it meets all day, so judge it the way a guest does: come back from a proper walk and take that first breath before your coat is off. A fragrance sprayed on top only gives a guest two smells instead of one. In a lot of French homes the curtains come down and go through the wash at the big spring clean. Follow your nose to the softest thing near the source and wash or air it, one machine cycle or an afternoon outdoors. A musty, damp smell you can’t trace needs a professional to find the water. Later tonight, press your nose into the curtain nearest the kitchen. In the cutlery drawer, a sheet of kitchen foil can turn black spoons bright without a single rub.

That foil is the tenth habit: bring dark silver back in a bath, not with a cloth, because the bath turns the black back into metal instead of wearing it away. Line a bowl with aluminium foil, lay the pieces on it so each one touches the foil, pour in very hot water and stir in a few spoonfuls of baking soda. Foil, silver and soda water make a small battery: the aluminium gives itself up, and in minutes the tarnish turns back into bright metal, for pocket change. Keep glued hollow handles and stones out of it, and any pattern whose darkened grooves you like, because the bath brightens those too. In a lot of French families silver-plated cutlery arrives as a wedding present; wash it by hand, because beside stainless steel in a dishwasher it can come out spotted. Here’s where I’d spend, though: where the plating has worn away, no bath covers it, and a silversmith can replate a good old set, often for more than a new canteen costs, leaving you heavy old forks in new silver, the better buy. Once the table’s cleared, give three blackened spoons a foil bath. The last habit is in your linen cupboard, where a clean-looking tablecloth is quietly changing colour.

That colour change is the stain nobody can see yet, and the eleventh habit prevents it: never put table linen away unwashed, however clean it looks. A drop of white wine, juice or sweet tea dries invisible on white cloth, and so does a smear of butter or the oil from a hand. In the dark of a cupboard the sugar slowly browns and the fat yellows, and months later the cloth comes out spotted brown and lined yellow along its folds. The stain was made at the table; the cupboard only develops it, like a photograph. So everything goes into the wash after one meal, spotless or not, and dries fully before it’s folded. In older French flats the linen often lives in l’armoire, the tall, two-doored cupboard a French household fills with folded sheets and cloths, and bare wood, oak especially, gives off acids that can yellow cloth where it touches, so line the shelves with a clean cotton sheet. My grandmother, Mémé Suzanne, washed the Sunday tablecloth even when nobody had spilt a drop, and I once asked her why. “What you can’t see tonight,” she said, pegging it on the line, “you’ll see at Easter.” I still lay two of her tablecloths, ma chère, and they are white. For linen that has already yellowed, soak it overnight in warm water with powdered oxygen bleach, never chlorine, which is hard on linen, then dry it in the sun, which whitens it further; one scoop and one bright afternoon. Last thing tonight, unfold one stored white cloth and look along its folds. Put the eleven side by side and they shrink to three laws.

Before you replace anything, look at what’s happened to its surface: grey on a plate, haze on a glass, black on a spoon and freckles on brass can often be undone. The work a guest notices most is work they never see: felt under the cloth, a warm plate, a cupboard where nothing goes in dirty. And meet your own home as a stranger would, by its sound, its smell and the light through its glass. Begin with the forks, turned over in a minute at the next table you lay, and add one more habit each week after that. My question for the comments: did your cloudiest glass come out of the vinegar clear, or still clouded? When you lay tonight’s table, let the first thing you set down be a fork, face down.
