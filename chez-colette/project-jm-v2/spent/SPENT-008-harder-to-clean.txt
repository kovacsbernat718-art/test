Eleven things in your home are making it harder to clean than it needs to be, and you chose several of them because they looked clean. The black tap you rub with your sleeve before guests arrive. The hallway wall with a shiny patch where a mark was scrubbed off. A whole Saturday morning, and a grey bloom back on the sideboard by Tuesday. A lot of French households do far less of this, and not because they scrub more often. In a French bathroom the shower head is usually hand-held, on a rail, so the tiles can be rinsed down in seconds before you step out. Much of the home is chosen the same way. Here are eleven things those households avoid, and what they keep instead. Number four explains the sticky grey film on top of your kitchen cupboards, and why an ordinary spray only smears it. And number eleven is under your sink: you bought it to clean with, and it's making the other ten dirtier.

The first thing is that matte black tap, and the white ring that comes back to it every morning. The ring is limescale. Hard tap water carries dissolved chalk, and when a drop dries on the tap, the water leaves and the chalk stays. On chrome it's a faint haze. On black it is a white circle you can see from the doorway. Soap will not shift it, and neither will an all-purpose spray, because chalk does not dissolve in them. It dissolves in acid. Much of France has hard water, and in the cleaning aisle of a French supermarket white vinegar is sold by the litre. Almost every black tap is a coating, and many makers forbid acid on it, so read the care sheet first. On chrome, or where the sheet allows it, soak a cloth in vinegar and warm water, half and half, wrap it round the tap for ten minutes, rinse and dry; a few cents' worth. Better still, stop it forming: scale only appears where a drop is left to evaporate, so a tap that is dried never scales. Before bed, wipe the tap dry after brushing your teeth, and look at it at breakfast. Keep that vinegar away from one surface, though, because the mark it leaves there can never be cleaned off.

Second on the list is polished marble where food is prepared, and the surprise is that marble and limescale are the same mineral. Both are calcium carbonate. So the acid that melts the ring off your tap also melts the worktop, and lemon juice, wine, tomato and vinegar are all acids. The dull circle a lemon leaves on marble is not a stain. A stain is something added. This is something taken away: a thin layer of the polish has dissolved, and no cleaner can wipe off what is missing. A sealer slows oil and colour from soaking in, and does nothing against acid. In older French flats the marble is on the mantelpiece and on top of la commode, the heavy chest of drawers in a bedroom or a hall, where nobody slices a lemon, ma chère. On marble already fitted, cut and pour on a board, and wash with plain washing-up liquid. A stone restorer can hone it to a soft matte on which new marks barely show; in a new kitchen, granite or a quartz composite shrugs off lemon juice. Now crouch until your eye is level with the worktop, look towards the window, and count the dull rings. While you are down there, look at the dark line where your carpet meets the wall.

Looking along a worktop costs nothing, and choosing well is mostly knowing where to look before you pay. Which sheen of paint suits which wall, how a rug's fibre and pile behave, where the money belongs in upholstery, and a list of what to leave in the shop are in my book, Colette's Home Bible, at the link in the description below or the QR code on the screen.

Wall-to-wall carpet is the third, and that dark line is not a strip your vacuum missed. It runs along the skirting board and under any door usually kept shut. Air moves constantly through the gaps beneath skirtings and doors, and the carpet strains it like a filter, keeping the finest dust in the pile. Grit also sifts down to the base of the fibres, beyond the reach of one quick pass, so a carpet can carry a great deal of dirt and still look clean. Many French flats keep the floor bare, wood or tile, with a rug small enough to be carried to the window and shaken. A bare floor shows its dust within two days, which is its advantage: a broom deals with it in two minutes. If you rent, or the carpet is nearly new, it stays, and what changes is the vacuuming. Go slowly, forward and back, because moving air needs a moment to lift what lies deep. The day it wears out, a hard floor with a washable rug is where I would put the money, and in an older building a professional should check what lies beneath before anyone pulls it up. This evening, open that closed door and look at the strip under it. Then go to the kitchen and look up, above the cupboards.

Number four is a kitchen with no door, and the film it leaves up there. Frying throws off a mist of oil droplets too fine to see. They ride the warm air until they meet a cooler surface and settle. A thin layer of cooking oil left in the air slowly hardens, by the same reaction that seasons a cast-iron pan and lets linseed oil dry into paint. Dust lands while it is still tacky. So that grey layer is dust set in varnish, which is why an ordinary spray only smears it. Older French flats give the kitchen four walls and a door, shut when something is frying, and the French name for a kitchen open to the sitting room is, word for word, an American kitchen. With no door, catch the mist at the source: a lid or a splatter screen, and the hood on before the pan heats and for ten minutes after. For the old film, use hot water, washing-up liquid and a spoonful of baking soda, since alkali loosens old grease. Then lay newspaper on each cupboard top and change it twice a year; an hour, and pennies. After supper, draw one fingertip along the top of the cupboard nearest the hob. The same mist reached the cupboard doors, and whether you can see it there depends on their finish.

The fifth is a finish: dark and high-gloss, on cupboard doors, a sideboard or a piano-black table. Dust has a colour. It is pale grey, so it shows most against black and least against the middle tones, oak, stone, putty. A glossy surface is also a mirror, and anything lying on a mirror interrupts the reflection, so every speck and fingerprint is outlined in light. My husband, Thierry, is a cabinetmaker, and he judges a finish by tilting the sample towards the window: "If I can see the window in it, I will see every finger on it." He would put oiled oak everywhere, since a scratch in oil rubs out with more oil; I hold that beside a sink a matte lacquer wipes down faster. On gloss you already own, use a barely damp microfibre cloth, then a dry one, and no polish, which only lays down a film for the next finger to print on. Two minutes a door. Tonight, tilt your head until the window appears in a surface, and find three that do it. Five of the eleven are behind you, the cupboard tops among them, and the bottle under your sink is still waiting. And if dust shows on one black shelf, your window may be holding sixty shelves more.

Sixth, the slatted blind, which is a stack of shelves hung in a window. A narrow-slat blind on an ordinary window has fifty or sixty slats, and laid side by side they would roughly cover a dining table for four. Dust falls. It settles on what is horizontal and largely spares what is vertical, so sixty level strips collect more than the whole wall around them. French windows often have only cloth inside: a plain sheer or a curtain that unhooks. A few times a year it comes down and goes into the washing machine, and it is hung back damp, so its own weight pulls the creases out as it dries. For the blind you have, tilt the slats fully closed, wipe from the top down with a dry microfibre cloth, tilt them the other way and repeat; three minutes. When it needs replacing, a roller blind is one vertical surface and among the cheapest coverings in the shop. Run a finger along a single slat at eye level. Whatever missed the slats kept falling, and it has travelled to the one place your vacuum cannot follow.

Thing number seven: furniture that stands a finger's height off the floor. Dust does not stay where it lands. Each footstep and each swing of a door nudges it along until it reaches still air, and the stillest air in a room is under the bed and behind the sofa. There the fibres tangle into grey rolls. In French, ma chère, those rolls are called sheep, and a flock gathers wherever nobody disturbs it. So a gap too low for the vacuum head becomes a permanent store. Many old French beds and wardrobes stand on feet tall enough for a broom to pass beneath. As a decorator I allow two heights and nothing between: the head of your own vacuum plus a finger, often around ten centimetres, four inches, or right down to the floor, so nothing gets under at all. Screw-in legs or risers lift a bed or a sofa in ten minutes for less than twenty dollars. Lay your phone on the floor tonight with its torch on, and look under the sofa. To stand up again you will put a hand on the wall, and that hand is the next problem.

For the eighth, look at your paint: dead-flat matte, anywhere hands reach. Matte paint looks matte because its surface is rough at a scale too small to feel; grains of pigment stand proud and scatter the light. Scrub a mark off and you flatten those grains. You have polished the wall, and the shiny patch is permanent. The stairwell of an old French apartment building is painted for exactly this. The lower wall, to just above the door handles, is very often in a tough gloss, and only the wall above is matte, so the part that gets touched is the part that can be washed. My father, Jean-Marc, a house painter, renewed a good many of those, and he never measured that line. He read it off the old wall, where shoulders and shopping bags had already greyed the paint. At home that means satin or eggshell in the hall, around switches, with matte kept for ceilings and quiet walls. A hall is one tin and an afternoon. Until then, press a damp cloth on a mark and lift, without rubbing. After dark, switch on the hall light and look along the wall from one end. Now go to the seat everybody uses, and lift its cushion.

Ninth is a sofa whose covers do not come off. Under a seat cushion, many sofas sold in the United States carry a tag with a code: W means water-based cleaners are safe, S means solvent only, W S means either, and X means a vacuum and nothing else. Read it before a wet cloth goes near the fabric, because water marks cloth in its own way. Wet one spot and the water creeps outward through the weave, carrying loosened dirt with it, and as it dries the dirt is left at the edge, as a ring. Upholsterers therefore clean a whole panel, seam to seam, or blot from the outside of a mark inward with a cloth that is barely damp. A cover that unzips avoids all of it. In a French furniture shop the label often says déhoussable, from housse, a cover: the covers come off, and that is printed there as a selling point. And a white cover that washes is less work than a grey one that cannot. On your next sofa, removable covers justify the extra cost. A large washable throw over the seats is five minutes and under forty dollars. Lift a cushion now and read the letter. The dullest patch in your home, though, is probably not on a sofa. It runs in a line from your front door.

The tenth thing is outdoor shoes on indoor floors, and the single thin mat meant to stop them. Look at the path from your front door to the kitchen. If the floor is duller there, that is not dirt, and mopping will never bring the shine back. Street grit is mostly fine sand, and sand is harder than varnish, vinyl or wood, so every step with grit under the sole sands the finish a little. A shoe also gives up its grit over several strides, not one, which is why shops and offices lay matting many steps long inside their doors. In a French apartment building the coarse mat lies outside each flat, on the landing, and in many homes a pair of slippers waits just inside. Put a stiff, scraping mat outside and a washable one inside, long enough for two full steps, and leave the shoes at the door; the pair comes to under fifty dollars. Tonight, lift your doormat and look at what has sifted through to the floor beneath. With the grit stopped at the door, ma chère, one kind of dirt remains, and you spread it yourself, from a bottle.

And the eleventh thing is in the cupboard under your sink: too much cleaner, in too many bottles. A cleaner works because each of its molecules has one end that grips grease and one end that grips water, so a rinse carries both away. Whatever is not rinsed dries where it lies, as a film too thin to see and faintly sticky, and its grease-loving ends go on catching: dust, footprints, the next spill. So a floor washed with a double dose looks duller and dirties faster than one washed with half. That film is on the floor, the worktop, the gloss doors and the tap, which is how one cupboard makes the other ten worse. Test it. On a floor you consider clean, drip a little plain warm water and rub with your fingertips. If it turns slippery or raises a few bubbles, you are standing on old product. Whatever the labels say, most of those bottles are one of two things: something alkaline for grease, or something acid for limescale. Sorted by room, kitchen, bathroom, floor and glass, that is four purchases; sorted by kind of dirt, it is two. Nobody planned that, it is simply how a shelf gets arranged. In a lot of French homes the whole kit is white vinegar, a block of Marseille soap or a bottle of black soap, and soda crystals. Mémé Suzanne, my grandmother, made curtains all her working life and cleaned with exactly that, plus a drawer of le linge de maison, the sheets and tea towels of the house, torn into squares once they were too thin for a bed. She wet the cloth and never the surface. "The cloth is wet," she said, "the table is not." So halve the dose, spray the cloth, use one product at a time, and go over the floor once with plain water; it costs less than what you do now.

Before anything new comes home, whether a tap, a rug, a sofa or a tin of paint, ask it three things. Does it show what lands on it, the way black shows chalk and gloss shows fingers? Does it hold what lands on it, the way pile, slats and a fixed cover do? And can a single cloth cross it without stopping, lifting or reaching? Three good answers, and the thing will stay clean on very little work. Take the eleven one at a time; several only apply on the day you replace something. The quickest is the first, drying the tap, at ten seconds. One question: which letter is on the tag under your sofa cushion? Type just that letter under this video. The towel is already hanging beside the basin, so begin there before you go to bed.
