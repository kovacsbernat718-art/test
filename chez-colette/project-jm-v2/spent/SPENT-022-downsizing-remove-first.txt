Eleven things in your home are sized for rooms you won’t have after downsizing, and an interior designer removes them first. Most of them aren’t clutter at all. They’re the big, good pieces, and each one fails a measurement you can take tonight. I’m Colette. In fourteen years of decorating Lyon’s small rented flats, the furniture that hurt a room most was usually good furniture, sized for somewhere else. Number four is your corner sofa, and its trouble is which hand it is. And the last is a roll of tape that lets you walk your new rooms today.

Number one: the big dining table. Its real size is the table plus a ring of floor around it, and nobody measures that ring. Every chair has to slide back and take a body, so designers leave about ninety centimetres, three feet, between the table’s edge and the nearest wall or furniture. Where someone walks behind a seated guest, they leave more. Now take a table for six, one metre eighty by ninety. That’s six feet by three. With its ring, it claims about three metres sixty by two metres seventy. Call it twelve feet by nine. In a smaller dining corner, that’s the whole room. So work backwards. Take a metre eighty, six feet, off the new space’s length and the same off its width, and what’s left is the biggest table it can carry. A bench helps, because it tucks fully under the table when nobody’s on it, so that side can sit closer to the wall. Does yours have leaves? Measure it at its shortest before you decide. It’s ten minutes with a tape, and it’s free. Tonight, pull one chair back as if to sit, and measure to the wall. Next, picture the wall cupboards in your new kitchen, because your dinner plates may be wider than those cupboards are deep.

If that chair met the wall sooner than you expected, like this video and subscribe. The next videos keep measuring the smaller home, one decision at a time.

Number two: oversized dinner plates. Lots of modern dinner plates measure twenty-seven to thirty centimetres across. That’s about ten and a half to twelve inches. A wall cupboard sold as thirty centimetres deep, twelve inches, is less than that inside, once the back panel and the door take their share. So the plate goes in at a slant, or the door won’t shut, and you find out on your first evening in the new kitchen. Annoying, and completely avoidable. Before a single plate is wrapped, get the inside depth of the new wall cupboards. It’s on the kitchen plan, or you can measure on a visit. Then stand one of your plates against the back of your own cupboard, and see where its rim lands against the door line. Love the big ones? They can still come. The deep drawers under a counter, usually around fifty centimetres inside, hold plates flat. A wooden rack on the worktop holds them on edge. And how many should you pack? In les arts de la table, the French craft of dressing a table from plate to glass, everything is counted in place settings, one per chair. So take the chairs from number one, plus two spares for breakages. A service for twelve belongs to a table you’re leaving behind. The sorting takes half an hour, and costs nothing. Before bed, measure one dinner plate across, then one wall cupboard inside. Your tallest wardrobe can be shorter than the new ceiling and still never stand up under it.

Settling the plates took one tape and one cupboard door. Colette’s Home Bible takes the bigger moves the same way. It has the clearances every chair and doorway needs, what lets furniture survive a move, and how one room can do four jobs. The link’s in the description below, or scan the Q R code on the screen.

Number three: the tallest wardrobe. A tall piece has two heights, and a ceiling only cares about the second one. Wardrobes and tall bookcases get built or carried lying on their backs. As you tip one upright, it pivots on its bottom edge, so its top front corner swings higher than the piece will ever stand. That highest point is its diagonal. Square the height, square the depth, add them, and take the square root, or just run a tape corner to corner across the side panel. Take an example. A wardrobe two metres thirty-six tall and sixty centimetres deep sweeps up to nearly two metres forty-four. Under a ceiling of two metres forty, just under eight feet, it stays on the floor. Perfectly built, and impossible to raise. I once found one like that in a client’s studio, lying across the floor with its doors facing the ceiling. Some flat-pack instructions print the ceiling height a tall frame needs to be raised. Find that line before the piece is packed. Plenty of newer French flats change the question completely. The bedroom already has le placard, a cupboard built right into the wall, from floor to ceiling. So a big wardrobe from an older home finds no wall waiting for it. Run the tape and compare the figure with the new ceiling, leaving a couple of centimetres for a light fitting or a cornice. Anything that doesn’t clear stays behind. The whole check is two minutes, and free. Raising any tall piece is a job for two, so ask for a second pair of hands. Tonight, run a tape corner to corner across your wardrobe’s side. Your corner sofa has a hand, like a glove. That hand decides which rooms it can live in.

Number four: the corner sofa. Like hands, most are made left or right, which limits the rooms they fit, and many can never switch. The long seat, or chaise, sits on one side only. The trade names that side as you stand facing the sofa, not as you sit on it. So a piece listed as left-arm facing has its long end on your right once you sit down. Confusing, I know. In the new sitting room, the corner it needs has to turn the same way. And the window, the door and the radiator all have to be somewhere it won’t block. Lift the seat cushions and look underneath. If the chaise is a separate piece, held by connectors that fit either side, it can usually swap. If its arm is built into one frame, it can’t. Many modular sofas also come apart into a two-seater and a chair, which often suits a smaller room better. Before any of that, measure the doorway it has to pass. Then find the sofa’s diagonal depth, which many listings print. Tipped on its back, that’s the figure that has to get through. In plenty of older French buildings with narrow stairs, a big sofa goes up the outside on a ladder lift and comes in through a window. To be fair, in an open-plan room its long back can do a wall’s work, closing the sitting area off from the kitchen without a single brick. There, ma chère, it earns every centimetre it takes. Checking the hand and the connectors is five minutes’ work. After supper, stand facing your sofa and say which side its long seat is on. Then sit in your biggest armchair, a forearm along each arm. How much of its width seats nobody?

Number five: the overstuffed armchair. Its arms can take more room than the person sitting in it. Rolled, padded arms often run twenty to twenty-five centimetres each, about eight to ten inches. So a club chair ninety centimetres wide may offer a seat barely fifty across. That’s three feet of chair for twenty inches of person. Sofas work the same way. Two that both seat three can differ by a third of a metre, a foot, entirely in their arms. My grandmother, Mémé Suzanne, would lay her tape between a chair’s arms, then across its front, and tap the difference with her pencil. “That’s cloth,” she’d say, “not seat.” Listings usually give the seat width separately from the overall width. In a smaller room, that pair of numbers is the one to compare. So what sits well in less space? A chair with slim arms, or none. A slipper chair, or an old cane-backed one, where nearly every centimetre is seat. A good chair that won’t fit can go to le vide-grenier, the neighbourhood sale where households set out what’s leaving on a trestle table. Your part of the work is the carrying. A big upholstered chair is a job for two people, so book the help before the sale. Later this evening, measure your armchair’s seat between the arms, then its full width. The door nearest you sweeps a quarter circle of your room every time it opens.

Number six: furniture with no wall to stand on. That door sweeps an arc as wide as itself, about eighty centimetres, thirty-one inches. Nothing tall can stand inside that arc, or on the strip of wall the open door folds back against. This is the most important item on the list, and it sits in the middle for a reason. Every other item is decided by how much clear wall the new home has. In a smaller home, wall runs out long before floor does. Windows take their stretch. Radiators take theirs. Light switches claim a strip too, because nothing tall can stand in front of the one you reach for. What’s left is a handful of clear runs. And the bookcase, the sideboard, the chest of drawers, the headboard and the television all compete for them. So take the new floor plan and draw each door’s arc, with a compass, or a string and a pencil. List every stretch of clear wall a metre or longer, with its length. Then list every piece you own that only works backed against a wall, with its width. Is the second list longer than the first? Then the extra pieces have nowhere to go, however much floor the plan shows. Give it an hour with a pencil and a ruler. You won’t spend a cent. Tonight, list every piece you own that only works against a wall. We’re past the middle now, and the roll of tape still waits at number eleven. Your bookcase may be taking a strip of floor from every room it stands in, for books that never reach it.

Number seven: the deep bookcase. Most books need about twenty centimetres of shelf, eight inches, but most bookcases give them thirty or more. A paperback novel stands only eleven to fourteen centimetres deep, four or five inches. An ordinary hardback is around sixteen, and only atlases and art books want more. Everything behind the books is floor the room pays for and nobody sees. Along a three-metre wall, every spare ten centimetres of depth is almost a third of a square metre. That’s over three square feet, gone. Shallow shelves also go where a deep case never could. Down a hallway. Behind a door. Beside a window. My husband Thierry built ours twenty-two centimetres deep, not the eighteen I asked for, then screwed it to the wall before a single book went in. “Tall and shallow tips forward,” he said, and on that point, ma chère, I lost. So how many books are you really moving? Measure them in metres of shelf, not in titles. A metre holds roughly thirty to forty ordinary books, and that length has to match one of the clear runs from number six. Keep a deep case only if a wall in the new home is waiting for it. And fix any tall case to the wall with a bracket and two screws. That job takes a quarter of an hour. This evening, measure how many metres of shelf your books actually fill. Hold a fridge magnet to your favourite pan, and whether it grips decides if that pan moves house.

Number eight: pans that won’t work on induction. That magnet matters because plenty of newer and smaller kitchens cook by induction. The ring heats the metal of the pan’s base itself, and only a magnetic base answers it well. So if the magnet holds firmly, the pan works, and if it slides off or barely clings, it won’t. Plain aluminium, copper and glass fail, unless a steel disc is fixed under the base. So do some stainless pans. Check the stainless ones twice. Size matters too. A ring may not detect a pan much smaller than its marked circle. Then count your rings. A hob only ever holds as many pans at once as it has rings. The stack behind the cupboard door is what’s left over when every ring is busy. Plenty of small French flats have just two rings, set into a short run of counter, and plenty of people cook every weekday meal on them. For a smaller kitchen, I’d keep one pan per ring, in the size you reach for most, plus one oven dish and one big pot for soup or pasta. Clearing that cupboard takes half an hour and spends nothing. Tonight, set every pan that failed the magnet out on the counter. Three to go before the tape, and the next one is hiding on the label of a fitted sheet.

Number nine: sheets for beds you won’t have. The number on that label is the pocket depth, and it matters more than the size name. A fitted sheet is sized twice, once for the mattress’s width and length, and once for its thickness. That second figure is printed on the label, often around thirty centimetres, twelve inches, and deep-pocket sheets go up to forty. Move to a smaller bed or a thinner mattress, and sheets that read the right size can still sag. They bunch. They spring off the corners in the night. Bed names don’t travel well either, since what one shop calls a double, another calls a full. So the word on the packet means less than the numbers beside it. Measure the new mattress first, all three numbers, before you touch the linen cupboard. How much do you really need? Two complete sets per bed is plenty, one on and one in the wash. Keep a third only for the bed you sleep in. Then go through le linge de maison, the household’s stock of sheets, towels and tablecloths. Anything sized for a bed that isn’t coming can go. Animal shelters often take old sheets and towels. Sorting a cupboard this way is an hour, and all it costs is a bag to carry things in. Before you turn in, unfold one fitted sheet and find the pocket depth on its label. Then think back. How many nights was your guest bed slept in last year? Hold that number.

Number ten: the guest bed. If your number was small, a daybed sleeps the same guests in the floor a sofa already takes. A single daybed is about ninety centimetres to a metre deep, roughly three feet, close to the depth of an ordinary sofa. With cushions along its back, it is a sofa, until somebody needs a bed. Many have a trundle, a second mattress on a low frame that rolls out from underneath, so two guests can sleep where one person sits by day. Pop-up trundles lift to the height of the daybed and make one wide bed. Plain ones stay low. Know which you need before you buy. Now set your number against three hundred and sixty-five. Say it was ten nights, ma chère. That leaves three hundred and fifty-five nights of a bed holding suitcases and the ironing. And a room kept for it slowly turns into a box room. In a smaller home, the guest bed belongs inside a room that works every day, the sitting room or a study. The frame you’re leaving behind is a two-person carry, the headboard especially. Line up the help early. Right now, look at what’s lying on your guest bed. The last item is probably in a kitchen drawer near you. A roll of masking tape.

Number eleven, the last: before anything goes, a roll of masking tape. It removes nothing, yet it decides everything above it. You tape your new rooms onto your old floor, at full size, before a single box is packed. Low-tack masking tape lifts cleanly, so clear one room and mark out the new sitting room’s walls. Draw the door with its swing as an arc, and the window with its sill. Then bring in what you plan to keep, or outline it in a second colour. Add each piece’s ring of use. The chairs pulled back from number one. The doors you just drew. And the main walkway, about ninety centimetres, three feet, wide. Now walk it. Sit where the sofa would be, and watch where the door would swing. A plan on paper is flat, while a floor you can walk round tells your body what living there will feel like. Look back at the list, and every item turns out to be a measurement of the home you’re leaving. A table sized for its dining room, plates for its cupboards, a wardrobe for its ceiling. A sofa for its corner, linen for its beds. None of them was wrong. They were right somewhere else. I measure the room before I want the thing, and the tape keeps that rule even when the thing is already yours. Mémé Suzanne never put scissors to a client’s fabric until she’d cut the shape in newspaper and pinned it over the chair, smoothing it flat with her palm. “Paper is cheap,” she’d say, “cloth isn’t.” Your floor is that newspaper, ma chère, and your furniture is the cloth. A roll of tape is a few dollars, and one room takes about an hour. Tonight, tape the outline of one new room onto any floor you can clear. Three questions, asked of every large thing you own, finish the job.

Where will it stand in the new home, on which wall, and clear of which door’s swing? How will it get there, through which doorway, up which stairs, and under which ceiling? And how much of the room it takes is actually used, the seat and not the arms, the shelf and not the air behind the books? Start with the pans. One fridge magnet, one minute in the kitchen. The table measurement takes ten, and none of the rest needs doing this week. The tape can wait until the new floor plan is in your hands. Under this video, answer with a single number. How many chairs will fit around your next table? Your nearest pan is a few steps away, so take a fridge magnet to its base now.
