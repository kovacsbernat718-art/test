There are eleven mistakes making your home look cheap, and from the seat you’re in right now, you can probably see four of them. Hardly any of them is about price: most are a measurement, and several can be put right before bed. Number four is two little screws beside your light switch. And number eleven is a five-second test you can run from any doorway.

The first mistake hangs over your head: your ceiling light is very likely too small for its room. Lighting designers size it with a quick sum: add the room’s length and width in feet, and the total is roughly the right diameter in inches. A room four metres by five, about thirteen feet by sixteen, wants a fitting close to seventy-five centimetres across, twenty-nine inches. The flat glass dome that builders tend to fit is often about thirty centimetres, a foot. At that size it reads as a dot on a wide white ceiling, and the room beneath it looks unfinished. If yours is a plain hanging bulb, a large paper globe shade clipped round it reaches the right size in ten minutes for a few dollars; give it an LED bulb, which runs far cooler. Keep a hanging shade at least two point one metres, seven feet, above the floor where people walk, and leave a new fitting’s wiring to an electrician. Tonight, measure the room’s length and width in feet and add them. Then sit on your sofa: the firmness you tested in the shop says almost nothing about how long it lasts.

A tape measure and one sum gave that fitting its proper size. Mistake two lives in the seat cushion: firmness and lifespan are separate numbers, and a shop only lets you feel one. Firmness is how hard the foam pushes back. Lifespan follows its density, and a light foam can be made to feel firm in a showroom yet sink into a hollow far sooner. French sofa listings often print the density outright. For a seat, thirty-five kilos per cubic metre or more, about two point two pounds per cubic foot, is the usual mark of lasting foam. Mémé Suzanne, my grandmother, tested it with her forearm, pressed into a client’s seat up to the elbow. If the dent was still there by the time she’d rolled up her tape measure, the foam was finished, ma chère, whatever the cover looked like. Ask for the density before you buy. If yours has sunk, a foam shop will cut new inserts for the old covers, wrapped in batting so the edges stay round, for far less than a new sofa, in an afternoon. A dip from broken springs belongs to an upholsterer. After supper, press your forearm into your usual cushion and watch it rise. Then catch a door frame in the nearest mirror and move your head slowly sideways.

Mistake three is a mirror that bends what it shows. If that door frame wobbled as your head moved, the glass isn’t flat, and everything in it ripples like a fairground mirror. Cheap full-length mirrors are often glass only two or three millimetres thick, and some are mirrored plastic that flexes under a fingertip. Thin glass screwed tight to a wall that isn’t perfectly flat follows every bump behind it. Good mirror glass runs about six millimetres, a quarter of an inch, stiff enough to stay flat by itself. A wide bevelled edge, ground into the glass itself, is a fair clue to thickness. Older French rooms gave the big mirror a firmer bed: the trumeau, a mirror set into the panelling above the fireplace, held by the woodwork rather than a nail. For yours, loosen each clip a quarter turn and slip a felt pad behind any corner that rocks, a quarter of an hour with pads that cost pennies, and hang anything heavy from a stud. Before bed, loosen one clip and see whether the frame straightens. On your way out, the light switch by that same door is holding mistake four.

The fourth is the pair of screws from the start, and their slots probably point in two directions. Careful electricians and finish carpenters turn every slot on a cover plate to the same angle, usually straight up and down. Some call this habit clocking, and slots that agree read as the work of someone paying attention. The plate itself often leans, and beside the straight line of a door frame, a tilt of a degree or two shows from across the room. On many switches, the holes fixing the switch to its box are long slots rather than round holes, precisely so it can be set level. A coin turns the plate screws in seconds; stop at snug, because overtightening cracks plastic. Anything that means loosening the switch itself waits until that circuit is off at the breaker, or goes to an electrician. Right now, a coin will turn every slot in one room upright. Then switch on a lamp: its shade has a line down one side, and it may be facing you.

Fifth comes that line, the seam. Almost every fabric or paper shade is a flat strip rolled into a tube and joined down one side. With the bulb lit, the overlap shows as a darker stripe exactly where your eye lands, and decorators turn it to the wall without thinking. Proportion is where far more lamps go wrong. A common working rule makes the shade about two-thirds the height of its base, and about as wide at the bottom as the base is tall. Undersized, a shade makes even a good base look like a bargain. Heavy old bases in ceramic, brass or stone turn up at almost every brocante, a French dealer’s market for second-hand and antique things. A shade sized by that rule makes one the best lamp in the room. Measure the base before you shop; a new shade is usually under thirty dollars and goes on in a minute. This evening, turn every seam to the wall, thirty seconds a lamp. And the bookshelf beside that lamp is probably sinking a little further every month it stays loaded.

A sagging shelf is number six, and chipboard never quite stops sagging. Chipboard and M D F creep: under a steady load they go on deforming slowly. A shelf that left the shop dead straight can carry a shallow curve a year later, a dip that shows from the doorway. The cure is a rule every joiner works by: a board’s stiffness rises with the cube of its depth, so doubling the depth makes it eight times stiffer. My husband, Thierry, turned a client’s bowed shelf over so the dip faced up, then glued and screwed a strip of oak on edge along its front, five centimetres tall, two inches. I’d have moved the heaviest books to the ends, over the supports; he called that buying time on a creeping board. With books on it, chipboard wants a support about every sixty centimetres, two feet, and a hardwood strip is a few dollars and an hour. Later, lay a spirit level along the middle shelf and look for daylight. Six are behind you, and the doorway test waits at eleven. Sit on the sofa, elbow on its arm: is the side table’s top above it or below?

Heights are the seventh mistake: surfaces that sit side by side should meet, and most don’t. A side table belongs level with the sofa arm or a little below. A bedside table should sit within about five centimetres, two inches, of the mattress top, and a dining chair’s seat about thirty centimetres, twelve inches, below the tabletop. Miss by a hand’s width and every cup goes down with a reach and a clatter. To my eye, that’s also when a room starts to look assembled from separate shopping trips, because matching heights read as one deliberate line and near misses read as chance. The usual culprit is the bedside table, which often outlives the mattress; a deep modern one can leave it well below the pillow. Swap tables between rooms first: a nightstand often suits a sofa arm. Risers cost little more than a coffee and go on in minutes, and a joiner can shorten legs that are too tall. Measure your sofa arm and the table beside it before you turn in. Then lower your eyes to where the wall meets the floor; that narrow strip is setting the room’s price.

The eighth thing to check is that strip, the skirting board, because a short one makes a room look temporary. The slim skirting in a lot of newer homes, about eight centimetres, three inches, reads as the least a wall could get. In many older French flats it stands fifteen centimetres or more. Under a ceiling around two and a half metres, eight feet, twelve to fifteen centimetres, five or six inches, looks settled. The trade’s cheap way up is a build-up. Fix a slim flat moulding a few centimetres above the existing skirting and fill the joints. Then paint skirting, moulding and the wall between them one colour, so the eye reads one tall board; a weekend, and a few dollars per metre of wall. If you rent, though, this one isn’t yours without asking. Anything nailed into a landlord’s woodwork is a change you owe back on the last day, with filler, sanding and paint, so in a rented room that money does more in a rug or a lamp. Hold a ruler against your skirting tonight and note its height. While you’re down there, look at your rug from the doorway, then again from the sofa.

Ninth, a discovery about that rug: it has two colours, and you probably chose one by accident. Pile fibres lean slightly one way. From one end you look into their tips, which swallow light and show the deepest colour; from the other you see their sides, which catch light and look paler and shinier. Velvet does the same. Stroke the rug away from you: if it feels rough, the pile leans toward you and you’re seeing its richest side; smooth, and you’re seeing the pale one. Turn it so the rich side faces wherever the room is mostly seen from, the sofa or the doorway; two minutes, and free. A rug that arrived rolled keeps trying to curl, so roll it the opposite way for a day. Then lay a thin underlay beneath it, which also stops the creeping ripples that make a good rug look tired, for far less than the rug cost. Once the house is quiet, stroke the rug both ways and turn it if needed. Then find the whitest thing in the room, because it’s making every other white look dirty.

Number ten is a habit, mixing whites, because beside a brighter white, any creamier one looks stained. Fan out a nuancier, the strip-by-strip colour deck at the paint counter, and every white on it leans somewhere: yellow, pink, green, blue or grey. Side by side, the eye compares, so a spotless ivory sofa beside brilliant white woodwork reads as grubby. New white towels and sheets push it further. They’re often treated with optical brighteners, dyes that turn invisible ultraviolet into a faint blue glow, so they look whiter than any paint around them. Keep each room to one family of whites, warm or cool; my own preference is to let the brightest white be the smallest thing in view. A new white towel held against walls, woodwork, sofa and curtains shows in two minutes which one is out of step, and a new cushion cover costs far less than repainting. Fetch a new towel now and hold it to the woodwork, then the sofa. Then stand in your doorway, because the last mistake explains most of the other ten.

And the eleventh mistake is choosing every piece at the size that felt sensible in your hand, which is what the five-second doorway test exposes. Stand in the doorway and squint until the detail blurs and only big shapes are left. Painters squint to see the masses in a picture, and a room gives itself away the same way. An expensive-looking room holds together as a few large, solid shapes: the sofa, a deep shade, a mirror, a rug. A cheap-looking one breaks into small pale blobs, and whatever vanishes is too small, too thin or too low to hold its place. Most of the earlier ten are this one in disguise: a fitting the size of a dinner plate, foam too light, glass too thin to stay flat, a shade too short for its base, a board too slight for its books, a skirting too narrow, tables too low. Size, thickness and weight are where the material is, so in most categories the cheaper version is the smaller, thinner, lighter one. The eye reads mass from across a room long before it could read a label. In a shop, most of these were weighed up at arm’s length, beside things the same size; at home, each one answers to a whole wall. That’s competition, not a plan: savings get made wherever buyers don’t measure. Madame Rivière, a client of mine on the Quai Saint-Vincent, had a lamp whose linen shade was nearly as wide as the armchair beside it. Sitting in that armchair with my eyes half closed, ma chère, I could still count every shape in her sitting room: four calm, solid ones, and nothing I’d have moved. So when two sizes tempt you, take the larger and own one fewer thing. Squint from your doorway for five seconds tonight and note what disappears first. Whatever went first is where you start.

Three laws, then. Size every piece against the room it will live in, never against your hand. Whatever sags, dents or ripples was made too thin, so put the thickness right before you change the cover. And neighbours line up: heights meet, whites share one family, and even the screw slots point the same way. Tonight’s easiest win is the lampshade seams, thirty seconds a lamp, then the screw slots at two minutes a room, ma chère; save the skirting and the foam for the next free Saturday that opens up. My question for below the video: when you squinted from your doorway, what vanished first? The nearest lamp is probably within reach, so tip its shade and find the seam.
