From your bathroom doorway, look at the shower curtain hanging well below the ceiling. Remove it and nine other things, and that room looks expensive. A few of them are quietly ruining whatever they touch. I’m Colette, a decorator in Lyon for fourteen years, until I left a trade whose only answer was to buy. In my clients’ rented, badly renovated bathrooms, the cure was usually taking something out, like plastic panels hiding old tiles. At number nine, the black creeping in from your mirror’s corners, which no cleaner shifts. Saved for last, the dark ring under a can on your shelf. It’s ink.

Number one: the standard-length shower curtain. Its size picked the height of your rod. Not your ceiling. Most shower curtains are sold as a square, seventy-two inches each way. So the rod goes wherever that square hangs right, usually a little over six feet up. Above it, bare wall runs to the ceiling, and the room seems to stop at door height. Designers do the opposite. They hang an extra-long curtain, eighty-four inches, about two metres thirteen, and raise the rod until the hem just clears the floor. A long liner tucks inside the tub. Under an ordinary eight-foot ceiling, two metres forty-four, that puts the rod a hand or two below the plaster. The wall becomes one tall fall of cloth. A tension rod needs no drilling, so it suits a rental. Rod and curtain cost a few tens of dollars, and they’re up in twenty minutes. Tonight, measure from the bathroom floor to the ceiling above the tub. Your darkest towel may have pale orange freckles, and the washing machine didn’t put them there.

A tape measure and a longer curtain gave that wall back. Like this video and subscribe if you’d rather hear about the next mistake before you pay for it.

Number two: coloured towels with bleach spots. Very often it’s benzoyl peroxide, the active ingredient in many acne washes and creams. It’s a bleach. Traces on your face and hands rub off onto the towel, and wherever they land, the dye lifts. The spots come out pale, orange or pink, depending on the colour underneath. They often show only after the next wash, which is why the machine gets the blame. Pillowcases fade the same way, overnight. Which tube has it? The label says, usually at two and a half, five or ten percent. White ends the problem. There’s no dye to lift, and it survives hot washes and bleach. That’s a big part of why hotels stock it. Give whoever uses the cream a white towel and pillowcase. When you replace the rest, check the label for G S M, grams per square metre. About six hundred or more feels plush. A plain white one that heavy is usually twenty or thirty dollars. Before bed, read the label on every tube and bottle by your basin. Down by the bath, the fluffy mat’s backing may be staining your floor for good.

A label on a tube explained those towels, and checks like that run all through Colette’s Home Bible. There’s what a curtain’s drop does to a wall, a page on the bathroom, and what a mirror really does for a room. Two routes lead there. Number three: a rubber-backed bath mat. On vinyl or linoleum, that rubber or latex back can mark the floor for good. I saw it in a rented flat I was redoing. I rolled up the tenant’s bath mat, and there was its waffle grid, printed in yellow on the landlord’s vinyl. Nothing lifted it. It came out of her deposit. Chemicals in the backing creep into the vinyl and react with it, leaving a yellow or brown patch. The stain is inside the floor, where no cleaner reaches. Vinyl makers’ care sheets often warn against these mats. On tile, a deep pile over a sealed back just stays damp and goes grey within months. French shops usually sell bath mats among le linge de maison, the washable textiles of a home. The good ones are heavy terry, the kind a smart guesthouse leaves folded on the tub’s edge. Swap yours for one. It costs about what a bath towel does. If the floor’s slippery when wet, add a thin grip pad labelled safe for vinyl. This evening, roll back the mat and look at the floor underneath. Now check any framed print in the room for a slow wave in its paper.

Number four: framed paper prints. That wave is the paper swelling and shrinking every time someone showers. Paper is a mat of plant fibres, and each fibre drinks moisture and swells, then shrinks as it dries. The frame holds the edges still, so the middle can only buckle. Framers call it cockling. Damp also brings foxing, small rust-brown freckles across the sheet. Museums keep works on paper at a steady humidity, near fifty percent. A shower room goes from dry to dripping and back, twice a day. Move the print to a hall or bedroom, and give the bathroom what water can’t change: glazed ceramic, glass or enamelled metal. My own choice, ma chère, is an old plate on a sprung wire hanger. Steam can’t touch it. That’s a few dollars and ten minutes. La brocante, the French second-hand trade in everything from dishes to doorknobs, is full of single plates whose set broke long ago. After supper, carry the print to the driest room you have. You’d call your tap and your towel bar the same silver. Very often, they’re two different metals.

Number five: towel bars that don’t match your tap. Chrome is mirror-bright with a faint blue cast, and polished nickel is warmer, with a hint of yellow. Stainless steel is greyer and a shade darker, and brushed nickel is a soft, warm grey. Alone, each looks like plain silver. Side by side under one bulb, the eye compares them. The warmer one looks yellowed, and the cooler one looks harsh. So which finish is your tap? That’s the one to keep. It’s costly to change, so it sets the metal, and the cheap pieces follow: towel bar, ring, hooks, roll holder. Most come off with a small screw hidden under each post. A matching bar, ring and hooks cost a fraction of one new tap, and they’re fitted in an afternoon. If you want two metals on purpose, make the difference unmistakable. Chrome with brass, yes. Chrome beside nickel, never. Before the light goes off, judge the tap and the towel bar in one glance. Halfway now, and the mirror’s black edge waits at nine. First, look up at your fan’s grey grille. It may not be pulling at all.

Number six: the lint clogging your bathroom fan. Behind that grille, many fans drone on for years, shifting almost no air. My father had a test for it. Before painting a bathroom ceiling, he held a strip of newspaper under the fan, and if it hung straight down, he warned the family. He’d be scraping that ceiling again within a year. The reasons are dull. Lint from your towels cakes the blades and the grille, the flap in the duct gets stuck, or the duct ends loose in the attic. The noise goes on, but the steam stays put, clouding the mirror and peeling the paint. Your newspaper is one square of toilet paper, held to the grille with the fan on. If it’s sucked flat and stays there, the fan works, and if it falls away, the fan’s doing next to nothing. Power off at the breaker first. Then squeeze the two spring wires, and the grille drops. Wash it in warm soapy water and vacuum the blades. A quarter of an hour. Not a cent. And if the paper still falls once it’s clean? Then the fan is too weak, jammed or venting nowhere. A properly ducted fan is the one purchase on this list I’d never skimp on. The usual rule is one cubic foot per minute of airflow for every square foot of floor, roughly five litres a second per square metre. Leave the wiring and the duct to an electrician. While the shower runs tonight, hold one square of paper to the fan. Before you leave, scratch a fingernail lightly across your toilet seat. Does it catch?

Number seven: a scratched plastic toilet seat. If your nail caught, blame the plastic. Most seats are one of two, and only one keeps a shine to match the bowl. Polypropylene is light and a little flexible, with a softer surface that scratches and dulls after years of wiping. So the seat goes matte while the glazed china stays glossy. Thermoset, sold as duroplast, is hard and heavy, almost ceramic to the touch, and resists scratching far better. Weight is the giveaway. A light seat that flexes in your hands is usually polypropylene. Before you buy, measure from the two bolt holes to the front of the bowl. About sixteen and a half inches, forty-two centimetres, means round. About eighteen and a half, forty-seven, means elongated. Pick a hinge that unclips, for cleaning underneath. A duroplast seat with a soft-close hinge is usually under fifty dollars, and fitting it takes two bolts and a few minutes on your knees. After brushing your teeth, lift the seat and feel whether it flexes. That grey ring in your basin keeps coming back however hard you scrub. The cause isn’t on the surface.

Number eight: the hair under your basin plug. Hidden there, it slows the drain, and that grey ring marks where the slow water waits. A basin that empties in a few seconds leaves almost nothing behind. One that drains slowly lets soap and skin oil settle at its waterline every time, so the ring is back days after you wipe it. So where’s the hair hiding? On many basins, a thin rod runs sideways through a loop on the plug’s tail, a few centimetres down the pipe. That tail is where hair winds into a mat. Put a bowl under the sink, find the nut where the rod enters the back of the drainpipe, and unscrew it by hand. Draw the rod back, and the plug lifts straight out with everything it’s been holding. Not pretty. Very satisfying. Pull the hair off, rinse, and put it back. Ten minutes, and you pay nothing. A plug that pops up when pressed usually unscrews from the top instead. Hair caught deeper comes out with a barbed plastic strip, a few dollars at any hardware shop. Late tonight, fill the basin, pull the plug and count the seconds. Now look up at the mirror over that basin, at its two bottom corners.

Number nine: ammonia glass cleaner. Those corners hold the black edge I promised you, and the spray you’ve been fighting it with is what put it there. A mirror is glass with a thin layer of silver on the back, sealed under paint. Along the cut edges, though, that silver is bare. Spray runs down and creeps into that bottom edge. Many glass cleaners contain ammonia, and with a bathroom’s daily steam helping, it eats the silver into dark patches spreading in from the corners. They’re behind the glass. No scrubbing will reach them. Mirror makers generally warn against ammonia and vinegar near the edges. Clean it with a microfibre cloth damped in plain water, then run a dry cloth along the bottom edge last. The black already there won’t turn back into silver. So cover it with a frame kit made for plain mirrors, or painted moulding over the edges. That’s an afternoon’s work, for a small slice of what new glass would cost. Hiding a flaw instead of replacing the whole thing is good bricolage, the word French households use for every job done with their own toolbox. I love that word, ma chère. Use only adhesive labelled safe for mirrors, because ordinary construction glue can attack the backing. From tonight, the glass spray leaves the bathroom, and water does the mirror. The can of shaving foam on your shelf is printing a ring there that no scrubbing will lift.

Number ten, the last: metal cans on the shelf. A steel can on a wet shelf, usually shaving foam, leaves a black ring on oak. That ring isn’t dirt. It is ink. How does a shaving can make ink? Oak is full of tannins, and when water carrying dissolved iron soaks in, the iron and the tannin combine into a blue-black pigment. It’s the same chemistry as iron gall ink, which scribes made for centuries from oak galls and iron salts. Chestnut and walnut do the same. The iron comes from the can’s bottom rim, where the coating chips and water collects. On stone or enamel, that same rusty circle prints orange instead. Scrubbing only spreads it, because the colour sits inside the fibres. The cure is chemistry too. Oxalic acid, sold as a powder labelled wood bleach, pulls the iron back out. A tub costs a few dollars. On bare or oiled wood, stir a spoonful or two into a cup of warm water. Put gloves on, brush it over the ring, wait a few minutes, and rinse with water. My husband Thierry once brushed it onto a foam can’s ring on our oak bathroom shelf, the one he won the argument over when I wanted stone. We watched the black fade like breath on a window, ma chère, and nothing in a tin has stood on it since. On stone or enamel, use a rust remover labelled safe for that surface, and test a hidden corner first. Then give every can a home, in a cupboard or on a glass saucer you can dry. By bedtime, every can in the room should be off wood and stone. Everything on this list follows three laws, and they hold in any bathroom.

Three laws, then. A room that steams twice a day keeps only what water can’t change. That means glaze, glass and stone, one matched metal, and cloth that goes in the machine. Test what you can’t see: a square of paper for the fan, a count of seconds for the basin, a fingernail for the seat. And before any product moves in, find out what it does to its neighbours. Peroxide to towels, rubber to vinyl, ammonia to mirrors, iron to oak. The easiest is the fan test from number six. Five seconds, one square of toilet paper. The rod, the seat and the mirror frame are bigger jobs. Do them in that order, one finished before the next begins. Did your square of paper hold, or drop? Answer in one word, below. On your way to bed, hang your towel on its bar and let your eye go from bar to tap.
