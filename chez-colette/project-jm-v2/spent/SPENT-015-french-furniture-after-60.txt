Past sixty, you’ve bought the good sofa and matching tables at least once, and your home still doesn’t look as expensive as they cost. A lot of French homes look richer furnished second-hand, and you likely own several of their pieces already. Number eight is a rubber band, likely sitting in a kitchen drawer. Then, past the tenth, turn a chair round: its back says where it belongs.

The first piece is a French dresser, because the underside of nearly every plate you own is scratching the plate beneath it. Turn one over: the ring it stands on is usually bare, unglazed clay, left that way so it won’t fuse to the kiln shelf, and it feels like fine sandpaper. Every time the top plate is lifted off a stack and dropped back, that ring scores a pale circle of tiny scratches into the glaze below. In a lot of older French kitchens, the answer is le vaisselier, a dresser built to show its plates: cupboards below, open shelves above, each with a shallow groove or a rail so the plates stand on edge, faces out. A row of plates on edge reads as a collection; a stack reads as storage. No dresser? A wooden plate rack on any shelf does the same job, five minutes’ work and under thirty dollars, and a paper napkin between stacked plates costs almost nothing. Before bed, lay a napkin between each of your best plates. Now the chairs round your table, and a belief about flat-pack. The classic Paris café chair arrived in a box.

Number two is that café chair, designed in Vienna in eighteen fifty-nine to travel in pieces: six lengths of steamed beech, ten screws and two nuts. Thirty-six of them, taken apart, fitted in a crate of one cubic metre, about thirty-five cubic feet, and it outlasts most chairs sold assembled, because its curves were bent, not cut. Beech softened in steam was pressed round an iron mould and dried, so its fibres run unbroken through every bend, while a curve sawn from a plank cuts across them and leaves short grain that snaps. On the classic model, the back legs and the hoop of the back are one bent length, with a smaller loop inside for stiffness. It’s light enough to lift with one hand, and old ones turn up at French flea markets in twos and threes. When one wobbles, it isn’t failing: the screws holding the legs to the seat ring have worked loose, and ten minutes with a screwdriver puts it right without spending a cent. This evening, turn one chair over and snug every screw you find. Then stand up from your usual armchair, and notice whether your hands pushed down or pulled you forward.

Third comes an armchair that lets you go as easily as it takes you in, and your hands just told you whether yours does. Rising is a push straight down through the arms; if you pulled yourself forward, the seat probably sits below your knees, or the arms stop short of its front edge. With hips below knees, the big thigh muscles start from a deep bend, where they have the least leverage, so you rock forward for momentum. A good seat sits level with the crease behind your knee, around forty-five centimetres, eighteen inches; measure your own, in indoor shoes. The arms should reach the seat’s front edge, and the seat should be no deeper than about fifty centimetres, twenty inches. That upright shape is the classic French armchair, and to my eye its visible wooden frame is what makes it look costly. Risers or a firm cushion will lift a low seat before the kettle boils, for the price of a sandwich; in a shop, stand up three times before you judge the fabric. Measure from the floor to the back of your knee tonight. Its closed-sided cousin shows its price not in the fabric you chose, but in a cord you never noticed.

The fourth piece is the closed armchair, upholstered right down its sides, with a loose cushion on the seat, and its piping shows how carefully it was made. That cord runs round the cushion and the arms, wrapped in a strip of the cover fabric, and on good work the strip is cut on the bias, diagonally across the weave, since only a diagonal strip stretches enough to round a curve smoothly. Along its threads, woven cloth barely gives; on the diagonal, the weave shifts like a garden trellis. Cut straight, the strip can’t follow a corner, so it bunches into tiny pleats at every turn. At her cutting table, my grandmother, Mémé Suzanne, found the true diagonal by folding one corner of the cloth down to its edge and pressing the crease with her thumbnail; every strip she cut followed that fold. On a chair that is cloth from floor to arm, smooth piping is the line that makes it look tailored rather than stuffed, ma chère. Ask for bias-cut piping at any re-covering; ready-made piping, a few dollars a metre, about a yard, saves an hour if you sew it yourself. Tonight, check the four corners of one cushion for pleats. Then open your wardrobe, because the tallest piece on this list was often built for a different job.

Piece five is l’armoire, the wardrobe that stands free on its own feet, and plenty of old ones were never meant for hangers. Many were built to hold a household’s folded linen on shelves, with a hanging rail fitted later, sometimes in a cupboard too shallow for it. A coat on its hanger needs about fifty-five to sixty centimetres inside, twenty-two to twenty-four inches; with less, hangers slant, sleeves press on the door, and it won’t quite shut. Measure inside, because the back panel and the door’s frame take several centimetres off the depth you see from the room. That height is the armoire’s whole effect: one tall, solid piece against a wall reads as part of the building. A shallow one suits folded sweaters and bedding, and a front-to-back pull-out rail fixes the hanging; budget half an hour and less than twenty dollars. Tonight, measure your wardrobe from the back panel to the door. Then sit at your dining table and count how many legs your knees can find.

Sixth, the round table on a single central foot, the shape of the classic Paris café table, and it seats more people than its size suggests. Four legs at the corners decide where the chairs go: nobody can sit astride a corner, and every knee that meets a leg costs a place. With one central foot there are no corners and nothing to straddle, so chairs slide round to wherever they’re needed. As a working guide, a top ninety centimetres across, three feet, seats four, and one of a hundred and twenty, four feet, seats six. The foot has to be heavy and spread wide, because a single pedestal is only as steady as its base; press down on the edge of a second-hand one before you buy, and walk away if it tips. I find it looks more expensive too: with no legs crowding the floor beneath it, a small dining corner reads as open and calm. Tonight, count the knees that meet a leg at your own table. You’re past the halfway mark, and the rubber band is two pieces away. First, try the top drawer of your chest; if it sticks, every tug wears it further.

The seventh lesson lives in that chest of drawers: on an old one, a sticking drawer can simply be sitting in the wrong hole. On a hand-made chest, each drawer was planed to fit one opening, and makers often marked them, a pencil number or a few knife nicks on the back or side, so each could find its place again. A well-fitted set is so close that pushing one drawer shut can nudge its neighbour open, because the air has nowhere else to go, and that soft puff is what an expensive chest feels like. In our flat, Thierry, my husband, pulls every drawer out and reads the backs before I’m allowed near one with sandpaper. “Sand it to fit the wrong hole,” he says, “and it’ll rattle in its own.” Once each drawer is home, rub a white candle stub along its lower edges and the runners, ten minutes with a candle you already own. Never oil them: oil soaks in, stays tacky and turns dust into grit. After dinner, read the backs of your drawers for numbers. Now open the drawer where the good cutlery lives, and look for that rubber band.

Eighth is the sideboard and its silver drawer, and that rubber band shouldn’t be anywhere near it. Tarnish is silver reacting with sulphur, and rubber is made with sulphur, so a band left round a bundle of spoons can print a dark stripe exactly where it lay. Egg yolk does the same, which is why old egg spoons were often gilded in the bowl, and so does wool felt. In a lot of French homes, the good cutlery waits in a fitted case in the sideboard of la salle à manger, the room kept for meals, and silver that comes out bright is what makes a laid table look costly. Silver used and washed often rarely needs polish, because each wash lifts the film before it can darken. Polish takes a little metal every time, and on plate, pink or yellow at the rims means the base metal is showing. Wrap rarely used pieces in acid-free tissue or a silver cloth: an evening’s wrapping, and a cloth bag under twenty dollars. Before you set the table, take the rubber band out of that drawer. Then look at the chopping board you use most, for rings like a tree stump.

For the ninth, a butcher’s block, and those rings tell you which way its wood was cut. Rings mean end grain: the ends of the fibres stand up like the bristles of a brush, so a knife slips between them and they close again behind it. Long stripes mean the fibres lie flat, so every cut slices across them, the board scars, and the knife dulls sooner. Old blocks from French butchers’ shops turn up at flea markets with tops worn into a shallow dish by decades of cleavers, and nothing new looks that earned. An end-grain board costs noticeably more than a flat one and repays it in knives that keep their edge. Wood hates soaking: rinse it, scrape it and dry it standing on edge, never in a dishwasher, where heat and water open the glued joints. Once a month, rub in food-safe mineral oil until the wood stops drinking it, five minutes from a bottle costing a few dollars. Wash your board tonight and leave it standing on its edge. If a pendulum clock in your home stopped years ago, the belief that it’s broken may be wrong.

Tenth on the list is the tall pendulum clock, and one that stops after a few minutes is usually standing crooked, not broken. Tick and tock should fall at exactly even intervals; if they limp, the clock is out of beat, the pendulum gets its push unevenly, and its swing soon dies. The long-pendulum clocks of the Franche-Comté stand in a lot of French family homes, and like any pendulum clock, they often stop after a move. Slide a thin card under one side until the beat evens out, then fix the case to the wall; twenty minutes, and a scrap of card. If it stops even in beat, the oil in the movement has often thickened, and here I’d spend real money: a clockmaker’s clean and service often runs to a few hundred dollars, and it’s worth it, because old oil and dust make a paste that grinds the pivots. Don’t add oil yourself; on a dirty movement it only makes more paste. A tall clock gives a room the strong upright line expensive rooms have, and a running one, ma chère, makes it sound lived in. Stand beside the clock tonight and listen for an even beat. Now turn the chair nearest you round, and look at its back.

So, the back of that chair. In eighteenth-century France, armchairs came in two families: flat-backed ones, made to stand against the walls and often left plain behind, and lighter curved-back ones, finished as carefully behind as in front, made to be carried into the room and turned towards whoever was talking. Museum curators still read it that way: carving on the back means the chair was meant to move. Your own furniture answers the same test: bare board, staples or plain lining behind means it wants a wall, and a back as good as its front means it was made to stand free. The dresser, the wardrobe, the chest, the sideboard and the clock are wall pieces, heavy and plain behind, so standing still they read as part of the building. The café chair, the armchairs and the round table travel, light and finished all round, made to go where people are. To my eye, a room looks expensive when it keeps that division and thrown together when it breaks it, with a heavy piece floating, raw back on show. A chair turned to face someone also works better, because you follow a voice more easily when you can see the face it comes from. Madame Rivière’s sitting room, on the Quai Saint-Vincent, was the finest room I ever worked in: her armoire and her clock never moved, yet her two light armchairs were never where I’d left them. By the time a visitor sat down, she’d turned one to face them. Walk round your pieces tonight, give every plain back a wall, and let one light chair loose.

Three questions will sort any piece of furniture. Does its back belong against a wall or out in the room, and is it standing where that back says? Where does it take its wear, and is something there to absorb it: a groove, a bias strip, a candle, a cloth? And can your body use it without a fight? Start tonight with the napkins between your good plates, two minutes’ work; the clock and the drawers deserve an afternoon when nothing else is waiting, ma chère.
