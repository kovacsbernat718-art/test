You’ve done the big clear-out, filled the car for the thrift store and bought matching baskets, and your home still won’t stay clutter-free for more than two weeks. The hall table goes first, under a parcel, a scarf and a takeaway menu, then the arm of the sofa. Plenty of French homes fight this far less, and tidier people aren’t the reason. Less comes in, and what leaves goes on a schedule; the first half shows in many apartment buildings, where letterboxes carry a small sticker turning advertising away. It comes down to twelve rules, and with them a cleared surface stays clear by itself. Rule three is the good tablecloth at the back of your cupboard, splitting along its creases while you save it. And rule twelve explains the other eleven: a one-week test showing what you’d never carry back into a room.

The first rule puts your tallest, busiest furniture on the wall the door is in. In most bedrooms it’s the reverse: the clothes rail, the laundry basket and the bookcase with the printer face the door, the first thing you meet. Walk into a room and your eyes go straight ahead and a little to each side; the wall you’ve just come through is the one you can’t see until you turn round. Whatever stands against it is invisible on the way in, and the far wall makes your impression of the room. Home stagers use this: the view from the threshold goes to a window, a bed or one lamp, and anything bulky stands where a visitor has already passed it. Same room, same things, calm or crowded depending on which wall carries them. Moving a basket or a rail takes ten minutes and costs nothing; a tall bookcase keeps its wall strap in the new spot. Tonight, stop on your bedroom threshold and name the first three things you see. If one is a bag of things you mean to give away, rule two is about that bag.

Ten minutes, nothing bought, and the room reads calmer: most of keeping a home clear is fixes that small. Colette’s Home Bible gathers dozens: storage when you can’t build a cupboard, the width a path through a room needs, and the room nobody uses. It’s in the description below, with a QR code on the screen.

Rule number two is a habit: anything leaving your home gets a departure date. That bag, wherever it sits, has probably been on its way out since spring. In France, these things leave on a calendar. From spring to autumn, towns and neighbourhoods hold le vide-grenier, a street sale whose name says what it’s for: clear out the attic. A private person may sell their own used things at these sales no more than twice a year, and the organiser has every seller sign a statement saying so. So a household rarely sells piece by piece. It gathers what’s going for months, in one box, for one Sunday, and on that Sunday the box goes. A box without a date is furniture with a lid. Give yours one: the next charity collection day or yard sale near you, in your calendar too. When the day comes, the box leaves unopened, because reopening it is how things climb back out. A marker pen, one minute. Before bed, write the date on it, big enough to read from the door. Then open the cupboard where you keep things for a special day.

Third, a mistake careful households make: saving the good tablecloth, which is wearing out while it waits. Find it, folded in the same quarters for years, and hold a crease up to the window. Linen is a stiff fibre with very little give, so a sharp fold left in place bends the same threads at the same point until they weaken and split. That’s why old linen so often tears along its folds while the rest is perfect. In many French families, le linge de maison, the home’s own cloth rather than anyone’s, came down as a trousseau with embroidered initials, and plenty of it has hardly touched a table. My grandmother, Mémé Suzanne, sewed for other people’s rooms all her life, and she refolded her own tablecloths every few months, in thirds and then in quarters, so no thread held a crease for long. Using the cloth is better still, ma chère: every wash gives it a new fold. Refolding takes two minutes and costs nothing. This evening, refold one saved thing along new lines, or lay it for supper. Then go to your front door, where the paper comes in.

Paper is the fourth rule, and it gets stopped at the letterbox, before it reaches a table. Look at the fan on your hall table: a pizza menu, a supermarket leaflet, a catalogue you never asked for, two credit card offers. Much of it can be stopped before it’s printed. In French apartment buildings the letterboxes usually stand together in the downstairs hall, and plenty carry that sticker refusing advertising, so leaflet distributors are meant to pass them by and the flyers never climb the stairs. Your version takes ten minutes, once. In the United States, the credit bureaus run an official opt-out for pre-approved credit and insurance offers: five years if you do it online, permanent if you post the signed form. Most catalogue companies will drop you from their list if you ask. Then move where the post gets opened: at the recycling bin, the moment it arrives. Envelopes and leaflets go straight in, and only what needs doing reaches a surface, in a single folder. After supper, open today’s post standing at the bin. When you take the recycling out, open the drawer where the shopping bags live.

Number five sounds like a rule about bags, and it decides where anything should live: where you’ll need it next, not where you emptied it. Your bag drawer is probably bursting: carrier bags folded into triangles, tote bags from conferences, a cool bag with a broken zip. They’re there because you unpack in the kitchen. Then you leave without one, buy another at the till, and the drawer grows by one. French shops have been barred for years from handing out thin plastic bags at the checkout, so a lot of French shoppers keep a folded bag where it will be wanted: on the coat hook, in a pocket, in the car. A bag that lives by the door gets used; a bag that lives in the kitchen gets kept. The umbrella and the dog’s leash obey the same rule. Keep as many bags as fit flat inside one, hang that one with your coat, and put the rest in the dated box from rule two. Five minutes, no money. When you get home tonight, sort the drawer that way. Then look at the small things lying loose on the hall table.

Sixth: small things never lie loose, because the eye counts objects, not volume. On that hall table there are probably keys, coins, sunglasses, a lip balm, a receipt and a hair tie. They’d fit in your two hands, and still the table looks messy. Ten small things scattered across a surface read as ten; the same ten inside one rim read as one, because the eye groups whatever shares a boundary. It’s why a good hotel stands its little bottles on a dish. A tray does a second job, too: its rim is a limit, and an overflowing tray tells you to empty it, which a bare table never does. So, one tray or shallow dish on each surface that collects things, smaller than the surface, so most of the wood stays bare. A plate will do for now, and a tray from a second-hand shop costs a few dollars. Last thing tonight, sweep everything loose onto one plate and look at it from the front door. You’re at the halfway mark, the one-week test is waiting at the end, and the keys on that plate deserve a better home first.

Lesson seven comes from a workshop: everything that wanders gets a home that shows when it’s empty. Think of what you hunted for this week: keys, scissors, the tape measure, a charger. Each probably lives in a drawer, and a drawer can’t tell you what isn’t in it. A hook can. An empty hook shows from across the room, so the gap does the asking and the thing goes back before anyone searches. Workshops settled this long ago with the shadow board, where each tool hangs over its own painted outline, so a missing one leaves a shape. My husband, Thierry, checks his own board last thing before he locks the workshop: one bare silhouette and he knows a chisel is buried on a bench. He’d outline every object in our flat if I let him; I say only the few that wander need it. Small hooks by the door or a magnetic strip in the kitchen cost a few dollars and twenty minutes. Once the dishes are done, give this week’s three lost things a hook each. Then look at your shelf of ornaments, which you’ve probably stopped seeing.

The eighth habit is letting ornaments take turns. On that shelf: candles, a little glass bird, a souvenir, three framed photographs. Each is un bibelot, the French word for a small thing kept only to be looked at, and after a few weeks in one spot none of them is being looked at at all. Your senses stop reporting whatever never changes, just as you stop feeling the watch on your wrist. A crowded shelf of things you no longer see becomes one grey blur, and that blur is what clutter looks like. Large museums meet this from the other side: most of what they own sits in storerooms, and the displays change so visitors keep noticing. Do what a curator does. Keep three or five things out, wrap the rest in tissue in one box, and swap a few each time the clocks change. An ornament back from the box looks new for weeks, ma chère, like a fresh present. Twenty minutes, in a box you already own. Before the evening’s out, clear one shelf and put back three. The rest need a cupboard, and the shelf you choose decides whether they come out again.

For the ninth, count movements: putting a thing away must take one, or it won’t happen. The frying pan lives on the stove, the hairdryer on the bathroom shelf, the blender on the counter, and each has a perfectly good cupboard. The difference is timing. You take a thing out at the start of a job, fresh, and put it back at the end, tired, often one-handed. Open a door, lift the colander off the stack, slide the pan in, put the colander back: four movements, and at ten at night four is too many. Kitchen designers plan around this, keeping what’s used daily between hip and shoulder height, at the front, with nothing stacked on top. So swap them. The everyday pan gets the easy shelf, and the box of ornaments and the once-a-year fondue set go high, low or to the back. Half an hour, and nothing spent. When the kitchen’s done for the night, notice what’s still out and count the movements its home demands. Then reach up to the high shelf you just filled, where a lot of things came home only because they were reduced.

Ten is about timing: buy on a calendar, never on a discount. Think of what came home marked down: a sweater in the leftover colour, a gadget too cheap to leave behind. France holds its official clearance sales twice a year, winter and summer, on dates the state sets in advance. Promotions happen in between, but many French shoppers save their wants for those two. The waiting is the point: a want that must last until January survives or fades, and you learn which before paying. So keep a dated waiting list on your phone, and buy nothing until it has stayed there thirty days; in my experience, you cross out most lines yourself. Now the other side, because waiting isn’t always the saving. A mattress you sleep on every night, the pan you cook in daily, a winter coat you’ll wear for ten years: buy the best you can afford, at full price if need be, because one good thing that lasts brings nothing in behind it, while a cheap one that fails sends you back to the shop. The list takes a minute and costs nothing. On the sofa tonight, add the last three things you nearly bought. Then open the cupboard where unwanted presents wait.

Eleventh, a rule for other people’s arrivals: give things that get used up, and ask for them too. Somewhere there’s a cupboard of presents: coasters, a joke mug, a photo frame, a craft kit you’ll never start. A French dinner guest usually brings flowers, chocolates or wine, and each is made to vanish: flowers fade, chocolates get eaten, wine gets poured. That’s exactly why they’re chosen. The thanks is said, the evening is remembered, and nothing needs dusting for ten years. A gift that gets finished can’t turn into a duty, because nobody asks whether you kept the chocolates. So copy both halves. When you give, choose something that will be eaten, poured, burnt or worn out. When people ask what you’d like, answer in the same spirit: coffee from their town, good olive oil, lunch out together. Most relatives are glad of a straight answer, ma chère, because they were guessing too. One message before the next birthday, typed in a minute. Tonight, pick one unused present from that cupboard and message someone who’ll use it this month. Then pick the smallest room you have, because the last rule needs one, and a week.

And the twelfth rule explains the other eleven: empty a room, and let back in only what you go out to fetch. Take that small room, or one cupboard, and carry every movable thing into the hall. Each time you need something during the week, fetch it and give it back its place. Whatever’s still in the hall at the week’s end has answered for itself. It works because of an imbalance nobody notices. A thing already in a room costs nothing to keep, because keeping asks for no decision. A thing in the hall has to be lifted, carried and found a place, and in your arms it gets weighed honestly for the first time. Two American economists, William Samuelson and Richard Zeckhauser, named that pull of whatever is already there in nineteen eighty-eight: status quo bias. Most of these rules tip that balance. The dated box makes leaving the default. The sticker, the waiting list and the gifts make arriving the exception. The tray, the hook and the turning ornaments expose whatever stays without a reason. Whenever my father, Jean-Marc, came to paint a room, the family emptied it into the hall first. When he packed up his brushes there was always a heap they’d stepped round for days and never carried back; more than one family asked him to take it away with his dust sheets. An hour to empty, a week to wait, no money at all. Start small tonight: one cupboard, emptied into a box in the hall, and only what you go back for returns.

Before you tidy a room, then, hold each thing in it to three questions. If this stood in the hall, would I carry it back in? Does it live where I’ll need it next, somewhere that shows me when it’s gone? And what decides when the next thing like it comes through the door: a date, a list, or a till? Rule one needs nothing but your eyes and a doorway, so it comes first; the cupboard test belongs to a quieter week, when the hall can stay full for seven days. Write me one thing underneath, in a few words: the date on your give-away box, and where it’s going. Your bedroom door is nearest, so open it now, stop on the threshold, and look.
