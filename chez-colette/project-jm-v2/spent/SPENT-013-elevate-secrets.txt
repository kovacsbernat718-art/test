You’ve elevated your space before, with new sheets, a better sofa and fresh white paint on the door frames, and each time the lift faded within a few years instead of lasting forever. Sheets bought for their thousand threads. A sofa arm worn shiny by its second winter. Frames gone the colour of old piano keys. Plenty of older French flats escape this, and money isn’t the reason. People there walk on wooden floors older than anyone in the building and sit in armchairs on their third or fourth cover. Those homes are built from things renewed instead of replaced, and eleven secrets explain how. Number nine is in your fruit bowl, deciding how long your flowers live. And number eleven shows how a stranger can guess the year your sitting room was furnished.

The first secret is on the label of your sheets: thread count measures less than you think. It counts threads in one square inch, and past about five hundred, ordinary single threads can hardly be packed tighter. So big numbers usually come from twisting two or three fine strands into each thread and counting every strand. A thousand on the packet can mean roughly three hundred and thirty real threads, each a tiny cord, woven into heavier cloth that breathes less. Good single-ply cotton sits around two to four hundred. And the most prized sheets in an old French linen cupboard often count under a hundred and fifty, because they’re linen: flax spins a thick, strong thread, so fewer fit, and the cloth softens with every wash for decades. Old linen sheets are a staple on French flea-market tables, often for less than new. So buy by fibre and the word single-ply, never by the count; checking takes ten seconds. As you make the bed tonight, find the sewn-in label and look for the word ply beside the number. Then take down one of the plates you keep for best; the thinnest hold a surprise.

Ten seconds with a label can save the price of your next sheets. Secret number two: the china you save for best is probably tougher than your everyday plates. Strength comes from firing heat more than thickness. Plenty of chunky everyday sets are earthenware, fired cool enough to stay porous and quick to chip. Porcelain is fired far hotter, around thirteen hundred degrees Celsius, well over two thousand Fahrenheit, until the clay partly turns to glass, so thin porcelain, bone china especially, often survives a knock that chips earthenware. Hold a good plate to a lamp and you may see your fingers’ shadow through it. Flick the rim: porcelain rings, earthenware clacks. Limoges became France’s porcelain town once white clay was found nearby in the eighteenth century, and French department stores give a section to les arts de la table, the dressing of a table and the craft of setting it. So move the good plates to the shelf you reach daily, and wash gold or platinum rims by hand, since a dishwasher wears the metal away. Five minutes, no cost. At supper, eat off one. Then look down at the floor under the table, which may have more lives left than you’d guess.

Third, a heating vent tells you how many lives your floor has left. Lift a floor-vent grille and look at the cut edge of the board. Solid wood can be sanded back to fresh timber many times. A thin skin of wood over plywood is engineered, and only that skin can be sanded; a printed sheet over fibreboard is laminate, with nothing to sand. Each full sanding takes off roughly a millimetre, a thirty-second of an inch, so on solid boards the wood above the tongue is the floor’s whole future. That’s how older French flats keep floors that outlive tenants: solid oak, often in point de Hongrie, chevron with each board’s end cut on a slant, sanded between owners. In an old flat, my husband, Thierry, once lifted a board sanded so often its tongue showed. “One more,” he said, “and it’s finished.” So before replacing a tired floor, price a sanding: a day or two’s work, usually a fraction of new boards. Buying engineered, ask for a top layer of three millimetres, an eighth of an inch, or more. Later, lift one grille and read its edge. Then grab the sofa on that floor by a front corner.

Fourth comes a test for any sofa: lift it by one front corner, about fifteen centimetres, six inches, and watch the other front leg. If it leaves the floor almost at once, the frame is rigid and its joints are tight. If it stays down while the sofa twists, every evening of sitting is working those joints looser. In France plenty of towns still have an upholsterer who will strip a worn armchair to its frame and dress it again. Re-covering isn’t cheap, though. Once a sofa has been stripped, re-webbed and re-dressed, the bill can approach the price of a cheap new one, so it only makes sense on a frame that passes. That’s why I’d pay more for a sofa that passes, ma chère, and accept a plainer cover to afford it: the frame is the part you keep. Second-hand, one that passes is often the best value in the shop. The test takes ten seconds and costs nothing. Tonight, lift your own before you sit. Then pull out a drawer in the oldest wooden piece you own and look at its screws, because they know its age.

Number five is a date hidden in every old screw. Until about the middle of the nineteenth century, screws were finished partly by hand: the slot often sits off centre, the thread wanders, and the tip is blunt, because sharp points only became standard once machines took over. A cross-shaped head generally means the nineteen-thirties or later, or a repair. A chest sold as antique can be forty years old under a distressed finish, so check the screws in its lock, hinges and handles. One or two newer ones only mean someone mended it. In France, old furniture passes from home to home through flea markets and dealers rather than sitting in storage, and plenty of French flats are half furnished that way. One old piece stops a room looking as if it all arrived on one delivery truck. Carry a torch and a pocket screwdriver when you hunt, a few dollars together; an old solid chest often sells for less than new flat-pack. This evening, read the screws in the oldest wooden thing you own. Then pull a chair out from the table and press a thumb into its seat.

Warm water is the sixth secret, and it tightens a sagging cane seat. Press the seat of a cane chair; many sag into a hollow over the years, and people assume it’s finished. Natural cane is the glossy outer skin of the rattan palm, cut into strips, and it swells when wet and shrinks as it dries. Caners weave it damp so the seat pulls taut as it dries, and yours can do that again. Turn the chair over and sponge warm water onto the rough underside, keeping the glossy top dry. Leave it overnight, away from radiators and direct sun, and give it a day before anyone sits. Cane-seated chairs crowd French flea markets, and plenty still stand round kitchen tables. It works on natural cane only, and broken strands need a caner, who still re-weaves by hand. Five minutes and a sponge. While the kettle boils tonight, wet the seat that gives most. Six secrets in, the fruit bowl is three away and the room a stranger can date follows it. First, look at the white paint on the nearest door frame, in the corner light never reaches.

Yellowing white woodwork is number seven, and darkness is the cause. If your skirting boards or door frames have gone the colour of butter, they were probably painted in oil-based gloss. Oil paint goes on changing for years and yellows fastest where it gets least light: behind a picture, inside a wardrobe. Daylight partly bleaches it back, which is why the frame beside a window often looks whitest. Jean-Marc, my father, only had to open a client’s old wardrobe to read its paint: butter inside, white outside, oil every time. Water-based enamels yellow far less. To learn which you have, rub a hidden spot with a cotton ball dampened in rubbing alcohol: water-based paint softens onto the cotton, oil stays put. Water-based paint laid straight over oil can peel off in sheets, so the old gloss needs washing, sanding and a bonding primer first. In an older home, have a chip tested for lead before sanding; if positive, call a professional. Two tins and a weekend do the doors. Tonight, try the cotton ball inside a cupboard door. Then glance at your sofa’s arms, where the fabric has gone shiny.

The eighth discovery is on the back of every upholstery sample, predicting how soon a sofa wears through. Before a fabric is sold for seating, a machine rubs it with a standard abrasive until it shows wear, and the count goes on the label: Martindale rubs in Europe, double rubs in America. Almost all the rubbing on a sofa lands on the arms and the front edge of the seat, which is why covers give out there while the back looks new. For daily seating, upholsterers generally look for twenty thousand or more on the European scale, or fifteen thousand double rubs on the American one. Some loose linens and silks rate far lower, fine for cushions, curtains or a chair used twice a month. When you order a cover, ask for an extra metre, about a yard, made into arm caps, small loose covers that take the rubbing and go in the wash. That metre costs about a dinner out. Swap the seat cushions weekly so wear spreads. Before the evening ends, look up your sofa fabric’s rating. Then go to the fruit bowl, because something in it is working against your flowers.

Ninth, as promised, the fruit bowl, shortening the life of the bouquet beside it. Ripening fruit gives off ethylene, a gas plants use as a signal to ripen and to age. Bananas, apples, pears and tomatoes release plenty, and a great many cut flowers answer it: buds that never open, petals that drop early, a bunch finished days ahead of time. So a vase beside the fruit on the kitchen table is close to the worst spot in the house. In a lot of French towns, flowers come home from market in the same basket as the peaches, and that’s the moment to separate them. Give the vase its own room, away from fruit, radiators and sun. Recut the stems on a slant, strip every leaf that would sit underwater, and change the water every two days, because bacteria in old water clog the stems. A bunch kept like that lasts noticeably longer, ma chère. Ten minutes, and only the flowers to pay for. Right now, carry the bowl or the vase into another room. Then open your wardrobe, where a bag of dried flowers is meant to guard your wool.

Mistake number ten is trusting the lavender bag, and a moth hole is never made by the moth you see. Holes in a sweater or a rug are chewed by larvae, tiny caterpillars; the adult clothes moth doesn’t eat at all, it only lays eggs. The larvae live on keratin, the protein in wool, silk and feathers, and they favour wool left undisturbed in the dark, slightly soiled. That’s why damage appears on the strip of rug under the sofa, or in the sweater put away after one wear, and almost never where you walk. Lavender bags fill a great many French linen cupboards and smell lovely, but at best deter a passing adult while fresh, and do nothing to larvae already feeding. Light, movement and cleanliness protect wool. So it goes into storage washed or cleaned, and the rug under the furniture gets vacuumed monthly. Kept that way, a good wool rug can outlast its buyer. Ten minutes with the nozzle, nothing spent. After dinner, slide the sofa forward and look at the edge of rug it has been hiding. From there, find the object that gives away the year you furnished the room.

And the eleventh, the one that lets a stranger date your sitting room, explains the other ten. Most rooms can be dated from one photograph to within a decade, and the more of a room bought at once, the closer the guess. Avocado green and harvest gold mean the seventies. A grey sofa on a grey floor between white walls means the twenty-tens. A trend reaches paint, fabric and furniture together, so a room furnished in one go takes its date from the shopping, then ages all at once. No one planned that. Shops need something new each season, new only sells once the old looks old, and a colour crowned for a single year is that habit with a name on it: incentives and routine, not a scheme. Linen, porcelain, solid floors, sound frames, old chests, cane seats, woodwork that stays white, cloth rated for sitting, wool kept from moths: not one of the ten tells anyone the year, ma chère. In a lot of older French homes, a room comes together across decades, and there’s a proverb for it: petit à petit, l’oiseau fait son nid, a bird builds its nest twig by twig. My grandmother, Mémé Suzanne, re-covered one Lyon family’s armchair three times in her working life, each new cloth on the same beech frame. “The wood is the chair,” she said. “The cloth is only the year.” So let the year live in small, cheap things: a cushion cover, a lampshade, a print. Tonight, stand in the sitting-room doorway and find the one thing that gives your year away.

All eleven come down to one pattern: the money goes into what can be washed, sanded, tightened, re-covered or simply used, and the year is left to things small enough to swap. None needs doing this weekend; taking them slowly is rather the point. Start with the quickest, the plates: ten seconds, a fingernail and two rims. The sofa corner and the cotton ball can follow, since neither costs a cent. When you lifted your sofa by its corner, did the far leg rise with it or stay on the floor?
