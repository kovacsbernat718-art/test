There are thirteen ideas built into ordinary French homes that would fix something in your home this week, and almost none of them needs a plug. Your bedroom is an oven by four in the afternoon, with the curtains drawn. The whole household queues for one bathroom at seven. The hall light burns at noon, and the dryer is quietly wearing out your towels. You've already paid to fight it: the blackout blind, the second fan, the fabric softener. A great many French homes have no air conditioning and no tumble dryer at all, and saving money is only part of the reason. Their answer is usually a plain thing put in a cleverer place, like a shutter on the far side of the glass. Thirteen of those, then, and you can copy most of them for little or no money. Number four is about the grey felt in your dryer's lint filter, and what it is made of. And number thirteen begins in a French kitchen that is not there.

The first idea is the shutter, and what matters is which side of the glass it is on. When the sun is on your window you draw the curtain or drop the blind, and the room goes dim and stays hot. By then the heat is already indoors. Sunlight passes through glass almost untouched, strikes the curtain and turns into warmth, and that warmth is a kind the glass will not let back out. It is the reason a parked car gets hotter than the street around it. On a lot of French houses and older flats you will see persiennes, slatted wooden shutters hinged to the outside wall. On a hot day they are pulled shut on the sunny side before the sun arrives, and everything is thrown open once the evening air is cooler than the room. Without shutters, hang the shade outdoors. A roll-up reed blind from the garden aisle, fixed above the outside of the frame, is an afternoon's work and costs less than a fan, and real shutters or an awning earn their price on a window that faces west. Tomorrow, close the blind on the sunny window first thing in the morning, while the room is still cool. That same window has a second trick, and it only shows itself after dark.

Idea number two hangs at that window: two curtains, not one, because of a reversal that happens at dusk. A thin sheer scatters light, and the eye sees through it towards whichever side is brighter. By day the street is far brighter than your room, so from outside the sheer is a blank pale panel. Then you switch on a lamp. Now your room is the bright side, the glass becomes a dark mirror for you, and from the pavement every detail of your evening is on show. So a French window very often wears two layers: a sheer that stays drawn while it is light, and a heavier, lined pair pulled across the moment the lamps go on. My grandmother, Mémé Suzanne, hung curtains in other people's flats for a living, and when a pair was up she sent me down to the street at dusk. "What can you see?" she would call from the window. A sheer on a tension rod inside the frame needs no drill and ten minutes, and the rod with a plain voile panel comes to under twenty dollars. Tonight, switch on your usual lamp, step outside and look back at your own window. From the window everybody can see into, go to the narrowest door in a French flat, which hides the cleverest wall in it.

Third, a wall, and it stands between the toilet and the bath. In a great many French homes the toilet has a tiny room of its own, with its own door, often off the hall, and the bathroom proper holds only the basin and the bath or shower. Put all three in a single room and that room serves one person at a time. Split them and two people are served at once, so one bathroom does most of the work of two. Its flaw is that the older ones have no basin, which is why newer French ones squeeze a hand basin the size of a dinner plate into a corner. If you ever redo a bathroom, have the plumber price that wall before anything else. Until then, use the principle: a queue forms wherever several jobs share one room. Teeth need a basin. Hair, make-up and an electric razor need only a mirror and a socket, so give them a mirror in the bedroom; that's ten minutes with a mirror you already own. Tomorrow morning, notice who is waiting, and for which of those jobs. And while they wait, a machine down the corridor is running with the filter I promised you inside it.

Moving a mirror is the size of most fixes a home really needs, and I have collected a great many more. The curtain measurements, how many lamps a room wants and where, what to do in a rented flat and a chapter for each room are in Colette's Home Bible, through the link in the description, or the QR code on the screen. Now, that filter.

The folding drying rack is the fourth idea, and it starts with the grey felt. Lint is not dust. It is fibre, rubbed off your clothes and towels as they tumble in hot air. Every load leaves a little of itself in that filter, which is how a towel goes thin and a shirt gives way at the seams. A lot of French households own no dryer. The washing goes on a folding rack, and it is usually dry by the next day. Moving air dries cloth as surely as heat does. Wet fabric wraps itself in a thin layer of damp air, and until that layer is carried off, little more can evaporate. So the method is spacing: ten centimetres, about four inches, between pieces, shirts on hangers, and the rack standing where air passes, beside a window or in a doorway. A rack sells for less than thirty dollars, and hanging a load takes five minutes longer than filling a drum. Towels do dry stiffer; five minutes in the dryer at the end softens them. This evening, pull out your lint filter and look at its colour, because it is the colour of your last wash. All that water has left the washing, ma chère, and it is now in the air of the room.

For the fifth, the French habit that sends that damp air outside, and it is the opposite of a window left ajar. A window cracked open all day lets out a thin stream of air and a steady stream of heat. The wall around it cools, and a cool wall is where water settles. First thing in the morning the bedroom windows are thrown wide, the duvet is folded back over the foot of the bed, and ten minutes later everything is shut again, winter included. In those minutes most of the air in the room is exchanged. The walls, the floor and the furniture hold far more warmth than the air does, and they have barely begun to cool, so the new air is warm again soon after the window closes. Wet air out, heat kept. It costs nothing, and it fits inside the time the coffee takes. If the panes still run with water every morning after a fortnight of this, or dark spots appear, airing is not the answer, and a building professional should look for the cause. Tomorrow, open the bedroom window fully before you do anything else. Then guard the warmth you have just kept, because most homes let it straight out through the front door.

Number six is a second door between you and the street. If your front door opens straight into the room where you sit, every time it opens a slab of that room's warm air is swapped for outdoor air. An older French flat puts a room in the way. It is called l'entrée, a small hall of its own with a door to each room, and in winter those doors are kept shut. Two doors in a row behave like a lock on a canal. Only the little hall changes its air; the room behind the second door hardly notices. Some older French houses add a third layer, a heavy lined curtain on a rail behind the front door, drawn across at night. If you have no hall, that curtain is the part to copy. A rail above the door frame and a thick second-hand curtain, or a wool blanket with rings clipped on, is an hour's work for under forty dollars. Before you go to bed, run the back of your hand around the edge of your front door and feel where the cold comes in. Six ideas, then, with the dryer's secret behind you and the missing kitchen still ahead. And the seventh is the one I would keep if I had to give back the other twelve.

The seventh idea is the top of the window, and it sits in the middle of this list because the two after it depend on it. How far daylight travels into a room is decided by how high the glass goes, far more than by how wide it is. Light from the top of the pane is the light that reaches the back wall. As a working rule, good daylight reaches about twice as far into a room as the top of the glass stands above the floor. Cover the top thirty centimetres of a window, the top foot, and it is the back of the room that goes dark. That is why old French windows climb almost to the ceiling. In the building on the Croix-Rousse hill in Lyon where I was a child, the windows stop a few inches short of it, and on a winter afternoon you could read at the table against the far wall with no lamp. The usual window has a blind lowered a third of the way to look tidy, and curtains bunched over the upper corners. Raising the blinds to the very top is free and takes a minute. Moving a pole fifteen centimetres, six inches, above the frame, and wide enough for the curtains to hang on wall instead of glass, takes an hour. Tomorrow morning, raise every blind fully and look at the wall furthest from the window. Once daylight reaches that wall, ma chère, it can do something better still, and go through it.

Eighth comes glass, in the doors inside your home. A solid door is a shutter, and most homes keep theirs closed across the only route daylight has into the hall. In older French flats the door between the hall and the sitting room is very often glazed, in small panes from the handle up. One window ends up lighting two rooms. A hall needs very little light to feel like part of the home, and what comes through a glazed door arrives free, all day. A salvaged glazed door often costs less than a new lamp, and a joiner can swap the upper panels of a door you own for glass in half a day; ask for toughened glass, reeded or frosted if the room behind needs privacy. In a rented flat, simply keep the door of your brightest room open through the day. The hall that stays dark anyway is page thirty-nine of the book, The Room That Gets No Sun, because some rooms can only ever be lit by borrowing. Tomorrow at noon, switch off the hall light and open the doors onto it one at a time, to learn which room feeds it. After dark that hall needs a lamp, and French stairwells have a famous way of making sure nobody leaves one on.

The ninth is a light that cannot be left on. In French apartment buildings the staircase light works on a timer. You press a small glowing button on the landing, every floor lights up, and two or three minutes later it all goes dark by itself. Anyone who has lived in one, ma chère, has been caught between floors with the shopping, feeling along the wall for the next button. But look at what the timer does. The question of who switches off was answered once, on the day the switch was fitted, and never has to be asked again. Your home has stairwells of its own: the closet, the pantry, the garage, the porch, the cellar steps. For a closet or a pantry, a stick-on battery light with a motion sensor costs a few dollars and goes up in a minute. For a wired light, a countdown switch or an occupancy sensor replaces the ordinary one, and that is a short job for an electrician, not for you. Leave alone the rooms where you sit still, or you'll spend your evenings waving at the ceiling. Before bed tonight, walk through your home and count the lights burning in rooms with nobody in them. Every one of those lights falls on a painted wall, and paint is where an old French house painter would pick a quarrel.

Idea ten is a paint that turns into stone. Limewash is lime and water, brushed on thin. As it cures it draws carbon dioxide out of the air and hardens into the same mineral as limestone, so what stays on the wall is a skin of stone full of fine pores, not a film. Water vapour passes straight through it. That matters on an old wall of solid stone, brick or earth, which always carries a little moisture and has to let it out. Seal that wall under a modern plastic-based paint and the moisture pushes from behind, until the paint blisters in the same place every other year. My father, Jean-Marc, painted houses in Lyon, and he never painted over a blister. He slit it with his knife first, to see whether the plaster behind was dark. On bare old plaster, stone or brick, limewash is a weekend's work, and the raw materials are among the cheapest in the trade; lime is strongly alkaline, so gloves and goggles. On plasterboard, or over a wall already coated in acrylic, limewash will not grip without a mineral primer, and it brings none of the benefit. There a good matte paint is the better choice, and in a rented flat, leave it. A blister that keeps returning means water is arriving from somewhere, and someone qualified has to find where before anyone paints. Tonight, press a fingertip on your worst patch of wall and feel whether it is dry and powdery, or cool. Ten ideas of thirteen. The three that remain are less about objects than about habits, and the last of them is that empty kitchen.

The eleventh is a straight line through the whole home. The eye sizes up a home by the longest distance it can see, far more than by the room it happens to be standing in; I hold that as a decorator's belief, since no tape measure will prove it. In an older French flat the main rooms are often strung along the window side with their doors lined up one behind the next. The word is enfilade, a row of rooms threaded on a single line of doorways, and with the doors open you can stand in the first room and see a window at the far end of the third. Three modest rooms on one clear line feel larger than one big room with a wall at the end of it. Find the longest line in your own home, then look at what interrupts it and what waits at its end. Very often it's a tall bookcase crowding a doorway, and a view that finishes on the side of the fridge. Move what blocks the line, and put one thing at the end that deserves the walk: a lamp left on in the evening, or a single picture. Fifteen minutes, and the only cost is the lifting. After supper, stand at your front door and find where your longest view ends. From the longest thing in a home to one of the smallest, a ring that sits beside a French plate.

Twelfth, the napkin ring, which was never an ornament. It is a name tag. In a French family each person very often has a cloth napkin and a ring of their own, and after the meal the napkin is rolled back into its ring to wait for the next one. It serves for several days and goes into the weekly wash. The ring is what makes that possible, because without a mark nobody wants a napkin that might have been someone else's. It comes from the time when laundry was done by hand, and a fresh napkin at every meal was more washing than a household could face. Once an object has an owner, it stops being disposable. A glass with a person's mark on it gets refilled all day instead of joining six others by the sink. Six plain cotton or linen napkins come to under twenty dollars. The rings can be anything that differs: a ribbon, a wooden peg with an initial, a loop of string in each person's colour. Setting it up takes one supper. At your next meal, lay cloth napkins if you own any, and give each one a mark. A napkin kept for a week is a small thing, ma chère. The last idea is the same habit applied to the largest things you own.

And the thirteenth idea is that a home can be taken apart, which brings us to that kitchen. Rent an unfurnished flat in France and the kitchen is quite often a bare room: a sink, the pipes, the sockets. The tenants arrive with their kitchen, and when they move, a good part of it leaves with them. Older flats were built without closets too, so clothes live in l'armoire, the tall wardrobe that belongs to the family, not to the building. When the big pieces of a home travel with the people, they have to survive the journey, so they were made to come apart. A traditional armoire is often held together by wooden pegs, with no glue in its main joints. The cornice lifts off, the doors lift off their hinges, the pegs are tapped out, and a wardrobe taller than a man goes down a spiral staircase as a stack of boards. Thierry, my husband, builds furniture for a living, and I have watched him do it on a landing in twenty minutes with a mallet and a wooden punch. He has one line I have never managed to answer: "If it cannot come apart, it cannot be mended." Now the chipboard wardrobe. Its fittings bite into pressed wood particles, and they grip best the first time. Take it apart and the board crumbles a little around every fitting, so every assembly after the first is looser than the one before. There is no plan behind that, only arithmetic: a low price pays for one assembly, and few buyers ask about a second. So before you pay for any large piece, ask how it comes apart. Look for solid wood or plywood, screws and bolts that go into wood, and a back that is screwed on, not stapled. Second-hand, a piece like that often costs no more than new chipboard, and this is where I would spend. Tonight, open one wardrobe door and look at how the hinge and the side are joined.

Thirteen ideas, and one pattern runs under all of them: each puts a simple thing in exactly the right place and then leaves it to work by itself. The shade outside the glass, the door before the room, the glass in the door, the name on the napkin, the peg in the joint. There is no need to do thirteen things this month. Most of them take an evening, and two or three are for the day you renovate. Begin with the easiest, the window test from the second idea, which takes two minutes. And I would like one answer from you, written under this video: when you stood at your front door, what was waiting at the end of your longest view? So switch on the lamp you sit beside, put your shoes on, and go and see what the street sees.
