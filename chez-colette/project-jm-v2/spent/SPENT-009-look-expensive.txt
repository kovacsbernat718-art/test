Switch on every light in your sitting room, stand in the doorway, and you’ll find the first thing making your home look cheaper than it is: a lamp glowing apricot beside a ceiling light glowing blue. Then a ceiling a shade greyer than its walls. A curtain hovering above the floor at one end and dragging at the other. Perhaps this year’s money went on a sofa and a rug, and the room still looks like a first rented flat. Price is rarely the cause. The eye reads a handful of small signals, most where nobody looks while shopping. Ten tricks fix them, and afterwards you can price any room from its doorway. Number six is a chest of drawers you probably own, and a two-second test that says if it’s the cheapest furniture in the room or the best. And after the tenth comes one more, unnumbered, explaining how all ten got into your home, with its proof on the back of everyday things.

The first trick is one white light per room, never two. In your sitting room the lamp by the sofa probably glows honey-yellow, the fitting in the middle of the ceiling gives a cooler, bluish white, and the hall light through the doorway adds a third with a greenish cast. Your eyes carry no fixed idea of white: they take it from the light around them, the way a phone camera sets its white balance, and in a room lit by a single kind of light they adjust so completely that its colour disappears. Put two whites in view and the eye can settle on only one. Beside a cool bulb, a warm one looks orange and grubby; beside a warm one, a cool one looks like a clinic corridor. That clash is one of the loudest cheap signals a room can give off. The white is written on the packaging as a temperature in kelvin. Around twenty-seven hundred is the warm light of a home. Four to five thousand is the light of offices and shops. The words on the front, soft, warm, bright, daylight, change from brand to brand, so read the number and ignore the words. Buy the whole room from one brand as well, because two bulbs both marked twenty-seven hundred can still disagree: that number measures only warm against cool, and one maker’s white may lean slightly green while another’s leans pink. Twenty-seven hundred for living and sleeping, three thousand at most in a kitchen. A box of four is usually under fifteen dollars, and a whole room takes ten minutes, each bulb switched off and cool before you touch it. Tonight, turn everything on and switch off whichever light disagrees with the rest. Then, with one white left, look straight up at the largest white surface you own, which has probably never looked white at all.

Trick number two is the ceiling, and the whitest white in the shop is the wrong paint for it. In daylight the ceiling is often the dimmest surface in a room. Light comes through the windows travelling sideways and downward, onto the floor and the walls, and the ceiling receives only what bounces back up, so brilliant white overhead reads as a flat grey lid. That’s why a ceiling painted last spring can already look tired, and colder than the walls it meets. The French call the ceiling le cinquième mur, the fifth wall, a name that asks you to choose its colour as carefully as the other four. When my father, Jean-Marc, had a ceiling to paint, he stirred a little of the wall colour into the white tin first, so that from below it still read as white, ma chère, but a white that belonged to the walls. Any paint counter can mix your wall colour at a quarter of its strength for the ceiling, usually for the price of an ordinary tin, and an average room is one tin and one day with a roller on a pole. Keep the finish flat, since any sheen overhead catches the window light and shows every ripple in the plaster. If you rent and cannot paint, correct it from below: a lamp whose shade is open at the top, perhaps one you already own, throws a warm circle onto the ceiling after dark and lifts that grey. Tonight, stand in a corner and follow the line where the wall meets the ceiling; if it looks like two colours meeting, the colder one is above you. Then let your eyes travel down the door frame to the hinges, and count how many were painted along with the wood.

Third: take the paint off everything that was never meant to wear it, and most of it lifts away in a pan of hot water. Look at the hinges on your doors, the screws on the switch plates, the latch, the window catches. Paint on metal is the signature of a hurried repaint, the kind done over a weekend between two tenants, and the eye reads hurried as cheap before it can say why. The metal loses its shine under a skin that cracks along the hinge every time the door swings, and dried drips hang from the screw heads. First, one check. In the United States a house built before nineteen seventy-eight, and in France a building from before nineteen forty-nine, may have lead in its oldest layers of paint, and paint like that should not be scraped, sanded or heated at home. Have a flake tested before anything goes in a pan, and if it is positive, hand those hinges to a professional. Otherwise, take off one hinge at a time, so the door hangs on the others, and drop it with its screws into an old pan you’ll never cook in again, covered with water and a squeeze of washing-up liquid. Simmer it gently for an hour or two, or leave it overnight in an old slow cooker, and the paint softens and slides off under a plastic scraper or an old toothbrush. Dry it, oil it, and screw it back before the next one comes off. The whole door is one evening. A painted plastic switch plate is quicker still: a new one is a few dollars and two screws, with that circuit off at the breaker first. Before bed, count the painted screw heads in a single room. Then sit down on the sofa and look at the cushion beside you, because its pattern is about to tell you what it cost.

Hot water and one evening, and the doors stop looking hurried; much of what reads as expensive costs no more. Colette’s Home Bible is two hundred pages of it: the bulb numbers for each room, what to do with a ceiling, how to test a colour before opening the tin, and what dimming should really do. For the fourth, a test that prices any sofa, cushion or curtain from across the room: look at whether its pattern is matched. On a well-made sofa a stripe runs from the back down across the seat cushion without a jump; the same flower sits at the same height on both curtains of a pair; a cushion carries its motif dead centre. On a cheap one the pattern lands wherever the cutting table left it: half a flower here, a stripe that breaks at every seam. Matching costs money because it eats fabric. To carry a pattern across a seam, the maker cuts away part of a repeat, and the larger the pattern, the more cloth goes in the bin. A matched pattern is cloth somebody paid for and then threw away so your eye would never stumble. It shows most clearly in toile de Jouy, the French printed cotton with little country scenes in a single colour on a pale ground, where a shepherd sliced in half by a seam is impossible to miss. The finest sofa I know belongs to Madame Rivière, in her flat on the Quai Saint-Vincent: a broad stripe that travels from the top of the back, across the seat, over the front edge and down to the floor in one unbroken line. From her doorway, ma chère, you can see that a hand made it. When you buy or sew a new cover, choose a small pattern, which wastes almost nothing, or allow one extra repeat of fabric and centre the motif; matching then costs that one repeat and an hour’s care. Tonight, spend five minutes turning and swapping the sofa cushions until every stripe agrees with its neighbour. Then look up at the picture above them, at the border around the print, because that border explains why the picture looks smaller than it is.

Fifth, a cheap print looks like a gallery piece in a wide border, as long as the border is wider at the bottom than at the top. Framers call it bottom weighting. They cut the lower margin of the mount, the card border inside the frame, a little wider than the top and the sides, by a centimetre or so, about half an inch, because the eye places the middle of any rectangle slightly above its true centre. Give a picture four equal margins and it seems to sink in its frame; give it a slightly heavier bottom and it looks exactly placed, and almost nobody could say why. Width matters as much as weighting. Most prints at home sit in ready-made frames with a thin strip of card or none at all, the glass stopping a finger’s width from the image. A small print in a generous border, eight to ten centimetres, three to four inches, all round, reads as something somebody chose to protect; the same print pressed to the edge of its frame reads as a poster. Buy a frame one or two sizes larger than the print and ask a framer to cut a mount to order with the bottom weighted, which usually costs less than a takeaway lunch, or buy a hand mount cutter and do it yourself in half an hour. Keep the card a soft off-white, never brilliant white, and the same in every frame in a room, so different prints read as one collection. This evening, lay one small print on a larger sheet of plain card with more card showing below than above, prop it up and step back. That brings you nearly to the halfway point, and the chest-of-drawers test from the beginning is next. So look at whatever that picture hangs above, because two seconds and a lamp will tell you what it is really made of.

The sixth trick matters most of all ten. I’ve put it halfway because the first five cost a few dollars each, while this one decides where your large sums go for years: veneer is not the cheap option. People see the word on a label and go looking for solid wood. Yet much of the finest furniture ever made in France, palace chests of drawers among it, is veneer: sheets of fine wood, often no thicker than a coin, glued over a carcass of plainer oak or pine. Veneer is how the most spectacular woods get used: a knotted burl or a wild figure that would crack as a solid plank stays flat as a thin sheet on a steady core. One winter my husband, Thierry, reglued a lifted corner of a walnut chest on our kitchen table and tilted it to show me the oak beneath. “That,” he said, “is the expensive way.” His trade, menuisier-ébéniste, carries it in its name: ébène is ebony, the wood the first veneer specialists in Paris cut into thin sheets. The cheap version is a printed picture of grain on paper, sealed under plastic and pressed onto particleboard. The test takes two seconds, ma chère. Find a spot where a lamp or window lights the top of your chest of drawers, and move your head from side to side. Real wood, veneered or solid, shifts as you move, light running across the grain the way it runs across silk, because its fibres catch light differently at every angle. A printed grain barely changes. A lifted edge of veneer is a small job for a restorer, while a chipped edge of print shows grey particleboard and stays chipped. Tonight, walk past every wooden-looking surface you own, head moving, and notice which shimmer. Then look down at your rug, where a shimmer can mean the opposite.

Number seven: distrust the silkiest rug in the shop. It’s soft as a cat, with a sheen that ripples when you brush a hand across it, sold as art silk or bamboo silk and priced well below wool. Almost always it is viscose, sometimes labelled rayon: wood or bamboo pulp dissolved and spun into a glossy fibre. It’s beautiful on the day it arrives. But viscose loses much of its strength when it’s wet, so a tipped glass of water can leave a mark, sometimes a brown tide line, that a cleaner struggles to shift, and wherever feet pass, the fibres crush flat and the sheen dulls into a path. Within a year or two the most expensive-looking rug in the room is the one that looks oldest. Wool behaves the other way round. Each fibre has a natural crimp, like a tiny spring, so it rises again after it has been trodden on, and its low, soft sheen doesn’t wear into tracks. The outer skin of a wool fibre repels water, too, so a spill often beads for a moment before it sinks in, long enough to blot. Before you pay, find the fibre on the label. In a room people walk through, choose wool, or a flat-woven wool, which costs less than a deep pile; a good wool rug costs more than viscose, and on a floor crossed every day, that is money that lasts. If you love a viscose rug, keep it where nobody walks, at the side of a bed. Page one hundred and nineteen, Rugs, Fibre and Pile, is there because the label under a rug tells you more than any photograph of its front. Before you sleep, fold back one corner of your own rug and read the fibre on its label. As the evening comes on, turn your lamps down, because what their light does when you dim it is the next trick.

Eighth, dim your lights as the evening goes on, and know that most LED bulbs dim the wrong way. When an old filament bulb was turned down, the filament cooled, and hot metal glows orange and then red as it cools, like an ember. So the light didn’t only fall, it warmed, sliding toward the colour of a candle, and a dimmed room turned gold. An ordinary LED holds exactly the same colour all the way down. It only gets weaker, so a dimmed LED room looks like an office after closing time: the same light, less of it. In the rented flats I worked on, I watched one client after another turn a new dimmer down on the first evening, frown, and ask why the room now looked like a waiting room. The dimmer was fine, ma chère. The bulb was the problem. Bulbs sold as dim-to-warm imitate the filament: they start near twenty-seven hundred and slide toward candlelight as you turn them down, for a few dollars more. Put them in the two or three lamps you sit beside, and give each lamp a plug-in or cord dimmer marked for LED, usually under twenty dollars. A dimmer in the wall is a different matter: the switch must be one made for LEDs, the bulbs must say dimmable, and fitting it is a job for an electrician. Then use them at l’heure bleue, the French name for the stretch of deep blue sky between sunset and full dark, switching the lamps on before the room asks for light, so the ceiling light never gets its turn. Tonight, if any lamp of yours already dims, turn it down slowly and watch whether its colour warms or only fades. Two tricks are left after this one, and then the unnumbered piece about the backs of things. Once it’s dark, draw the curtains and look at exactly where they stop.

Trick nine is a hem cut to your own floor, not to a packet. Look at the bottom edge of your curtains: a gap of a few centimetres at one end, the fabric brushing the floor at the other. That uneven gap is usually the floor’s doing, because the floor under a window is often not quite parallel to the pole above it. In many homes, older ones especially, the drop from pole to floor changes by a centimetre or more across a single window, so even a perfect length hovers at one corner and drags at the other. Cloth that ends in mid-air shortens the whole wall it hangs on, and a stranger reads it at once as bought rather than made. Ready-made curtains come in a handful of standard lengths, chosen to suit the most windows, so they fit almost none exactly. That’s why a curtain maker measures the drop at both ends and in the middle, and cuts to the shortest. For curtains you draw every day, aim for a hem that clears the floor by about a centimetre, under half an inch. For curtains that stay open, a soft break of two or three centimetres, about an inch, onto the floor is lovely. Buy the next standard length up rather than the one that looks right on the packet, and take up the hem with a needle or iron-on hemming tape, an evening’s work for a few dollars. If yours hang on clip rings, you can fine-tune each panel in minutes by gripping the fabric a little lower or higher. Page ninety-three, The Drop, exists because the length printed on a packet is almost never the length of your window. After supper, slide a ruler under each end of one curtain and write down both gaps. Then step out of the room, pull the door shut behind you, and listen to the sound it makes.

And the tenth you can test with one knuckle: the sound of your doors. Knock on an interior door at head height. A thin, boomy answer means two skins of hardboard a few millimetres thick on a slim wooden frame, with a honeycomb of cardboard between them. A low, flat note, like knuckles on a tabletop, means a solid core. What your knuckle is measuring is weight, and weight is what stops sound. A voice gets through a door by making the whole door shake, and a heavier door shakes less: as a rough rule, every doubling of its weight cuts the sound that passes through by about six decibels, enough for the ear to hear clearly. A solid-core door weighs two or three times as much as a hollow one. But sound also takes any way round, and a door that stops a finger’s width above the floor leaves an opening that lets voices past even the heaviest core. That is why a good hotel room door closes onto a rubber seal all round and a strip along the bottom. No trick turns a hollow door solid, and a solid-core door, hung, usually runs to a few hundred dollars. The gaps cost almost nothing, whether you own the door or rent it. Stick a self-adhesive foam seal along the stop, the strip of frame the door closes against, and fit a brush strip along the bottom; the two together are usually under twenty dollars. Then move the strike plate a couple of millimetres, or bend its little tab with pliers, until the latch catches with one firm click and holds the door against its seal. On your way to bed, knock on every door you have and count the hollow ones. That makes ten, and the last piece has no number: it explains the other ten, and you’ll need to turn things over to see it.

Here is how all ten got into your home. Every cheap signal on this list lives where a shopper hardly looks: on the back, inside, along an edge, overhead, in the small print on the box. Every expensive version is one where somebody spent time or material there anyway. Go back to the rug you folded over for its label, and look at the back itself. On a hand-knotted rug the whole pattern shows through, knot by knot, almost as sharp as the front. On a tufted one you’ll find a sheet of cloth glued across the back and no pattern at all. Lift the hem of a curtain. A made-to-measure curtain has a deep hem, often ten centimetres, four inches, folded double, partly for weight, partly so it can be let down. A ready-made one is turned up just far enough to hold. Pull out a drawer and look at its corners: interlocking dovetails, or staples. Run back through the ten: the single white, the tinted ceiling, the bare hinge, the matched stripe, the weighted mount, the veneer, the wool, the warming bulb, the hem, the core of the door. Each is care spent where a buyer glances for a few seconds at most. It happened slowly, without anyone intending it. Shoppers judge fronts in seconds, the money has followed the fronts year after year, and the backs have grown thinner. In a stranger’s sitting room, my grandmother, Mémé Suzanne, would turn over the corner of a curtain before saying a word about the colours. A couturière d’ameublement, a seamstress who sews for rooms rather than for people, reads a room from the back of its cloth. “The front is what they paid for,” she told me once, “and the back is what they got.” So turn it over before you pay, ma chère: the rug, the hem, the drawer, the back of the frame, the underside of the chair. It takes ten seconds. Tonight, turn up the hem of one curtain and measure how deep it is.

So, three laws to carry out of this video. A room’s light agrees with itself: one white in every socket, warming as the evening falls. Nothing stops short of where it should, so the curtain reaches the floor, the pattern crosses the seam, the mount sits heavier at the bottom, and the paint ends before the hinge. And whatever you are about to bring home, you turn it over first, because the back of a thing tells you what the front cost. Take the ten one evening at a time, beginning with the quickest: the bulbs, which take ten minutes and a single box. The ceiling and the door can wait for a free weekend, and the veneer test costs nothing the next time you pass a second-hand shop. If you’ve already done the knocking, here is my question about your doors: how many of them answered hollow? Give it as a fraction, the hollow ones out of all of them. Now stand in your sitting-room doorway with every light on, find the one white that disagrees, and switch it off before you do anything else.
