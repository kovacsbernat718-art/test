Find the last peel-and-stick you put up at home, the tile behind the tap or the film on a drawer, and look at it with the light from the side. There is a bubble mid-sheet and a seam opening onto the old colour, and those faults stop a few cheap rolls from completely transforming a rented home. Add the hook that fell twice and the pale patch where a strip took the paint. French tenants stick up a great deal, it seldom ends like that, and better rolls are not the reason. A French tenancy opens with a signed inventory of every wall and floor, and closes with the landlord walking the rooms holding that same paper. Their difference is ten hacks; with them a kitchen, a bathroom and a window change in a weekend and come back clean. Number six goes on your bathroom window with no glue at all. And number ten decides whether paint leaves with the sticker, and it is not the strength of the glue.

Hack number one is pressure, because the glue on peel-and-stick never dries. The trade calls it pressure-sensitive adhesive. It stays a soft, slow-flowing layer for its whole life. Seen close up, even a smooth cupboard door is a landscape of small hills and hollows. Pat a sticker on with your palm and the adhesive touches only the hilltops. Under firm pressure it begins to creep down into the hollows, and it goes on creeping for roughly three days, which is why a strip holds far better on Thursday than it did on Monday. A cold wall defeats it for the same reason: chilled, the layer stiffens and stops flowing. So work in a warm room, rub every piece down hard from the centre outwards with a plastic card, and push on a hook for a slow count of thirty. Then hang nothing on it for an hour, and nothing heavy until the next day. The pressing is free and adds a minute. Tonight, take the hook that has already fallen once, give it a fresh strip, lean on it, and leave it empty until morning. That is half of the bond. The other half is what you are pressing onto, and in a rented flat that is very often the cheapest paint in the shop.

The second is a test, done before you buy a single roll: rub the wall with a damp white cloth. A sticker is never stuck to your wall. It is stuck to the paint, and it can only hold as well as that paint holds on to the plaster behind it. Rentals are very often rolled in the cheapest flat matte, heavy in chalky filler and light in binder, and a strong adhesive grips that film harder than the film grips the wall. If white or colour comes off on your cloth, a peel-and-stick wallpaper will come down one day with a skin of paint on its back. Fresh paint is a second trap: dry to the touch in hours, it goes on hardening for about a month. The third is texture: a great many French rented flats have walls covered in a woven glass-fibre fabric, painted over, with a grain like coarse canvas, and there a sticker touches only the tips of the weave. So French tenants put their vinyl where it is smooth and hard: tile, glass, cupboard doors. A wall with a slight sheen that leaves the cloth clean has passed. The cloth itself is thirty seconds on one corner of one wall, this evening. If yours fails, the best surface in your home for adhesive is waiting behind the kitchen sink.

That test is a damp cloth and half a minute, and a rented flat is full of checks that small. Reading the lease, the five permissions worth asking a landlord for, curtains without a drill, hanging without nails and what peel-and-stick does to a deposit fill a whole part of my book, Colette's Home Bible, behind the link in the description and the QR code on the screen.

Third come the tiles behind that sink, and the trick is to stick inside the lines, never across them. A glazed tile is a skin of glass fused onto clay, and it cannot shed its surface, which makes it the kindest thing in a rented home for adhesive. The grout between tiles is the opposite, sunken and sandy. Lay one big sheet of thin film across both and within weeks it sinks into every joint, and the grid of the old tiles comes up through your new marble like ribs. In a lot of French kitchens and bathrooms the tiles are plain squares of fifteen or twenty centimetres, six or eight inches, and French hardware shops sell stickers cut to exactly those sizes, one for each tile. The real grout stays visible between them, and real grout is what convinces the eye. Measure one tile, buy squares that size, and a splashback is an evening's work, usually for under thirty dollars. Check the heat figure on the pack, and leave the row behind a gas burner bare. The same squares are sold for floors, ma chère, and there I would stop. Feet, grit and mop water work at every edge. An ugly rented floor is the place to spend real money once, on the largest good rug the room will take, because the rug leaves with you. That is my judgment as a decorator. Before bed, measure a single tile and count how many you would cover. Then look down at the cupboard doors beneath them, where a joiner would stop your hand.

The fourth hack is film on those cupboard doors, and it starts with one question: is the door a single flat slab, or a frame around a panel? A laminate slab is stable, and film lies on it for years. A framed door is built to move. Its centre panel sits loose in a groove so the wood can swell in a damp summer and shrink in a heated winter, and a film stuck across that joint is stretched and wrinkled twice a year. Thierry, my husband, has spent his working life on doors like that, and he showed me on our own bedroom door: every January a hairline of bare wood appears along the edge of the painted panel, and by July it has gone. He would never put film on solid wood at all. I say a rented kitchen in tired laminate is exactly the place for it. Rolls of decorative vinyl are an old staple of le bricolage, the French weekend habit of improving a home with your own hands. First wash the door with washing-up liquid, because every kitchen door wears a coat of cooking grease, and adhesive bonds to the first thing it meets. Wrap the film two centimetres, about an inch, round onto the back, and warm that fold with a hairdryer so the vinyl softens and hugs the edge. Twenty minutes a door, and a roll under twenty dollars covers several. Tonight, wipe one door near the cooker with a white paper towel and a drop of soap, and look at the towel. A flat fridge is where the bubbles begin.

Hack five is a spray bottle, and it is how the bubble never forms. People who fit window film and shop-window lettering for a living very often put vinyl on wet. They mist the surface with water carrying a drop or two of washing-up liquid, and the film floats on that skin of water instead of grabbing at first touch. It can be slid, squared up, lifted and laid again. Then a squeegee, worked from the middle to the edges, drives out the water and the air together, and the adhesive takes hold as the last moisture dries over the next day or two. It works on anything that cannot drink, glass, glazed tile, laminate, the enamelled steel of a fridge, as long as the pack does not say the vinyl must go on dry. Keep water away from bare wood, raw chipboard edges and painted walls, which swell or stain. A spray bottle costs a few dollars, and a fridge door takes about half an hour. For a bubble that is already there, leave the sheet down, prick it with a pin and press the air towards the hole, which is a ten-second job for this evening. You're at the midpoint now, and the spray bottle is also how the film with no glue, promised at the start, goes onto your bathroom window.

Sixth, that window film, which has nothing sticky on it. The pack calls it static cling, and the name misleads, because static electricity has very little to do with it. It is a very soft, very smooth vinyl, and once the water and air are squeezed out from behind it, it holds to the glass much as a suction cup does. Nothing bonds, so nothing is left behind. Lift a corner with a fingernail and the whole sheet comes away in one piece, and goes with you to the next address. In a French city flat the neighbour's window is often a few metres away across a courtyard, and the lower panes have long been screened, once with a little lace half-curtain, now very often with frosted film. Cut yours two millimetres, a sixteenth of an inch, smaller than the glass all round so the edges cannot catch on the seal, spray the pane, lay the film, and squeegee from the centre. A bathroom window is fifteen minutes and usually under twenty dollars. Stay with pale frosted film; a dark or mirrored one on double glazing in full sun is a question for a glazier first. Your job for tonight is a tape measure on the glass itself, width and height. Film gives a window privacy, ma chère. Softness takes cloth, and cloth has weight, which is a different problem for glue.

The seventh hack is the curtain, and the direction it pulls. An adhesive strip is strong when the load slides along the surface and weak when the load pulls away from it. The figure on a pack of hooks, often a kilo or two, a few pounds, is for a weight hanging straight down against a smooth wall. Turn that same strip onto the ceiling, under a stick-on curtain track, and the whole weight pulls it straight off. A French window is usually hinged at the sides and swings into the room, so a light curtain is traditionally fixed to the window itself, on a thin rod across the glass, and it travels with the frame. Two stick-on hooks on that glossy frame carry it easily. My grandmother, Mémé Suzanne, was a furnishing seamstress, and before she chose a fixing for any pair she had sewn, she hung them from a brass spring balance. "The wall carries kilos, not cloth," she said. A voile weighs a few hundred grams, well under a pound. A lined, floor-length pair runs to several kilos, and that belongs on a sprung pole braced between the two sides of the window recess. Hooks and a thin rod are five minutes and under ten dollars. Put one curtain on the kitchen scales tonight. With the window dressed, the wallpaper comes next, and its first strip does not start where you'd think.

Eighth: peel-and-stick wallpaper, and the mistake of lining the first strip up with the corner. Very few corners are truly vertical. A paperhanger's first act in a room is to hang a weight on a string, let it settle, and pencil a line down the wall beside it, a little less than one roll's width from the corner. The first strip follows the pencil. Start from the corner itself and a lean of three millimetres, an eighth of an inch, on the first strip is a visible slope by the fourth. Vinyl also shrinks a fraction as it ages, so strips laid edge to edge can open into hairlines of bare paint, and many makers ask for a small overlap, so read the pack. If your wall failed the cloth test, look inside an old French wardrobe. It is l'armoire, the big freestanding cupboard for clothes and linen, and at a flea market you will quite often find one papered inside. The back of a bookcase, the inside of a wardrobe and the bottoms of drawers are smooth, they belong to you, and they move out when you do. One roll, usually under forty dollars, does a bookcase in an hour. This evening, tape a thread with a key on the end beside a corner, and compare the gap at top and bottom. Paper changes a wall's colour. The next hack changes its shape, and old French ceilings did it the same way.

Hack number nine is the stick-on wall moulding, and it is closer to the real thing than it sounds. The plaster ceiling roses and garlands of old French flats were, for the most part, never carved. They were cast in workshop moulds, in plaster strengthened with fibre, and fixed up in pieces. The raised rectangles on the walls are usually slim lengths of plaster or wood applied to a flat surface, a lighter cousin of boiserie, the carved wooden panelling of grander French rooms. What gives a stick-on version away is colour and proportion. In the real rooms the moulding and the wall are very often painted as one, so on a white rented wall buy plain white lengths with a matte face, never glossy plastic. Keep the frames tall, with one margin repeated everywhere, from the ceiling, the skirting board, the corners and each other; the margin I use is ten centimetres, four inches. The foam lengths weigh almost nothing, so on a wall that passed the cloth test, leave the backing on their own permanent tape and fix them with removable mounting strips every forty centimetres, about sixteen inches. A wall is an afternoon, generally under fifty dollars. Mark one frame on the wall in painter's tape tonight and look at it from the sofa. Everything so far has been about getting things to stay up, ma chère. The last one is about the morning they come down.

The tenth and last is how fast you pull, because the speed of your hand decides what comes off the wall. The adhesive is the same slow liquid from the first hack. Yank it and it has no time to flow, so it behaves like a solid and passes the whole force of your arm to the paint, and the weakest layer tears. Warm it and pull slowly, and it stretches, thins and lets go of the wall first. So hold a hairdryer a hand's width away for half a minute. Then fold the film back on itself and draw it along the wall, low and flat. A hook with a stretch tab is designed for the same motion: pull the tab straight down, parallel to the wall, slowly, as far as it will stretch, and never towards you. My father, Jean-Marc, repainted flats between tenants, and the first tool out of his van was an old hairdryer; he went round every room warming what the last people had stuck up before he touched a brush. "Warm and slow," he said, "or you are buying plaster." Stickiness left on tile or glass softens under a drop of cooking oil. The word removable describes the glue on the smooth, sound, fully hardened surface it was designed for, and the small print lists the exceptions: often matte paint, fresh paint, texture. A product that comes down at every move is also bought again at every move. That is habit and repeat custom, with no scheme behind it. A French tenant has that signed inventory from the first day. Make your own: photograph every wall the day you get the keys, before the first sticker, and keep one offcut and the pack. Tonight, warm a single price label with the hairdryer and peel it as slowly as you can bear.

Underneath all ten sit three laws. Press it and wait, because the glue is a liquid that needs time. Judge the surface before the sticker: smooth, sound, and yours wherever possible. And take it down warm and slow, the way a painter would. Ten is too many for one Saturday, so pick two. The window film and the tile squares are safe in almost any flat, whatever the walls are painted with. The quickest of all is the second hack, the damp cloth, at thirty seconds, and it tells you which of the others your walls will allow. Write me one word when you have done it: nothing, colour, or white. The cloth is under your sink, and the wall is the one you have wanted to change since the day you moved in.
