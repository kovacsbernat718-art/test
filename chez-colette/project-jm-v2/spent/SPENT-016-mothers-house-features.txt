Open your fridge: the bread and tomatoes on its shelves are the first of eight problems that French homes solved with simply genius features. My mother’s flat in Lyon had all eight, and most can be copied in yours. Number seven is the grey grid of grout across your kitchen floor. And number eight is the crack along your ceiling line that keeps coming back.

The first feature is the food cupboard older French kitchens relied on before fridges: mesh-sided for air, and in some old Paris flats built into the north wall with an opening to the outside. My mother’s sat under the kitchen window. It existed because some food wants cool, not cold, and bread shows it best: it goes stale faster in a fridge than on the counter. Staling isn’t drying out; the starch in the crumb packs itself back into stiff crystals, fastest at fridge temperature and barely at all in a freezer. Tomatoes kept below about twelve degrees Celsius, fifty-four Fahrenheit, lose much of what makes them taste of tomato, and a fridge runs at around four, under forty. A hard loaf isn’t lost: ten minutes in a hot oven melts those crystals and softens it for that meal. So give one cupboard that old job, away from the oven and the fridge’s warm back, for bread, tomatoes and onions, and slice and freeze whatever bread you won’t finish in two days; five minutes, and free. Tonight, take the bread and the tomatoes out of the fridge. A French door that scrapes the floor is cured with a brass ring smaller than a coin. Yours has its own version.

Second comes the hinge French interior doors usually hang on, every door in my mother’s flat included: open the door wide, lift, and it slides straight off its pins without a tool. So when one has dropped and scrapes, it comes off in seconds, a thin brass ring goes over each pin, and it hangs back a couple of millimetres higher, under a tenth of an inch. Doors drop because their whole weight hangs from one edge, so year by year the top hinge pulls away from the frame and the far corner sinks. Close any door and look at the gap above it: wider at the hinge corner than at the handle corner means it has dropped. Your own hinges probably have pins you tap out from below, and the trade’s cure for those is one long screw. On the top hinge, take out the frame screw nearest the strip the door closes against and drive in one about three inches long, seventy-five millimetres, so it bites into the wall framing behind; as it tightens, it draws the door back up toward the hinge side, five minutes’ work for a screw that costs pennies. When new tiles raised our kitchen floor by a few millimetres, the door stopped clearing it. My husband, Thierry, lifted it off one-handed, dropped rings over the pins from a jam jar he keeps by his bench, pure système D, the French art of improvising a repair from odds and ends, and had it swinging again within a minute. Before bed, find the door with the widest gap at its hinge corner. Meanwhile, whatever you keep in the garage or the attic is being frozen every winter and baked every summer.

A ring from a jam jar or one long screw put that door right, and Colette’s Home Bible treats the bigger decisions the same way: the kitchen as a real room, ceilings too low or too tall, and the room with five doors. Feature three answers that, and it lies under many French apartment buildings: la cave, a small locked storeroom in the basement, one for each flat, my mother’s among them. A few metres underground the temperature barely shifts between January and July, because the earth around it evens out the seasons, while an attic or a garage swings hard the other way, which makes them the worst places in a home for a good deal of what gets stored there. Water-based paint that freezes can turn lumpy and never mix smooth again. Candles slump under a roof in August, and spare batteries drain faster in that heat. Photographs and papers age fastest when heat and damp keep changing, which is why archivists steer people away from attics and garages. A French household sends the wine down, because wine wants a steady temperature more than a perfect one, along with jars, apples and bicycles. A cellar is often damp, though, so the books, the photographs and the winter coats stay upstairs. Sort your garage and attic into what shrugs off weather, the tools, the bikes, the glass, and what needs calm, the paint, candles, photos and papers, and move the second pile to the lowest cupboard on an inside wall; an afternoon, at no cost. A min-max thermometer, a few dollars, left out there for a week shows you the swing in numbers. This evening, carry the paint tins in from the garage. Arms full of tins, try opening the next door you reach with only your elbow.

If that door wouldn’t budge, the fourth feature explains it: French interior doors very rarely have round knobs, and my mother’s flat had not one. They open with levers, and American accessibility rules for public buildings don’t accept hardware that needs a tight grip and a twist at once, which is exactly what a knob demands. A lever gives way to an elbow, a wrist or a hip, and because it turns the same latch from several centimetres further out, it needs far less force, the reason a longer wrench loosens a stuck bolt. Think of the bathroom door with wet, soapy hands, or the kitchen door with a hot dish in both of them. Before you buy, measure the backset, from the edge of the door to the centre of the knob: in America it’s usually two and three-eighths inches, about sixty millimetres, or two and three-quarters, about seventy, and many new latches adjust to both. Check whether the lever is reversible, too, because some are made for a door that swings one way only, and buy a privacy set, the kind with a push-button lock, for the bathroom, and plain passage sets everywhere else. Swap the bathroom and kitchen knobs first; a basic lever set usually costs under twenty-five dollars and goes on with a screwdriver in about ten minutes. In a rental, keep the old knobs and their screws in a labelled envelope in a kitchen drawer, ready for moving day. After supper, count the knobs in your home, starting with the bathroom. Somewhere near one of those doors is a thermostat, reading one temperature for a room that never has just one.

Height is the fifth feature, and that thermostat can’t see it: warm air is lighter, so in a heated room it gathers under the ceiling, often a few degrees warmer than the air around your ankles. The taller the room, the wider the gap. Many old flats in Lyon’s weavers’ quarter have a wooden mezzanine built into their height, a sleeping platform up in the warmest air of the flat. French property listings still treat hauteur sous plafond, the distance from floor to ceiling, as a selling point in its own right. In my mother’s flat on the Croix-Rousse, my bed was up on that mezzanine, an arm’s length below the ceiling. On winter evenings I did my homework up there in a T-shirt, ma chère, while my parents sat below in sweaters. The same warm layer is why the tops of kitchen cupboards are the worst spot for oil, wine or chocolate, and why a loft bed in a tall room sleeps warm. If you have a ceiling fan, set it turning clockwise at its slowest speed in winter: it draws air up the middle of the room and pushes the warm layer down along the walls without a draught, and it’s usually one small switch on the motor housing and thirty seconds on a stepladder. Tonight, put one thermometer on the floor and one on a wardrobe. That’s past the middle, with the floor and the crack still ahead, and the mattress you’ll sleep on tonight may be damp underneath.

Number six sat under every mattress in my mother’s flat, as it does under a great many in France: a base of wooden slats with gaps between them, so the mattress breathes from below. Through the night a sleeper’s warmth and damp travel down into the mattress, and on a solid board or a bare floor the damp reaches something colder and stays put. It’s what fogs a cold glass: warm, damp air touching something cooler leaves its water behind. Lift one corner and feel the underside: cool and faintly clammy, or a ring of stains on the board, means the water is staying. Dark spots of mould mean that mattress is finished, and if the floor or wall beneath is damp as well, have someone find where the water comes from. Many mattress makers write a maximum gap between slats into their warranty, often about three inches, seven or eight centimetres, because foam sags into anything wider. French slatted bases often add sliding clips on the middle slats, under the hips, to firm up that one zone. On a solid platform, lay a breathable spacer mat under the mattress, the kind sold for boat bunks, or drill a grid of holes through the board; either is an hour’s work, and the mat is usually under fifty dollars. This is one place I would spend, ma chère: you lie on a mattress for roughly a third of your life, and a cheap base can ruin a good one from underneath. While the kettle heats, slide a hand under the foot of your mattress. Now glance at your kitchen floor, at the grey grid between the tiles that never quite comes clean.

Seventh, as promised, the floor: the six-sided terracotta tiles underfoot in a great many old French kitchens, my mother’s included, and their shape is the genius. Of every shape that covers a floor with no gaps, the hexagon wraps a given area in the least edge; it is the same reason bees build in hexagons. Laid in tiles of equal size, hexagons leave roughly seven per cent less joint than squares, and the joint is where the dirt goes. Grout is porous, so a mop pushes grey water into it and every pass leaves a little behind, until your kitchen floor wears a grid you can’t scrub out. Those old French tiles were fed with linseed oil and then wax, so a spill sat on the surface long enough to wipe. For your own floor, scrub the grout once with a stiff brush and a paste of baking soda, then brush a penetrating grout sealer along every line; an afternoon for a kitchen, and a bottle of sealer is cheap. If you ever retile, ask for larger tiles or hexagons, and a grout colour close to the dirt it will meet. Tonight, rub one grout line by the cooker with a wet paper towel. Now look up to where your wall meets the ceiling, at the hairline crack you’ve already filled once.

Last, the eighth: the plaster cornice running round the top of so many older French rooms, which besides being ornament hides that crack. The line where wall meets ceiling is the one joint in a room certain to move, so filler pressed into it splits again, usually in the same place, by the next winter. Wall and ceiling are built differently and warm and cool at different rates, and in a lot of newer houses with roof trusses, the ceiling lifts slightly every winter and settles back in summer. Rigid filler can’t stretch. Plasterers expect that line to open, and a cornice bridges it, so the movement happens out of sight behind the moulding. My mother’s flat on the Croix-Rousse had all eight of these, the cornice included, and the first time my father, Jean-Marc, painted it, he ran the flat of his hand slowly along that moulding. “Filler is for holes,” he told me. “This line moves.” In a room without a cornice, the answer is something flexible: a painter’s caulk, the acrylic kind marked paintable, run along the joint, smoothed with a damp sponge and painted the next day; a tube costs a few dollars and a room takes about half an hour. One exception: a crack running at a slant from the corner of a door or window, one wider than a few millimetres, about an eighth of an inch, or one that keeps growing is something else, and it belongs to a structural engineer before any caulk. All eight share one habit: each expects something that will happen whatever you do, bread staling, a door sinking, seasons swinging, hands full, heat rising, a sleeper’s damp, dirt finding every joint, a ceiling line moving, and gives it somewhere harmless to go instead of fighting it, ma chère. Tonight, find the stretch of that crack that has opened more than once.

Three laws to keep from my mother’s flat. Expect what will happen anyway: bread stales, doors sink, seasons swing, ceilings shift. Give it somewhere harmless to go, a cool cupboard, a cellar, a slatted base, a cornice. And build so your own hands can adjust it, with a ring on a pin, a screw or a slider on a slat. Two minutes at the fridge is tonight’s whole task; the door gaps and the grout take ten more, and the hinge screw, the levers and the caulk can be spread over the coming weeks. Below this video, give me two numbers: how many of your inside doors have levers, and how many have knobs. The fridge is closest, so start there, with the loaf on its middle shelf.
