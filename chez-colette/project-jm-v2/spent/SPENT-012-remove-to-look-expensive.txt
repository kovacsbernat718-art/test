Pick up a throw cushion from your sofa and look along its seam: a white tag hanging out of it is one of eleven things to remove from your home to make it look expensive. The lampshade beside it may still wear the shop’s plastic, the hardbacks on the shelf their glossy wrappers, and the table a cloudy ring from a cold glass. A room rarely looks cheap because of what it lacks. It looks cheap because of what was left in place: a wrapper, a film, a cover, a job stopped just short of done. There are eleven of them, nearly all free to take out, and once they’re gone, what you own starts to look like what it cost. Number five is that white ring, and a hairdryer may lift it tonight. And number eleven is lying on the best wood you own, put there to protect it, quietly drawing its own outline.

The first thing to go is that tag, with everything else your furniture wore on its way to you. Read it. On most, in capital letters, it says that under penalty of law it must not be removed, then, on the same line, three more words: except by the consumer. The warning is aimed at the shop; the tag tells a buyer what the filling is before they pay, and once you’ve paid, it’s yours to cut. The plastic on the lampshade is the same kind of thing, a film made to survive a truck and a warehouse; in a room it crinkles, throws a hard glint back at every lamp and holds a line of dust in each fold. So is the film still on the television’s frame. A room looks expensive partly because nothing in it announces where it came from, or how recently. Photograph each tag first, since the filling it lists matters the day an upholsterer re-covers the piece, then snip close to the seam rather than pulling. Leave the one on the mattress, though: some makers ask to see it for a warranty claim. A whole sitting room takes ten minutes. Before bed, cut every tag in that room. And while the scissors are out, walk to your bookshelf, where most hardbacks are still dressed in something once meant to be thrown away.

One snip is the entire price of that fix, and plenty of others cost about as little. Colette’s Home Bible holds many more, among them why linen reads expensive, how wood’s grain and finish age, what patina really is, and ten things worth buying instead. It’s linked in the description below, and the QR code on the screen opens it too.

Second, take the jackets off your hardbacks, because the dust jacket started life as packaging. In the nineteenth century it was often a plain paper wrapper that kept the cloth clean in the bookshop, and plenty of buyers threw it away at home. Later, publishers printed it with pictures and praise and turned it into an advertisement: glossy, loud, a different colour on every spine. Underneath, most hardbacks are bound in cloth or paper over board, the title stamped down the spine, and a shelf of those reads like a library, ma chère, not a shop window. For a long time French books were sold in plain paper covers, and families who could afford it had the ones they loved bound. At la brocante, the weekend market of old and second-hand things that fills a French square, those bindings still turn up by the boxful, faded to soft browns and greens. One exception: on a collectable first edition the jacket can carry much of the value, so those stay dressed. File the rest flat in a drawer, and keep the shelf out of direct sun, because bare cloth fades. A full shelf is twenty minutes, and free. This evening, undress five books on the shelf you see most and step back. Then look past the shelf to the nearest window, at the fine grey grid between you and the street.

Number three is that grey grid, and it comes out of every window you won’t open before spring. An insect screen lays a mesh across the glass that dims the daylight and hazes the view, which is the largest and most expensive picture any room owns. Photographers who shoot houses for sale often lift the screens out before they start. A lot of French windows have no screen at all, and the view comes through clean. When you do need one, the colour of the mesh matters more than anyone expects: charcoal or black is far easier to see through than silver or light grey, because a pale strand catches the daylight and glows, while a dark one vanishes against what lies behind it. So take out the screens on every window you keep shut this season; most come free with two small tabs or a pair of springs. Pencil on each frame which window it belongs to, and store them flat. When one needs new mesh, choose charcoal: a roll and the rubber spline that holds it come to under twenty dollars, and a frame is about half an hour. Tonight, take out one screen, and in the morning light compare that window with its neighbour. Then turn round, because that cleaner daylight is about to cross your dining table and show you a shine that isn’t the wood.

The fourth discovery is that shine: on most tables sprayed every week, it’s polish, not wood. Many aerosol furniture sprays leave an oil on the surface, and some contain silicone, which never soaks in and never dries. Over the years the layer builds into a greasy gleam that smears when you wipe it and clouds when low sun crosses it, while well-kept old furniture has a dry, soft sheen instead. Silicone has a second cost, hidden until a piece needs restoring. When a sprayed table reaches my husband Thierry’s workshop, the first coat of new finish pulls back from the wood in little round craters, a fault the trade calls fish-eye. He’d dust our own table with a dry cloth and nothing else; I give it a thin coat of paste wax once a year, and we still argue about it. To lift the build-up, dampen a soft cloth with mineral spirits, sold as white spirit in Europe, test it underneath first, and wipe with the grain a section at a time, with a window open. Most cured finishes aren’t harmed by it, and the old polish comes away grey on the cloth. A table takes half an hour, and the can is cheap. Tonight, drag a fingertip across the top and see whether it leaves a stripe. While you’re bent over it, look for a pale, milky ring where a glass once stood.

Fifth, as promised, the white ring, and it isn’t in the wood at all. That milky circle is moisture trapped in the clear finish, usually left by a cold glass or a hot mug one evening; countless tiny pockets of water scatter the light, so the ring shows white while the wood beneath is untouched. Old pieces finished in shellac, the resin behind the deep, glassy French polish of antique furniture, cloud like this especially easily. Because the culprit is water, gentle heat can let it out. Set a hairdryer to low, hold it a hand’s width away and keep it moving over the ring for a minute, then let the surface cool and look again. Repeat, and often the cloud shrinks in from its edges and goes. If it holds on, lay a folded cotton tea towel over it and press a dry iron, at its lowest setting with no steam, for a few seconds at a time, lifting to check. Stop at once if the finish turns soft or tacky. A dark ring is another matter: black or grey means water went through the finish into the wood, and that belongs to a furniture restorer. Trying costs nothing, and each round takes a couple of minutes. Before you sleep, try it on the smallest ring you can find. Once the table’s clear, sit back on the sofa and feel the little grey balls on the cushion under your hand.

Lesson six: shave off the pills, and learn which fabrics keep them. Those bobbles on the sofa arm and the throw form when loose fibre ends work out of the yarn and tangle under friction. On wool or cotton the fibres are fairly weak, so many pills break away in the wash or against a sleeve. Polyester is far stronger, and in a blend it holds each pill to the surface like a tether, so the bobbles stay and multiply until the seat used most looks like the oldest thing in the room. So a label reading mostly polyester blended with a softer fibre, on anything you’ll sit on daily, deserves a second look. A battery fabric shaver, one of the cheapest gadgets in the hardware aisle, does the job: hold the fabric flat and move in light circles. Test a hidden corner first, and keep the shaver away from bouclé and any loose, loopy weave, which it will cut. A cushion takes ten minutes, a whole sofa an evening. You’re past the middle of the list now: the white ring is behind you, and the thing on your best wood is five items away. After dinner, shave one cushion and set it beside one you haven’t touched. Then carry the fluff to the bathroom bin, and look at the white line round the edge of the bath.

Then the seventh, that line: the old sealant round the bath, the basin and the kitchen sink. New, it’s a crisp white bead; with age it yellows, greys at the edges and lifts at the corners, and that tired line says old rental louder than any tile. Most people learn the next part after wasting a tube: new silicone won’t stick to old silicone. Run a fresh bead over the top and it looks perfect for a month, then peels away in one strip, because cured silicone repels almost everything, including more of itself. Paint won’t hold to it either. My father, Jean-Marc, ran his thumbnail along every joint in a kitchen before he opened a tin, and if the bead stretched like rubber, that joint stayed unpainted. So the old bead comes out completely: slice along both edges with a utility knife, pull it free, rub off the last film with rubbing alcohol, and let it dry overnight. Next day, tape both sides, lay a thin bead, smooth it with a wet fingertip, and pull the tape before the surface skins; then keep water off it for a full day. Tube and tape cost a few dollars each, and the job is one evening plus drying time. If dark spots creep back within a few months, moisture is staying in that room, and a professional should find out why. Right now, press a thumbnail along the bead and find where it’s lifting. On your way out, glance at the frame in the hall that never quite hangs straight.

A mistake hides behind almost every frame, and it’s the eighth thing to remove: one wire on one hook. A wire hanging from a single point is a hinge. Every slammed door, every pass of a duster and every heavy step on a wooden floor nudges the frame, and with nothing to stop it, it swings until the picture sits crooked; you straighten it on Sunday and it leans again by Wednesday. Galleries and museums generally hang from two points, one fixing on each side of the frame onto two hooks at exactly the same height, and a picture held twice has nothing to pivot on. So take the wire off. Screw a D-ring into each side, about a third of the way down, and set two hooks the same distance apart, levelled with a spirit level or your phone. A small clear bumper under each bottom corner keeps it square to the wall. If you rent, that’s one extra small hole, and filler hides both. The hardware is pocket change, and a frame takes a quarter of an hour. With the evening still young, rehang whichever frame tilts most. Then sit down on the leather sofa, if you have one, and run a hand along its arm, because part of it may not be leather at all.

Ninth, something to remove from your shopping list as much as your sitting room: bonded leather. It’s shredded leather scraps mixed with a binder and spread on a fabric backing, then given a polyurethane skin pressed with a printed grain. After a few years of sitting, that skin cracks and flakes in brittle patches, and a flaking sofa looks cheaper than an honest fabric one ever could. A second label phrase is worth knowing: leather match. It means real leather where your body touches, on the seat and the fronts of the arms, and colour-matched vinyl on the sides and back. New, you can’t tell. Ten years on, the leather has softened and deepened where you sit while the vinyl hasn’t changed, so one sofa ages in two directions. This is where paying more is the right call, ma chère. If you want leather, buy it on every surface, or buy fabric on a solid frame an upholsterer can re-cover in fifteen years; a seat nobody can repair turns out to be the expensive one, whatever it cost. The cheapest route to good leather is second-hand, and chiner, the French verb for rummaging through second-hand stalls until something good turns up, was made for an old leather armchair already softened in the right places. If yours is already flaking, a fitted stretch cover goes on in ten minutes for a fraction of a new sofa. Tonight, check the label of anything in your home that looks like leather for the word bonded, or the word match. Then look at the wall above it, at the square of tester paint you tried once and never painted over.

The tenth habit is removing the unfinished, and builders have a ritual for it. At the end of a good job, the contractor walks every room with the client and a roll of blue painter’s tape, sticking a small piece on every flaw: a paint drip, a gap above a skirting board, a door that won’t latch, a missing cover plate. That’s the punch list, and it often separates a house that looks builder-grade from one that looks expensive, because the materials can be identical and only the finishing differs. Your home keeps its own: that tester square, a cupboard missing one handle, a dead bulb in a three-bulb fitting, nail holes from frames long gone. Each tells the eye the work stopped. Paint straight over a tester square and its outline usually shows through as a faint ridge, so sand the edges flat first. Touch-ups can show too, as a shinier or duller patch even from the same tin, because sheen changes with how paint goes on; painters touch up with the same kind of roller, or repaint the wall corner to corner. Most of these jobs take under ten minutes and next to nothing in materials, so do two a week. Before the lights go out, walk one room with a roll of tape and mark everything that isn’t finished. On your way back, stop at the best piece of wood in the house, and look at whatever is lying on top of it.

And the eleventh is the protection on that wood: the lace runner on the sideboard, the clear vinyl sheet on the dining table, the glass laid over a desk, and the cloth under the lamp on la commode, which is French for a chest of drawers and comes from the word for convenient. Each went there to keep the wood safe, and each is slowly writing on it. Wood changes colour in daylight: cherry darkens towards a deep red-brown, walnut lightens, oak and pine turn more amber. Under a cloth that lay in one place for years, that light never arrived, so lift it and you’ll often find its exact outline, paler or darker than the wood around it. Vinyl leaves a second kind of mark: the softeners that keep it supple can migrate into some finishes and soften them, until a mat left for months prints its texture into the varnish. And long before any of that, covered wood stops reading as wood. Under glass, a table becomes a picture of a table; under a runner, a sideboard becomes a shelf. At my grandmother’s walnut table the cloth came out only for meals; afterwards Mémé Suzanne folded it away and ran the flat of her hand across the bare top, the way she smoothed a finished chair cover in search of a wrinkle. It stands in my kitchen now, ma chère, with every dent and knife mark, and I wouldn’t sand away one of them. So lift everything off. If a ghost appears underneath, leave the surface open; with many woods the difference softens as the light catches up, over weeks or months. A mat goes under a hot dish and a coaster under a glass, only while they’re needed. It’s five minutes’ work, and free. Tonight, lift the oldest cloth in your home and see what the light has been doing underneath it.

Here is a test you can run on any room, one object at a time, in three questions. Was this put here for a journey, a shop or a season that’s already over? Does it stand between your eye and the real material, as a film, a wrapper, a mesh, a skin or a cloth? And is it finished, or did the work stop just short? Of the eleven, the tags ask least of you, ten minutes and a pair of scissors; the sealant, the screens and the punch list are jobs for a slower weekend. In the comments, answer me in one word: under the oldest cloth you lifted, was the wood paler or darker? The scissors are in a drawer somewhere near you, so fetch them, and pick up the first cushion.
