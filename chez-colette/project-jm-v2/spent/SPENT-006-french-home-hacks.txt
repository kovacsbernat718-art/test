You've stuck the vinyl tiles over the backsplash, bought the battery candles and changed the cushion covers, and your home still does not look elevated. It looks like the same rooms with more in them. That was a weekend and a couple of hundred dollars, and one corner of the contact paper is already lifting. An ordinary French flat rarely has this problem, and the reason is not money or plaster mouldings. A rented flat in France is often handed over with a bare bulb hanging from every ceiling, so a tenant's first job is to deal with what the place already has, before buying a single ornament. The difference comes down to ten easy hacks. Most need a screwdriver, a tape measure or your two hands. Number six is for the curtains in your sitting room right now, and it takes three strips of cloth and three days. And number ten begins with the most copied French colour in the world, and what restorers found underneath it.

The first hack is the bulb, and a small number printed on its box. It is the colour rendering index, C R I, which scores how truthfully a bulb shows colour, with daylight at the top of the scale at one hundred. A great many ordinary LED bulbs score about eighty, and what they are short of is deep red. Red is in more of your home than you'd think: in oak, in leather, in terracotta, in a tomato, in the skin of everyone at your table. Under a bulb weak in red, all of it turns faintly grey, and grey wood and grey faces make a room look cheaper than it is. The French tenant with the bare wire has one advantage: the bulb is the first thing they are forced to choose. Look for ninety or above in the small print, written as C R I or as the letters R A. It adds a dollar or two per bulb, and changing one takes a minute. Start with the lamp you sit beside and the light over your table. Tomorrow morning, hold the back of your hand under the kitchen light, then walk it to the window, and watch the colour come back into your skin. Once the light tells the truth, your cupboard doors are about to start bothering you.

Hack number two is already inside those cupboards: the small screws on every hinge. If you open a door and see a metal arm and a round cup sunk into its back, that hinge was built to be adjusted. One screw slides the door left and right, another moves it in and out, and the plate on the cabinet lets it travel up and down. They exist because hinges settle with years of use. The eye is remarkably good at comparing two lines. It catches a gap one millimetre wider at the top than at the bottom, so doors with wandering gaps look like a cheap kitchen, and the same doors with one even gap, about three millimetres, an eighth of an inch, look fitted. My husband, Thierry, hangs doors for a living, and when I wanted to repaint our kitchen he took a screwdriver from his pocket and spent ten minutes on the hinges first. "You don't paint a crooked door," he said. I did not repaint. Give a screw a quarter turn, close the door and look; a whole kitchen is ten minutes with one cross-head screwdriver. If a screw spins without gripping, the board behind it has given way, and that is a repair for a joiner. Tonight, straighten the single worst door. Once those lines run true, your eye goes hunting for the next crooked thing, and it is on the wall above your sofa.

That kitchen cost nothing but a screwdriver, and there are a great many corrections just as small. The ten-minute room audit, the hanging heights, the rug sizes room by room and the list of what not to buy are all in my book, Colette's Home Bible. The third hack is on that wall above the sofa, and it lowers almost every picture you own. Museums and galleries hang to a single line: the centre of the picture sits about one hundred and forty-five centimetres from the floor, fifty-seven inches, roughly the eye height of a standing adult. At home, pictures drift upward, for a mechanical reason. You hang them standing, arms raised, and the nail lands where the arm is comfortable. Then you look at them from a chair. A picture hung too high stops belonging to the furniture under it, and the bare gap beneath the frame becomes the thing you notice. Over a sofa or a chest of drawers a second measurement takes over: the bottom of the frame sits fifteen to twenty centimetres, six to eight inches, above the furniture, so the two count as one group. In some older French flats the answer is la cimaise, a slim rail below the ceiling that pictures hang from on cords or thin rods, so a frame can travel up or down without one new hole. A modern metal one screws in once, an easier thing to ask a landlord for than twenty nails. Rehanging a picture takes ten minutes, and filler for the old hole costs a few dollars. Tonight, measure from the floor to the middle of the picture you look at most. Then sit down beneath it, ma chère, and look at the lamp at your elbow, which is making the room darker.

Hack number four: if you can see the bulb, the lamp is at the wrong height. From your usual seat, look at the nearest lamp. If a slice of bare bulb shows under the rim of the shade, your eyes are adjusting to it. The pupil sets itself by the brightest thing in view, much as it does for oncoming headlights, and once it has closed down for that bulb, everything else in the room looks darker than it is. So the brightest lamp in the house can be the reason the corners look gloomy. A shade is there to hide a tiny, fierce source and replace it with a large, gentle one, and a large source is what gives soft shadows on a face. The working rule in the trade is that the lower rim of the shade sits level with your eyes when you are seated. The correction is usually two or three books, slipped under the base or taken away, or a move to a lower table: two minutes, and free. Tonight, sit in every seat you use and count the bulbs you can see. Then reach over to switch that lamp off, and pay attention to the next thing your hand closes on.

Fifth, the handles on your drawers, whose screw holes turn out to be standard. A handle is held by two screws from behind, and the distance between them follows a few fixed sizes: three inches, ninety-six millimetres, which is about three and three-quarter inches, and a hundred and twenty-eight millimetres, about five. So a new handle in the same size drops straight into the old holes. That matters because the handle is the one part of a cupboard you actually touch, and the hand weighs things without being asked. An inexpensive handle is usually a zinc casting under a thin plating that rubs through to grey at the edges. Solid brass and iron have real weight in the hand, and like wood and porcelain, they wear in where plating wears out. The tall windows of an older French flat lock with long iron rods worked by one oval knob that fills the palm, and that knob is often as old as the building. Take one handle off, measure from the centre of one hole to the centre of the other, and buy to that figure. Solid ones start at a few dollars apiece. If you rent, bag the originals and screw them back the day you leave. Tonight, remove a single handle and feel what it weighs in your open palm. You are halfway through the ten now, and the three strips of cloth from the start are waiting at your window.

For the sixth, your curtains need training, and it takes three days. A curtain fresh from its packet flares out at the hem like a skirt, because cloth keeps the last shape it was held in, and until now that shape was a flat fold in plastic. In a curtain workroom the maker gathers the curtain into even folds by hand, the way you would close a paper fan, and ties it loosely in three places. A few days later the fibres have set, the ties come off, and the curtain drops in straight columns each time it is drawn back. Mémé Suzanne, my grandmother, sewed curtains to order in Lyon, and no pair of hers was left loose on the day she hung it. She tied each one with strips torn from the lining offcuts. "Not before Thursday," she told the family. That is le système D, the French name for solving a problem with whatever is already lying on the worktable. Folds need cloth to form in, so the two curtains together should measure about twice the width of the pole. Ready-made pairs are often far scanter, which is why they hang like a bedsheet, and a second pair of the same inexpensive curtain cures it. The tying is ten minutes with three ribbons or bootlaces. Tonight, fold one curtain into pleats the width of your hand and tie it. While it sets, ma chère, turn round to the sofa, where something is flatter than it should be.

Seventh comes the cushion, and the discovery is that the pad should be bigger than its cover. Upholsterers fill a cover with a pad about five centimetres, two inches, larger than the cover itself, so a forty-five centimetre cover, eighteen inches, takes a fifty centimetre pad, twenty inches. Squeezed in, the filling is pushed out into the corners and the cushion stands up square. Many cushions sold complete come with a pad the same size as the cover, in polyester fibre. That is why the corners hang empty like a dog's ears and the middle goes flat: polyester fibre mats down where you lean on it and never fully shakes back. In a feather pad, each feather has a springy spine, and one good shake spreads them out again. So keep your covers and change what is in them. Unzip one, read the label on the pad and measure both. Feather pads one size up are sold separately, generally for under fifteen dollars each. If feathers do not suit your household, a pad one size up in any filling still fills the corners. Tonight, try the free version: push the fattest pad you own into your smallest cover. From that sofa, look across at the kitchen counter and count the words you can read.

Hack number eight removes the words from your rooms. A fluent reader cannot look at a word and decline to read it. The psychologist John Ridley Stroop built a famous test on this in the nineteen thirties: show people the word red printed in blue ink, ask for the colour of the ink, and they stumble, because the reading has already happened. Now count the print on your kitchen counter and round your bath. The washing-up liquid, the hand soap, the cereal box, four bottles on the edge of the tub. A label is designed to win attention on a shelf crowded with rivals, and it goes on doing that work in your home, all day. It is one reason a hotel bathroom feels calmer than yours with the same four products in it: there, the bottles are plain and alike. The fastest fix is to turn every label to the wall. A better one is to pour the two soaps you use daily into plain pump bottles, which cost a few dollars. Tonight, stand in your kitchen doorway and read aloud every word you can see without moving. My own mark as a decorator is ten; below that, a room goes quiet. A counter with no print left on it has space for one object that deserves looking at, and in a lot of French homes that object did not come from a shop.

Number nine: one old thing in each room, chosen by its wear. On Sunday mornings, towns all over France close a street or a car park for a second-hand sale, and the verb for walking it is chiner, to hunt slowly through other people's old things. The best buy on those tables, ma chère, is usually an empty picture frame, and it can be read. Traditional gilding is real gold leaf laid over a layer of fine clay, usually red. Where generations of dusting have rubbed the high points, the gold thins and the red glows through. Gold paint is bronze powder in varnish, which with age goes brown and dull all over. So tilt the frame to the light and look at the ridges. Red on the high points usually means leaf. A flat brownish gold means paint. An old frame lifts a room because its wear is logical. It is rubbed where hands and cloths went and nowhere else, and the eye trusts that in a way it never trusts a factory finish distressed evenly from corner to corner. Frames like this turn up at flea markets, charity shops and yard sales for a few dollars to perhaps thirty. Tonight, count the objects in your sitting room that are older than you are. And an old gilt frame asks for a wall worth hanging on, which brings in paint, and that grey.

And the tenth hack is how to choose a paint colour, beginning with a mistake. The grey known as Trianon grey, or simply French grey, is tied to the Petit Trianon at Versailles. When restorers went down through the layers, the famous grey proved to be a nineteenth-century repaint. The original underneath was a pale green. So the most imitated French colour there is was never the one its first owners chose. And that grey will not hold still: in a dim room it looks like cement, and in strong sun it warms to the colour of barley. No colour is fixed, ma chère. A chip two centimetres wide, under an inch, seen under shop lighting, shows you almost nothing. And four painted walls light one another, each throwing its colour onto the rest, so a room always comes out stronger than the card. Jean-Marc, my father, mixed his own colours for the walls he painted, and before any job he brushed two coats onto a sheet of lining paper as wide as an open newspaper, pinned it to the client's wall and left the tin shut for two days. "The tin doesn't decide," he said. "The window does." A tester pot is a few dollars and the sheet takes twenty minutes. Move it from the wall beside the window to the darkest corner, and look at breakfast, at midday and with your lamps on. If your lease forbids paint, this hack is not yours, and no sticker stands in for it. In a rented room colour arrives in the largest piece of cloth you own, the curtains or the bedcover, and it is tested the same way, with a sample carried home before you pay. And where you do paint, buy the better tin: cheap paint generally carries less pigment and needs a third coat, so the saving is gone before the wall is dry. Tonight, tape one sheet of white paper to the wall you like least and watch what colour it turns by bedtime.

Ten hacks, and behind them three questions you can put to any room before you spend a cent on it. What is the light doing to this? Does it line up, with the eye, with the floor, with its neighbour? And what does the hand meet when it reaches out? A bulb, a shade and a sheet of painted paper answer the first. A picture, a hinge and a tied curtain answer the second. A handle and a cushion answer the third. Nobody needs all ten by Sunday. The quickest is number eight, the labels, at ten seconds a bottle, and the hinges are ten minutes after that. Leave me one number below: the C R I figure printed on the bulb you sit beside, or the word none if the box does not say. Now go to the kitchen, turn the first bottle to face the wall, and open the cupboard door beside it.
