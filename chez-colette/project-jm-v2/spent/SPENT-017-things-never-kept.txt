Nine things in your home right now are the reason it never stays clean for long, however hard you scrub. Several were bought to make cleaning easier, and plenty of French women past forty simply don’t keep them. Number seven is sitting in your toilet tank. And number nine is the white dust that’s back on your shelves by morning.

The first is the sheet of foil on the floor of your oven, and it can ruin the very surface it was laid there to protect. In a lot of ovens the lower element is hidden just under that floor, and foil resting on it can melt into the enamel for good, which is why oven makers warn against it in their manuals. Plenty of French kitchens have an oven that cleans itself with heat alone, running empty at around five hundred degrees Celsius, over nine hundred Fahrenheit, until every spill is a pale ash you wipe out with a damp cloth; if yours has that setting, every scrap of foil comes out before it runs. Catch drips where they fall instead, with a baking tray slid onto the shelf below anything likely to bubble over, thirty seconds and a tray you already own. If foil has already stuck fast, don’t scrape at it; ask the oven’s maker first. Tonight, once the oven is cold, lift the foil out. Then drip a little water onto your bath towel and count how long it takes to sink in.

One tray, one shelf down, and the oven floor stays clean for good. If that water sat on top for a second or two, the second thing is to blame: fabric softener, and the dryer sheets that do the same job. Softener works by coating every fibre in a thin, waxy film, which is exactly why a towel feels smooth and drinks a little less after each wash in it. That film ruins the cloths you clean with, too. A microfibre strand is finer than silk and split into wedges, leaving countless tiny channels where dust and grease lodge; softener fills them, and the cloth starts pushing dirt around instead of lifting it, which is why most microfibre care labels rule softener out. Wash towels and cleaning cloths with detergent alone, the microfibre on a warm cycle away from cotton, whose lint it would grab and keep, then dry them on a rack or on low heat; a few softener-free washes usually start to clear the build-up. For softness, give each towel a hard snap before you hang it, which lifts the loops back up. Before bed, move the softener to the back of the cupboard. And the tea towel by your kitchen sink is leaving more on your glasses than it takes off.

Third is that terry tea towel, because the soft loops that make it feel so absorbent shed lint onto every glass it dries, and into the dust on your shelves. Cotton fibres are short, a few centimetres, about an inch, so terry is a mass of loose ends, and every loop dragged across a glass lets a few go. Flax, the plant linen is spun from, gives fibres that can run longer than your forearm, which is why linen has long been the cloth for polishing glass. In a lot of French kitchens the tea towels are linen, or linen woven with cotton, part of le linge de maison, the cloth a household keeps for its beds, its table and its kitchen. Of everything on this list, linen is where I’d spend, ma chère. A linen tea towel can cost several times a terry one and feels stiff, almost papery, for its first few washes, but it dries faster between uses and a good one lasts for years; a linen and cotton mix sits halfway on price. Keep two for glasses and plates only and give the terry to hands, usually twenty to forty dollars for a linen pair. This evening, hang the terry towel by the sink for hands alone. Then look down: the machine that leaves your floor gleaming may be swelling every joint in it.

The fourth thing is a steam mop, which drives hot moisture down into the joints of a wood or laminate floor. Steam slips into seams no damp cloth could reach, cools there and turns back into water, and the board edges drink it. Solid wood swells and cups; laminate has a core of pressed fibreboard that swells along every joint and never shrinks back, which is why many floor makers rule steam out. Kneeling on the laminate in a flat I was redoing, Thierry, my husband, pressed a fingertip along a raised seam and asked the owner one question: “Steam mop?” She had bought it that winter. On older French parquet that’s still waxed, the habit is a cloth wrung almost dry, because standing water leaves pale marks in wax. A drop of water tells you what your own floor can take: if it beads, the finish is whole; if it soaks in and darkens the wood, keep water off that floor altogether. Use a flat microfibre mop wrung until barely damp, so the floor dries within a minute; one with spare pads costs well under thirty dollars. After supper, wring your mop until no drop falls from it. The vinegar and baking soda in your cupboard come next, and the fizz you trust is the trouble.

Fifth, a mistake: the homemade cleaner that mixes those two, because that fizz is them cancelling each other out. Vinegar is an acid and baking soda is a base, and when they meet they become carbon dioxide, which is the bubbles, plus water and a dissolved salt. Ordinary white vinegar is only about five percent acid, so in most recipes it’s spent within seconds, and the bottle is left holding salty water with some wet baking soda in it. Apart, each does real work. Baking soda is soft, about two and a half on the hardness scale geologists use for minerals, below glass, steel and enamel, so as a paste it scrubs without scratching. Vinegar works only while it’s still sour, so it never shares a bottle with anything alkaline. Keep them in two labelled containers and use one, rinse, then the other if the job needs it; a minute of sorting, and you stop paying for two products that neutralise each other. Right now, pour any mixed batch down the sink and rinse the bottle. While you’re at the sink, check your baking tray: that sticky amber rim probably came out of a can.

Cooking spray is the sixth, and that rim is what it leaves behind. Besides oil, most cooking sprays contain lecithin, the emulsifier on many chocolate wrappers, and at oven heat it bakes into a gummy amber layer that washing-up liquid barely touches. It builds up along edges and corners, where the mist landed and no food covered it, and on non-stick it slowly gums up the very coating it was meant to help, which is why several cookware makers ask you to keep sprays off their non-stick entirely. A French cake recipe very often opens by telling you to butter the tin and dust it with flour, and that’s the habit to copy: a pastry brush and a little oil or butter, only where the food will sit, or a sheet of baking paper. To lift that old layer, spread on a thick paste of baking soda and a little washing-up liquid, leave it an hour, then scrub with a non-scratch pad, ten minutes’ effort for a few cents. Once the kitchen’s quiet, coat your worst tray in that paste and leave it. Then go to the bathroom, where a tablet bought to keep the bowl clean may be quietly wasting water.

Number seven, as promised, is in your toilet tank: the slow-dissolving tablet that turns the water blue. Many of these tablets contain bleach, and sitting in the tank day and night it attacks the rubber flap at the bottom that holds the water back, until the flap warps or softens and water trickles into the bowl around the clock. Several toilet makers warn against in-tank tablets for exactly that reason. If your tank sometimes refills with a short hiss when nobody has flushed, that’s the leak. Plumbers test it with food colouring: a few drops in the tank, no flushing for twenty minutes, and any colour in the bowl means the flap is letting water through. Take the tablet out and give the bowl itself a brush and a squirt of cleaner; if the dye shows a leak, close the little valve behind the toilet, flush the tank empty and fit a new flap, about ten minutes and a few dollars. Before you turn in, lift the tank lid and fish the tablet out. Then run a finger round the rubber fold inside your washing machine’s door.

Whatever came away on your finger belongs to the eighth habit: keeping the washing machine shut between washes. The deep rubber fold in a front-loader’s door holds back a little water, lint and detergent after every cycle, and with the door closed the drum never dries, so the fold turns grey, then slimy, then speckled with black. Makers ask you to leave the door ajar and the detergent drawer pulled out after the day’s last wash. Few French flats have la buanderie, a room given over to the laundry alone, so the machine often stands in a small, steamy bathroom, where a closed drum has even less chance of drying. Most front-loaders also hide a filter behind a small flap at the bottom, catching coins, hairpins and lint, that plenty of owners never open; with the machine off, set a towel and a shallow dish down first, because water comes out too. Wipe the fold dry after the last wash and leave the door open, ten seconds and free, plus an empty hot wash once a month. Last thing tonight, prop the door open and pull the drawer out. Something in your bedroom may be misting right now, turning tap water into dust.

And the ninth discovery, that white dust, comes out of an ultrasonic humidifier filled from the tap. That kind of humidifier shakes water into a cool mist with a small plate vibrating far faster than you can hear, and everything dissolved in the water rides out into the room with it. Each droplet dries in the air, the minerals it carried are left as a powder fine enough to float, and overnight it settles on dark wood, on screens, along the top of the dresser. Wipe it off and it’s back by breakfast, because the machine makes a fresh batch every hour it runs, and the harder your water, the more there is. Rub a little between finger and thumb: ordinary dust is soft and grey, and this is white and faintly gritty. Humidifiers that work through a wick, or by boiling water into steam, leave those minerals inside the machine as a crust you can see and scrub. In a lot of older French homes, small ceramic troughs of water hooked onto the radiators do the same quiet job. On the Croix-Rousse, in the silk weavers’ building of my childhood, one hung on our hall radiator, and every few weeks I scraped a white crust out of it with a teaspoon. That crust, ma chère, is what an ultrasonic machine sends into your room instead. So fill yours with distilled or demineralised water, the kind sold in jugs for steam irons, a dollar or two each, or choose an evaporative model when this one wears out, and empty and dry the tank every day, as its maker asks. At bedtime, tip out the tap water and leave the tank open to dry. Line all nine up and they share one habit, which doubles as a test for anything new.

That habit is the pattern behind all nine: each one leaves something behind, a film on a fibre, lint on a glass, water in a joint, gum on a tray, chalk in the air, and every trace a home collects has to be cleaned away by someone, sooner or later. So whenever a product promises to make cleaning easier, ask what will be left on your things once it has done its job. Tonight needs ten seconds and a bath towel: a few drops of water, and if they bead, the softener goes to the back of the cupboard. The other eight can follow, one room each week. Leave me one number under this video: how many seconds your towel took to swallow those drops. Your bath towel is probably a few steps from where you’re sitting, so go and drip the water now.
