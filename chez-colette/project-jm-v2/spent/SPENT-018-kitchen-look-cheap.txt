Stand at your kitchen counter and look down: the shadow on your chopping board is the first of ten things making your kitchen look cheap. Not one of them is the cabinet boxes you’d blame first. Number nine is the side of the cupboard you see from the doorway. Number ten is the line where your kitchen meets the wall.

That shadow comes first, and you’re the one casting it: in many kitchens the main light hangs mid-ceiling, behind you whenever you face the counter. Your head and shoulders block it exactly where your hands work, and the wall cupboards lay a second, darker band across the back of the worktop, so even good stone looks dull. The cure is a strip of light under those cupboards, and where you fix it matters more than which one you buy. Against the wall, it floods the tiles with glare and leaves the front of the counter dim; near the front edge of the cupboard’s underside, it spreads across the whole surface you chop on. On a glossy worktop, bare LEDs reflect as a row of bright dots, so choose a strip behind a frosted cover. Ceiling downlights belong over that front edge too, never in a grid across the floor. A plug-in strip is twenty minutes’ work and usually under forty dollars a run; anything wired into the wall goes to an electrician. Tonight, hold your phone’s flashlight under one cupboard and watch the counter change. Now look at what’s plugged in under that cupboard: one of those machines can peel the door above it.

One strip of light and twenty minutes brought that counter out of the shade. Colette’s Home Bible has the next steps: light at three heights, which fittings are worth buying, and the kitchen a lease won’t let you touch. Your phone’s camera will read the QR code on the screen, and the description has the address written out. For nothing at all, like this video and subscribe: your other rooms get lists like this one.

The second is a habit: the kettle or coffee maker working under a wall cupboard, breathing steam into its lower edge every morning. On plenty of doors that edge is a plastic strip held by a glue that softens when warmed, and many smooth white doors that pass for painted are a vinyl skin over fibreboard. Warm, wet air, day after day, loosens both: the strip lifts at a corner, the skin bubbles, and one door looks years older than its neighbours. Doing up other people’s rented flats, I could tell where the kettle lived before opening a single cupboard, ma chère: one door with its bottom edge curling, right above a socket. A dishwasher does the same to the worktop above it, which is why many ship with a foil strip for the counter’s underside. The fix is free and takes a minute: anything that steams works under open air. Tonight, move the kettle to a spot with nothing above it. From that counter, follow the wall upward: does the backsplash stop after a hand’s width, or reach the cupboards?

If it stops after a hand’s width, that strip is the third thing: the four-inch backsplash, about ten centimetres of counter turned up the wall, a hallmark of builder-grade kitchens. American kitchens usually leave eighteen inches, about forty-five centimetres, between counter and wall cupboards, so more than a foot of painted wall sits above that strip: a low stripe that stops for no reason. In a lot of French kitchens the panel behind the worktop runs right up to the wall cupboards, in tile, glass or steel, so the wall reads as one finished surface. Wherever tile ends at a side, look at the edge: a glazed tile is clay under a skin of glaze, and a cut shows the clay as a thin pale or reddish line, which a careful tiler hides with a rounded-edge tile or a slim metal trim. Tiling from counter to cupboards, with the strip lifted first where it comes away cleanly, is a weekend and usually a few hundred dollars or less. Before bed, run a finger along your tile ends and look for bare clay. Below the counter, the frame showing between your doors isn’t the style choice it appears to be.

Fourth is that band of frame between the doors, and the hinge behind each door decides how much of it you see. Open a cupboard and look at the hinge: if it’s the hidden kind, a round cup is sunk into the back of the door and an arm reaches across to the cabinet, and the bend in that arm sets how far the door overlaps its frame. A straight arm swings the door over nearly all of the frame, leaving only a fine shadow line; an arm with a slight bend lets it cover part, leaving the band; a deeply bent one sets the door inside its opening, with an even gap all round. From across the room the banded version reads as builder-grade: the eye meets two surfaces and dozens of short lines instead of one calm wall of doors. So new full-overlay doors on old partial-overlay hinges will gape or collide, and refacing means new hinges matched to the new overlay. Hinge makers print the overlay on the box, so order doors and hinges together, usually a few days’ work and well under the cost of new cabinets. Right now, open one door and look at the bend in its hinge arm. Then look down at the strip along the floor beneath them: every time you mop, it drinks.

Number five is that strip, the toe kick, and the swollen, flaking line along its bottom edge is mop water. The recess earns its place, about four inches high and three deep, ten centimetres by seven and a half, so you can stand close to the counter; remove it, as some suggest, and you’ll lean over every job. Its face, though, is usually particleboard under a thin skin, its raw bottom edge on the floor, and each wet mop pushes water in until it swells into a soft, crumbling line. In a lot of French fitted kitchens the cupboards stand on adjustable plastic legs behind a board that clips on and off, and a rubber seal can slot along its lower edge to keep water out. Where the toe kick is part of the box, a thin plywood skin glued over the old face hides the damage, an afternoon and some tens of dollars. If the swelling is only under the sink, or the floor there feels soft, suspect a leak and call a plumber first. After supper, press a thumbnail into the toe kick beside the sink. A few steps along that same floor, your fridge is probably standing out of line.

Your fridge is the sixth thing, and its depth gives it away: a standard American fridge is deeper than the counters beside it, so it juts out of the run like a wardrobe left in the kitchen. Counters stand about twenty-five inches deep, sixty-three centimetres, while a standard-depth fridge usually measures thirty to thirty-six inches, about seventy-five to ninety centimetres, doors included. French fridges tend to stay close to the worktop’s depth, and in plenty of fitted French kitchens the fridge hides behind a cupboard door. Here, though, I’d keep the deep one for a family that cooks, ma chère. A counter-depth model sits nearly flush but holds noticeably less for its width, and a handsome kitchen with the milk living on the counter has failed at its first job. So make the deep one look intended: a tall panel as deep as the fridge on its open side and a deep cupboard above, an afternoon for a carpenter and usually a few hundred dollars, keeping the air gaps the manual asks for. Before bed, measure how far it stands past the counter. Then compare the handles on your widest and narrowest drawers: same size?

Seventh, a mistake plenty of kitchens make: one handle size on every drawer, so the widest look as if their hardware shrank. Designers size a pull at about a third of the drawer’s width, so a pan drawer ninety centimetres wide, three feet, wants a handle of roughly thirty, a foot, or two knobs, while a narrow cutlery drawer keeps a small one. Placement follows the hand: near the bottom corner on wall cupboards, near the top corner on base cupboards, centred on drawers, always the same distance from the edge. Fitters drill through a template, a scrap of wood with two holes in it, because a handle a few millimetres off shows along the whole row. Start with the widest drawers; if the hole spacing differs, fill the old holes and touch up the paint, or choose a pull with a back plate that covers them, an evening’s work at a few dollars to a few tens a pull. While the kitchen’s quiet, measure your widest drawer and divide by three. That raised panel along the back of your range tells you which kind you own.

That panel makes number eight: a freestanding range pushed into a fitted run. Built to stand anywhere, a freestanding range has finished sides and a raised panel across the back, so between cupboards it looks like an appliance parked in a gap, cutting the backsplash off behind it, with a crumb-catching slot each side. A slide-in range puts its controls at the front, has no back panel, and its cooktop laps over the counters on both sides, so the tile runs straight behind it and the slots disappear. Most American ranges are thirty inches wide, seventy-six centimetres, so a slide-in usually fits the same opening when yours is due, though the counter edges or the connection may need adjusting; ask the installer before you buy, and leave any gas line to a licensed fitter. Until then, T-shaped silicone gap covers lie over both slots and trim with scissors, five minutes and a few dollars. Later tonight, slide a butter knife down the gap beside the range. Now walk to your kitchen doorway and turn round: the first cupboard side you see is number nine.

Ninth, as promised, the side of that cupboard, and in a lot of kitchens it’s simply the bare side of a box. Doors carry a profile or a grain, but the end of the run shows its carcass: a flat board in a slightly different white, or a printed wood that doesn’t quite match, ending in a raw corner at the toe kick. A finished kitchen adds an end panel in the same style as the doors, carried down to the floor, so the run ends like a piece of furniture instead of a crate. Kitchen makers sell matching panels, but the cheapest route is often a spare door of the same style, screwed on from inside the cupboard: le système D, which in France means a clever fix built from spare parts rather than a purchase. Use screws a few millimetres shorter than side and panel together, so no point breaks through; an hour’s work for the price of one door, tens of dollars to a couple of hundred. This evening, open that end cupboard and measure how thick its side is. Then follow that side back to the wall and look at what fills the gap.

And the tenth, the line where your kitchen meets the wall, decides whether it looks fitted or merely delivered. No wall is straight: plaster bows and corners lean, so between a dead-straight cabinet and the wall there’s always a gap, wider at one end than the other. A hurried install fills it with a bead of caulk, fat at the top and thin at the bottom, and a dark, tapering line climbs the wall beside your cupboards. A careful fitter scribes instead, copying the wall’s real shape onto the panel and cutting to that line. Thierry, my husband, slides a pencil down the wall pressed against a small wooden block, so every bump in the plaster is traced onto the wood. Then he planes to the line and offers it up again; if he sees daylight anywhere, ma chère, it comes back down. For an existing gap, use scribe moulding, a thin, bendy strip of wood about six millimetres by twenty, a quarter of an inch by three-quarters, nailed to the cabinet, never the wall, so it flexes to follow the plaster; it’s an hour and a few dollars a length. Now notice where all ten were hiding: each lives at a join, where one thing meets another, your body and the light, a kettle and a door, a cupboard and the floor, a range and the counter, a cupboard and the wall. A kitchen is chosen in a showroom one door at a time, and the joins only exist once it’s fitted into your room, so that’s where a rushed install saves its minutes, out of habit rather than any plan. French gives this craft a double name, menuisier-ébéniste: one half fits wood to a building, the other makes furniture, and a good kitchen needs both. On your way out, run a fingertip down the joint beside your last cupboard. Three questions from your kitchen doorway will find most of the ten.

Standing where you chop, where does the light come from, and is your own body blocking it? Where does each surface end, a door at its frame, a run at the floor, the wall or the fridge, and does it finish or simply stop? And what touches this kitchen every day, a hand, a mop, a kettle’s steam, and was that spot built to take it? Moving the kettle costs one minute and nothing else, so it goes first; the handles, the toe kick and the scribe moulding each want an afternoon of their own. Under this video, name what sits beneath your wall cupboards: kettle, coffee maker, toaster, or nothing. The kettle comes first, so set it down somewhere with open air above it before tonight’s last cup.
