You’ve bought the bins and the vacuum bags, and your tiny apartment still has no room. Twelve French organization tricks win it back, mostly tonight. Plenty of Paris studios are under two hundred square feet, and they’re rarely rescued by another bin. I’m Colette. Before I left decorating, my trade was small rented studios in Lyon. In rooms like those, the bed, the desk and the kitchen often share one wall. Number two is your frying pan, taking nearly twice the shelf it needs. And number twelve is a cupboard half a wardrobe big, and you sleep on it.

Number one: the empty strip beside your bed. Most double beds stand with both long sides open, and the walkway on the side nobody uses covers twice the floor of a wardrobe a metre wide. That strip is about sixty centimetres wide, two feet. In a lot of French studios the bed goes into a corner instead, with one long side against the wall. Do the same. Lift it with someone on the other end rather than dragging it, ten minutes’ work that costs nothing. Then fill what you’ve freed straight away. The chest of drawers, the laundry basket and the shoes crowding the wardrobe door can all move into that strip. Sleeping alone? You lose nothing. For two, whoever takes the wall side climbs in from the foot. You make the bed by sliding it out a little, tucking the wall edge and pushing it home. Honestly, it’s the cheapest floor you’ll ever find. Tonight, measure the walkway on the side nobody uses. Then open the cupboard where your frying pan lives, and see which way its handle points.

One strip of floor, ten minutes, and a row of storage. Like this video and subscribe, because the coming videos take a tiny flat apart room by room, measurements included.

Number two: the frying pan’s handle. It takes more shelf than the pan itself. A pan twenty-four centimetres across, nine and a half inches, usually carries a handle about twenty long. So it claims a strip of shelf nearly twice as long as the pan is wide. It also stops the next pan nesting inside. In a lot of French kitchens the handles click off. A frying pan, two saucepans and a sauté pan sit in one tower, and one handle serves whichever is on the stove. Very neat. Nest your own pans largest at the bottom, and turn each handle a quarter turn from the one below so they fan out. Stand the lids on edge in a plate rack, five minutes and a few dollars. Are your pans cast iron or thick steel? Then keep them. A riveted handle is always stiffer than a clip-on one, and cast iron can outlive its owner. Clip-off handles are the upgrade for the day a pan wears out. Before bed, nest every pan you own into one stack. Then look at the round jars on your pantry shelf, and at the shape of the air between them.

Five minutes and a stack of pans gave back a shelf. Colette’s Home Bible has dozens more. Number three: round jars on a shelf. That air is a little over a fifth of the shelf, lost in the gaps. A circle covers only about three quarters of the square drawn around it, and those leftover corners can’t hold a thing. Pure geometry. Square containers of the same height sit flush against each other and against the back wall. So the same shelf holds roughly a quarter more. Pour the dry goods behind the cupboard door into square things you already own, takeaway tubs or freezer boxes. Lots of French kitchens decant lentils, rice and sugar into glass jars, and the classic French preserving jar, with its orange rubber ring, is round. Keep those out on the counter, where they’re looked at. Behind a door, only capacity counts. Does your shelf divide neatly? One that’s thirty centimetres deep, twelve inches, takes two rows of fifteen-centimetre boxes exactly. Twenty-centimetre ones leave a dead strip at the back. A matched set of square boxes is the later upgrade, a few dollars a box. This evening, refill one shelf with square containers only. Now think of the door under your kitchen sink, and the floor it takes every time it opens.

Number four: a curtain instead of a cupboard door. That swing is the hidden cost of every door. A cupboard door forty centimetres wide, sixteen inches, needs that much floor clear in front of it every time it opens. A curtain asks for no floor at all. Older French kitchens often hid the space under the sink behind a gathered curtain on a thin rod. Under my own sink there’s no door, just a strip of old linen on a wire. I push it aside with my hip when my hands are full, ma chère, and I’ve never once missed the hinge. Where does it help most? Under the sink, across open shelves, and on a wardrobe door that catches on the bed frame. Fit a tension rod inside the opening, so there’s no drilling, and hang any heavy cotton you own, half an hour and under twenty dollars. Choose cloth you can machine-wash. Kitchens are messy. If you rent, unscrew the doors, bag the hinges with their screws, and put everything back on moving day. Later tonight, measure the cupboard door that swings into your busiest path. In your linen cupboard, the bulkiest thing is the one most homes keep two of.

Number five: the off-season duvet. It fills a whole shelf for half the year, because even folded, a duvet is mostly trapped air. It belongs in a suitcase instead. Fold it in three lengthwise, roll it tight from the foot, and slide it into the big case you’ll meet in number six. French bedding shops have a smarter answer for later, though. They sell it as the four-season duvet: a thin layer and a medium one that join at the edges with press studs or ties. The thin one alone is for summer, and the medium one covers spring and autumn. Joined, they’re the winter duvet. That’s three duvets in the space of two. The label gives the filling’s weight in grams per square metre, about two hundred for summer and three hundred for mid-season. When a guest stays, the pair comes apart into two duvets for two beds. Keep a good duvet until it wears out, then buy the joined set, so the cost waits until you’d be shopping anyway. Last thing tonight, roll the duvet into the case and zip it shut. Every other case you own is still taking a box’s worth of room to store air.

Number six: the suitcases you keep empty. A big checked case holds roughly eighty to a hundred litres, and a cabin case about forty. Stored empty, each takes the room of a packed box and holds nothing. Seems silly, doesn’t it? Few tiny flats come with a storeroom to put them in. So make the suitcase the storeroom. The big one already holds the duvet from number five. Put a soft holdall inside the cabin case. Then fill the gaps with what only leaves home when you do: adaptors, the wash bag, beach towels, ski gloves. Keep the travel things in one cloth bag, so on the morning you pack, it lifts out whole. Tie a luggage label to each handle listing what’s inside. Nobody has to hunt. It takes a quarter of an hour and costs nothing. Before you sleep, put the holdall inside the cabin case. Half the list is done. The cupboard you sleep on comes at twelve. First, the folding board behind a door, which a curtain maker would call too small to work on.

Number seven: the ironing board. A curtain maker would find it far too narrow, and so should you. Folded, it stands about a metre and a third tall and needs a door or a gap to hide behind. Open, its top is only about thirty-eight centimetres wide, fifteen inches, so a shirt gets shifted again and again. Your table does the job better, a minute to set up and free. Lay a wool blanket folded double, then an old cotton sheet. That’s it. The wool soaks up heat and steam, and the cotton won’t print a weave onto the shirt. In her workroom, my grandmother, Mémé Suzanne, pressed curtain panels three metres long flat across her table, over a grey blanket worn thin and a bleached sheet. “A board,” she said, “is for collars.” Watch your finish, though. On a varnished or polished top, steam that gets through can leave a white cloud, so fold the blanket thicker or use the kitchen table. The board can go to someone with a laundry room. Later, press one shirt on the table and count how often you move it. In the smallest room you have, a stretch of wall taller than you is holding nothing at all.

Number eight: the wall above the toilet. It’s taller than you’d think. A cistern stands about eighty centimetres high, so above it there’s usually a metre and a half of bare wall, as wide as the toilet. In many French flats, the wall above the cistern carries shelves or a small placard, the cupboard that comes with the flat rather than with the furniture. Spare rolls and cleaning bottles go up there, and the basin cupboard breathes. Two rules matter. Keep the lowest shelf at least thirty centimetres, a foot, above the lid, because the lid has to lift off for any repair. And keep the shelves shallow, about twenty centimetres, so they don’t loom over whoever’s sitting. Pipes often run up behind a cistern, so pass a detector over the wall before you drill. If you rent, a freestanding unit that straddles the toilet needs no holes. Two brackets and a plank take forty minutes and under twenty dollars. Small room, big wall. Next time you’re in there, measure from the lid to the ceiling. Now open the door of the room you’re in as wide as it goes, and see where it stops.

Number nine: the strip of wall behind an open door. If the door stopped a hand’s width short, that gap is storage. Nobody walks there. And when a kitchen or bedroom door stands open most of the day, nobody sees it either. The handle sets the gap, usually six or seven centimetres. A longer door stop holds the door about twelve centimetres off the wall, five inches, and that fits a row of hooks or a slim rack. That’s room for a mop, a broom, the vacuum hose and the ironing blanket from number seven. It works like a sliver of la buanderie, the laundry room most small flats never get. Swapping the stop takes a screwdriver, a few dollars and about as long as a coffee. The hooks go on the wall, never on the door. Is your front door a fire door? Then leave it out of this, because screwing into one can weaken it. I like this one a lot, ma chère. Before you turn the lights off, find the door that stays open longest. By your front door, every pair of shoes is taking more shelf than it needs.

Number ten: the shoes by your front door. Two shoes side by side take the full width of both. Turn one around, heel to toe, and the pair sits narrower, because the slim heel tucks in beside the wide toe. A cupboard door that never stands open in a walkway can carry an over-the-door rack, a dozen pairs for under twenty dollars. In a lot of French homes shoes come off at the door and slippers go on. So the shoes stay in l’entrée, the entrance hall, or in a studio, the square metre just inside the front door. A man’s shoe is about thirty centimetres long, so a rack where pairs lie flat needs that much depth. The later upgrade is a slim cabinet with flip-down compartments. It stands shoes steeply, toe up, at about seventeen to twenty-five centimetres deep. That’s seven to ten inches. Those cabinets are top-heavy once full, so the wall fixing goes in before the first pair. Boots won’t tilt. Stand them at the end of the row, on a tray. Tonight, line your shoes up heel to toe and count the pairs. In your kitchen, there’s a band of wall above the counter, and it’s holding nothing.

Number eleven: a rail above the kitchen counter. That band runs between the worktop and the wall cupboards, usually about forty-five centimetres tall. Nothing can stand there without blocking the counter, but things can hang. In a lot of French kitchens a slim metal rail runs along that wall, with S-hooks for the ladle, the whisk, the spatula and the sieve. Everything hangs within reach of the stove, and the utensil drawer ends up half empty. Simple, and it works. Try it tonight with what you have. A spare curtain rod on two screw hooks will carry light tools, and a bag of S-hooks costs a few dollars. Hang only what you use every week. Space the hooks a hand apart so nothing tangles. Keep the rail clear of the back of the hob, where steam and splashes coat everything in grease. A proper rail screws in with two brackets, twenty minutes and under twenty dollars. If you rent, ask before you drill. After dinner, empty the utensil drawer and hang the five tools you use most. That rail freed one drawer. The space under your bed could swallow several.

Number twelve: the space under your bed. It’s far bigger than it looks, once you give it some height. Picture a mattress a hundred and forty by a hundred and ninety centimetres, the classic French double and about the size of an American full. Raise it so there’s thirty centimetres underneath, about a foot. That gap encloses around seven hundred litres, more than half of a wardrobe a metre wide. Bed risers make that gap tonight. They go under each foot and lift the bed ten or fifteen centimetres, enough for flat lidded boxes on wheels. Because your bed from number one stands against the wall, the boxes roll out from the foot and the open side. Lift each end with someone else, never alone. Risers and two boxes cost a few tens of dollars and take a few minutes. The upgrade can wait. In a lot of small French bedrooms the whole base lifts, on two gas struts like the back of a hatchback car. It opens from above, so it needs no floor in front of it, which suits a bed against a wall. The struts are rated in newtons for a mattress’s weight. Too weak, and the base won’t stay up. Too strong, and it fights you. So give the seller your mattress weight, and fit it with a second pair of hands. My husband Thierry ran a thumb down one of those struts and said gas tires and wood doesn’t, so he’d fit drawers. Drawers need sixty centimetres of floor to open, I said, and in a small bedroom that’s the walkway. We sleep on his drawers anyway, ma chère. At bedtime, measure the gap under your bed with your phone light and a ruler. Put all twelve side by side, and they share one pattern.

Every one of the twelve finds space you already have and never counted. The floor beside a bed. The air around a pan’s handle, and between round jars. The swing of a cupboard door. The inside of a suitcase, and the table you eat at. The walls above a cistern, behind a door and over the counter. The width of a pair of shoes, and the gap under a mattress. So before anything new comes home to hold your things, find that air first, and measure it. The pans are the easiest, five minutes and one cupboard. The jars, the duvet and the shoes fit into the same evening. The rail, the toilet shelf and the risers each want an afternoon and a screwdriver, and the bed needs a helper.
