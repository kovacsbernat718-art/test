There are ten design choices in your home right now that decide whether it feels cozy, and French homes tend to make them differently. You’ve bought the fleece throw and the vanilla candle and turned up the thermostat, and you still sit on the sofa in a sweater, feet tucked under you. Plenty of French flats are heated to about nineteen degrees, sixty-six Fahrenheit, and still feel snug, and blankets aren’t the reason. Whether a room feels warm depends as much on what touches your skin, what your body faces and where the light falls as on the thermostat. Ten tips cover all three, and with them a cozy room takes an evening, not a shopping trip. Number three is probably under your window, heating the wrong side of your curtains. And number ten explains the rest, starting with why your grey sofa changes colour after dark.

The first tip is something your feet get wrong: the floor that feels coldest usually isn’t any colder. Walk barefoot from kitchen tiles onto wood and the tile bites, yet in one room the two settle at almost the same temperature. Your skin doesn’t actually measure temperature. It measures how fast heat leaves you, and stone and ceramic draw heat from a bare foot several times faster than wood, while wool, full of trapped air, hardly takes any. In sixteen eighty-four, the marble floors in the king’s private rooms at Versailles began to give way to wooden boards, and those square panels, criss-crossed inside, are still called parquet de Versailles, Versailles parquet. So put something warm exactly where bare feet land first: beside the bed, in front of the sofa, at the basin. A small wool rug is usually under a hundred dollars new and far less second-hand; on bathroom tiles, use cork. Tonight, stand with one foot on tile and one on wood, and feel a difference no thermometer would find. Your feet lose heat by touch; on the sofa, your shoulders lose it to something they never touch.

Ten seconds barefoot showed you where the money belongs. There are many more tests that fast in Colette’s Home Bible: how low a table light should hang, what a lampshade really does, and what goes behind a bed. Get it through the link in the description below, or the QR code on the screen.

Second, a habit for nightfall: cover the glass, because much of the chill beside a window isn’t a draught at all. Sit beside one after dark, every pane shut, and one shoulder still goes cold. Your body sends heat out as invisible infrared to every surface facing it, and each sends some back, but a cold one returns less than it takes, and at night the coldest surface in your sitting room is the window. Heating engineers weigh the temperature of the walls and glass around you about as much as the air’s. In a great many French homes the shutters close every evening, winter and summer, putting wood and still air between room and night. My grandmother, Mémé Suzanne, sewed winter curtains with a hidden layer of thick cotton flannel behind the face fabric, so once drawn, the whole wall became soft cloth at room temperature, ma chère, instead of black glass. Drawing lined curtains takes ten seconds, and hook-on thermal linings usually cost less than a new pair. When it’s dark, hold your palm near the glass, then near the wall, and feel which pulls. First, though, look under that window.

Third, the mistake I promised at the start: a long curtain drawn over the radiator or heating vent beneath your window, which sends your heat up the cold glass and out of the house. The heater sits there on purpose. Glass chills the air touching it, that air sinks towards your ankles, and heat placed right under the window warms it on the way down. Hang a floor-length curtain in front, and the heat is trapped in the gap behind the cloth and climbs straight up the coldest surface in the room. A sofa against a radiator or over a vent does much the same. So at night, tuck the hem behind a water radiator or onto the sill, keeping the heat on your side of the cloth; never let cloth touch an electric heater. Leave every radiator and vent clear, no rug across a grille. A shelf fixed just above a radiator turns rising heat out into the room: an hour and a few dollars of wood. After supper, draw the curtain, slip a hand behind it above the radiator and feel the warm air climb. Then switch on the ceiling light and look at the faces underneath it.

Fourth, a rule for the evening: light never comes only from above. Look at whoever is under that ceiling light: dark hollows round the eyes, a shadow under the nose and chin, the face of someone who slept badly. Light falling from straight overhead catches the brow, nose and cheekbones and leaves everything below them in shade, which is why portrait photographers avoid the midday sun. A room where everyone looks worn out never feels restful, however soft the sofa. Light from the side reaches into the eye sockets and under the chin, and the same faces look rested. It also lets the ceiling go dark, so the room seems to draw in around the people in it. So after dark, the ceiling light goes off and two lamps take over, one either side of the seats. Carrying a lamp you already own to a side table takes two minutes and not a cent. This evening, photograph your face under the ceiling light and again beside a lamp, and compare your eyes. One ceiling light deserves to stay on, and in most homes it hangs far too high: the one above your table.

Fifth: bring that light down until it lights the table instead of the room. The fitting went up at a height people can walk under before anyone knew where your table would stand, and nobody walks under a table. The usual height for the bottom of a pendant shade is about seventy-five centimetres, thirty inches, above the tabletop. There, it pours one circle of light over plates and faces and leaves the room around them dim. Hung high, it lights the corners and the clutter instead. Plenty of French families still eat supper under a hanging light, and it begins with à table, meaning to the table, the call that brings everyone in. Many pendants hang from a chain or adjustable cord that shortens in minutes with the power off at the breaker, and a ceiling hook costing a few dollars swings the cord over an off-centre table. If the cord must be cut or the fitting moved, call an electrician. Once the plates are cleared, measure from tabletop to shade. That circle only holds if the walls let its edges fall dark, which depends on a number beside your paint colour.

Number six hides on paint charts: every colour carries a number for how much light it gives back. It’s the light reflectance value, printed on many charts, running from zero for black to a hundred for a perfect white. Most popular whites sit above eighty, so lamplight bounces round until every corner is lit. Below about fifty, the walls keep more than they return, and light stays where you put it. This part is my opinion as a decorator: a small room used mostly at night should be painted deep, because after dark it wraps the lamp like a coat. It comes at a price, though. A deep room needs an extra lamp, since dark walls leave less light to read by. A room you work in by day, especially facing north, can’t spare the daylight a deep colour swallows, so it stays pale. And in a rented flat, ask before you paint, and keep a weekend free to paint it back before you leave. A tester pot is the price of a coffee or two. Later tonight, look up your wall colour’s number. Then look down at the rug those walls surround.

For the seventh, correct a mistake most sitting rooms make: a rug too small for the seats around it. Usually it’s a rectangle in the middle, seats standing round it on bare floor. The eye reads a rug’s edge as a border, so whatever stands inside belongs together and whatever stands outside floats. A small rug makes an island and strands every seat against the walls. The trade rule is that at least the front legs of every seat rest on the rug, with a band of bare floor, roughly thirty to forty-five centimetres, a foot to a foot and a half, round the edge of the room. Then sofa, chairs and low table share one floor, and the group becomes a room inside the room. Big wool rugs are expensive, but a flat-woven jute or cotton one costs a fraction as much, and the small rug you love can lie on top. Once this video ends, spend ten minutes marking the size you need with painter’s tape, and leave it down for a day. You’ve drawn a small room on the floor, and old French bedrooms built a real one around the bed.

Tip eight puts walls around your sleep, the way those bedrooms did. In Brittany, farmhouses had beds built like wooden cupboards with doors, and on winter nights sleepers shut themselves in. In grander houses the bed often stood in an alcove with curtains across it. A sleeping body gives off heat all night: in a big room it rises and drifts away, while in a small closed space it stays around the sleeper. Check your own bed: very often the headboard stands against an outside wall, sometimes under a window. Move the head of the bed to an inside wall, one with another room behind it rather than the night. Then give it a tall padded headboard, or a half-canopy: fabric hung from a rod near the ceiling, falling behind the pillows. Moving a bed takes half an hour, and a rod with brackets is under twenty dollars. At bedtime, lay one palm on the wall behind your pillow and the other on an inside wall. A warm room’s oldest centre is a flame, ma chère, and in a good many older French flats it belongs in a hearth gone cold for years.

The ninth belongs in that empty hearth: give the room one living flame. A candle burns at about nineteen hundred kelvin, far warmer in colour than any bulb sold as warm white. And it moves. The edge of your vision is especially quick to catch movement, so a flicker keeps tugging the eye back, and everyone’s attention gathers where it burns. For centuries the fire did that job. In a lot of older French flats the fireplace was sealed long ago, and a common answer is to fill the cold hearth with candles so the room keeps its centre. Do the same in an unused fireplace, or in a deep dish on the low table: five or six candles of different heights, a few dollars and five minutes. Keep them clear of curtains, and put them out when you leave. If you’d love a real fire, have a chimney sweep inspect the flue before the first match. Before the night is out, light one candle, switch everything else off for a minute, and watch where your eyes go. Then look at the sofa in its glow: if it’s grey, it’s about to explain all ten tips.

And the tenth explains the other nine: choose everything by the light you’ll live in, which is lamplight. In the shop, that sofa was soft dove grey; by candlelight it’s a muddy lump. A surface can only send back colours present in the light falling on it, and lamplight is rich in red and orange and poor in blue, so cool greys and whites go flat after dark while wood, rust and terracotta glow. Run back through the list: the floor was chosen in shoes, the curtains hanging open by day, the ceiling light in an empty room at walking height, the white walls on a sunny Saturday. Nearly everything in a home is decided on a bright afternoon, and coziness only happens after dark. A showroom lights its sofas far brighter than any evening sitting room, because fabric looks crisp that way. Nobody’s tricking anyone. A shop lights the moment of choosing, the one moment in a sofa’s life it ever sees. My father, Jean-Marc, never mixed a grey from black and white, because that grey leans cool and dies under a lamp. He built his greys from white with a touch of ochre and umber, colours dug from the earth, warm before any lamp touches them, and after dark our kitchen on the Croix-Rousse glowed like a bakery window. If the cold grey is already here, give it warm company: a rust throw, a wooden tray, a terracotta pot, the colours lamplight flatters. Last thing tonight, put a grey cushion and a wooden spoon under the same lamp and see which still looks alive.

Put the ten together and they come down to three laws. Warmth is what touches your skin and what your body faces, as much as the number on the thermostat. After dark, light belongs low and beside people, never only overhead. And nothing large comes home until you’ve seen it under the lamp it will live with. The curtain tucked behind the radiator is a minute’s work, so let that one be tonight’s, before the room has cooled. One question for underneath this video, in a few words: how far is it from your tabletop to the bottom of the light above it? Your curtains are probably still open, so go and look at what stands under your window now.
