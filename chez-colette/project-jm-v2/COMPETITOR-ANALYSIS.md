### What loses

- **One room in the title, on a small channel**: kitchen, bathroom or bedroom versions get 1–5% of the whole-home version's views (The Expensive Look: 293K for the home, about 4K each for the kitchen and bathroom).

## 4. How the best scripts keep people watching

Here is what the strongest scripts have in common, and where each technique appears:

1. **The first sentence repeats the promise in the title.** The viewer knows instantly they clicked the right thing. *(All of them.)*
2. **The problem is a scene in the viewer's own home.** "There is a set of china in your cabinet right now…" *(JM, Home Central, The Expensive Look)*
3. **A specific tease, then a saved payoff.** "Number seven is sitting on your coffee table right now." "Stay for the last one; it does more than the other nine combined." *(The Expensive Look, JM, Hayley Wells, Daniel Hale)*
4. **Each item opens with its point.** No warm-up sentence. *(Vanished Rooms, Reynard)*
5. **Each item ends by pointing at the next one.** "And the next swap hides behind a door you open every single morning." *(Hayley Wells, Five By Nine, JM)* The viewer never reaches a natural place to stop.
6. **Something new roughly every minute.** A named effect, a number, a story, a reversal.
7. **The family story sits in the middle of an item, as proof.** JM puts "my mother", "my aunt" and "my sister" at 34–41% and 90% of the video. Never in the hook.
8. **Small sales mentions are woven in.** Reynard's app plug is two sentences and goes straight back into content. JM's book pitch grows out of the tip before it.
9. **No wind-down at the end.** Five By Nine's last 20 seconds are "watch this video next, where I'll teach you…". Every sentence of the ending is still content.

## 5b. The pitch: why yours loses people and theirs doesn't

Here's every Japanese Method pitch in the top 10, reduced to its moves. They all follow the same pattern:

1. **The link:** "that test costs you nothing, and that's true of every rule on this list" / "that's the pattern behind all 11 of these".
2. **The more:** "there are hundreds more like it, small changes that keep a home calm without spending a cent".
3. **Where:** "they're all in my book; scan the QR code on your screen or click the link in the description".
4. **Back:** "Now, the fourth one is…", straight into the next item.

That's 2–3 sentences, 15–25 seconds. It never leaves the viewer's home, and it sounds different every time because it grows out of the tip that came just before it.

Colette's pitch was a 75-word story about her grandmother's notebook, word for word in every video, arriving after only one tip. To a stranger, a story about someone they've never met is the clearest signal that the useful part has stopped. Returning viewers hear the exact same paragraph every time. The notebook story is now retired from the scripts. It works well in the video description and on the website, where people are already interested in the book.
