This book is educational. It describes principles of light, colour, proportion and material,

Nothing in this book asks you to carry out electrical, gas, plumbing or structural work.
Where a correction touches any of those, you are told to stop and call someone licensed —

BEFORE WE START
A word from Colette
-------------------

I grew up in a building that was never meant for people. It was built for looms.

The Croix-Rousse is the hill above Lyon where the silk weavers worked. Their Jacquard looms
stood nearly four metres tall, so the buildings were put up with four-metre ceilings and
enormous windows facing north, because north light does not move and does not glare and you
can match a thread by it all day. The weavers are long gone. Ordinary families live in their
workrooms now, and mine was one of them.

So I was raised being told which windows were the good ones. That is a strange thing to grow
up knowing, and it turns out to be the most useful thing in this book.

My grandmother made curtains. Not as a hobby — as a trade, for forty-one years, in other
people’s apartments all over that city. Curtains, blinds, cushions, loose covers,
upholstery. She saw the inside of hundreds of homes and she had hard opinions about every
one of them. She kept a cloth-bound notebook of every window she ever measured: the drop,
the width, the fullness, the colour she had mixed to go with it, and the name of the family.
Near the end of her life she made me promise I would write it all down properly before it
was lost.

This is me keeping that promise, with fourteen years of my own work added to hers.

Those fourteen years were not spent in beautiful houses. They were spent in small flats,
rented flats, ground-floor rooms that never saw the sun, and apartments that a landlord had
ruined with the cheapest white paint and the wrong lights. That is the work I know. If you
are looking for a book about châteaux, this is not it.

I left the trade because it only knows one verb. Buy. A client would ask me why her sitting
room felt wrong and the profession had trained me to answer with a shopping list. And most
of the time the shopping list was not the answer. Most of the time the room was suffering
three or four specific things that could be named, and two of them could be fixed before
dinner with what was already in the flat.

That is the whole argument of this book, and I will say it once here and then spend two
hundred pages proving it.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE ARGUMENT
  | A room is not decorated. A room is corrected.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

Your taste was never the problem. Nobody ever showed you what to look at. Let me show you.

CHAPTER ONE
The room is not the problem
---------------------------

You have already tried. That is the part nobody credits you for.

You have moved the sofa. You have moved it back. You have bought cushions, and then
different cushions. At some point you painted a wall, and it looked better for about a week,
and then it went back to looking the way it looked before. You have a folder of saved
photographs of rooms you like, and not one of them looks like yours, and you cannot say why.

So you have quietly arrived at the conclusion that the problem is you. That other people
have an eye and you do not. That your room is simply an ordinary room and this is what an
ordinary room looks like.

I have been in several hundred of these rooms. That is not what is happening.

What is happening is that you are being asked to solve a problem you have not been given the
vocabulary for. Nobody taught you to look at a room the way a decorator looks at a room,
which is not as a collection of objects but as a set of relationships — between the light
and the wall, between the height of a thing and the height of your eye, between one material
and the next. Without that vocabulary there is only one move available to you, and that move
is to buy something. So you buy something, and it changes the objects in the room without
changing any of the relationships, and the room looks the same.

And every single part of this industry is content for that to continue.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | WHAT WE ARE ACTUALLY DOING
  | Almost no ordinary room is short of objects. Nearly all of them are suffering three or
  | four specific, nameable errors.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

Three or four. Not thirty. That is the good news buried in this, and it is the reason this
book is possible at all. The errors repeat. They are the same errors, in flat after flat, in
one country after another, and once you know their names you will see them in every room you
walk into for the rest of your life — including on television, including in the photographs
you saved.

Most of them cost nothing to correct. Some of them take twenty minutes. One or two are worth
real money, and when we get to those I will tell you plainly.


[ page 7 ]

The four errors
---------------

Here they are. Every one of them gets its own page after this, and every one of them gets a
whole part of the book later on. For now, just learn the four names.


ONE · THE ROOM IS LIT FROM ONE HEIGHT

There is a light in the middle of the ceiling and that is all there is. Every object in the
room is lit from the same angle, so nothing has any depth, and everyone in the room looks
tired.


TWO · EVERYTHING IS HUNG TOO LOW

The pictures, the curtain pole, the mirror, the pendant. All of it sitting lower than it
should, which drags the whole room down towards the floor and makes the ceiling feel closer
than it is.


THREE · ONE TEXTURE, REPEATED

Everything in the room is the same kind of surface. Four soft things, or four shiny things,
or four flat painted things. The room reads as a showroom, or as a waiting area, and you
cannot say why because nothing in it is ugly.


FOUR · NOBODY HAS EVER LOOKED AT THE CEILING

It is the largest surface in the room. No furniture will ever hide any part of it. It is the
last thing you see at night and the first thing you see in the morning. And you painted it
white without once asking whether that was the right answer.

ERROR ONE
The room is lit from one height
-------------------------------

Go and stand in your sitting room tonight with the lights on as you normally have them. Now
look at the ceiling, and count the sources.

In most rooms the answer is one. There is a fitting in the middle of the ceiling, it is
doing all of the work, and everything you own is being lit from directly above.

Here is why that single fact is doing so much damage.

Light from directly overhead falls on the top of everything and nothing else. The top of the
sofa arm is bright, the front of it is not. The top of your head is bright and your eye
sockets are in shadow, which is why everyone in a room lit this way looks exhausted. Nothing
has a lit side and a shaded side, so nothing has any apparent depth — and a room without
depth reads flat whatever you paid for the furniture in it.

And because that one fitting lights the whole room from one point, it has to be bright. So
the brightness is the thing you notice, and the room feels like a place where tasks are
performed rather than a place where anyone sits.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | COLETTE’S RULE OF THE THREE HEIGHTS
  | Light must arrive at three heights — overhead, table, and floor. A room lit from one
  | height is a room lit like a corridor.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

This is my rule rather than a law of physics, and Part Two is entirely about it. But the
correction is so cheap that I want you to have it now.


THE TWENTY-MINUTE VERSION

Tonight, turn the ceiling light off. Put two lamps on in the room instead — any two lamps
you already own, one at roughly the height of a side table and one lower or taller than that
— and put them in two different corners rather than side by side. Then sit down in the chair
you actually sit in and look at the room.

Nothing bought, nothing moved more than a metre. And every object in that room now has a lit
side and a shaded side, which is the whole difference between a room and a corridor.

  [ IF YOU ONLY HAVE ONE LAMP ]
Use it, and turn the ceiling light off anyway. One lamp and a dark corner beats one ceiling
fitting and none — page 102.


[ page 9 ]

ERROR TWO
Everything is hung too low
--------------------------

This is the most common error in this book and the cheapest one to correct, which is a
combination you will not meet often.

Almost everything that hangs in an ordinary home hangs lower than it should. Pictures,
mirrors, curtain poles, pendant lights, shelves, coat hooks. It happens for a reason that is
completely understandable: you hang a thing at the height where it is comfortable to hold it
up against the wall. That is roughly the height of your own hands at rest, and your hands at
rest are a good deal lower than where the object needed to go.

Repeat that across nine or ten objects and the effect is cumulative. The whole visual weight
of the room collects in a band about a metre off the floor, everything above that band is
empty, and the empty part reads as unfinished while the crowded part reads as cluttered.
Both at once, in the same room.


THE TWO NUMBERS THAT FIX MOST OF IT

    A picture hangs with its centre at ...................................... 145 cm · 57 in
    A curtain pole goes as close to the ceiling as it can, and the curtain drops to the
    floor
                                                                            0–1 cm clearance
Go and measure one picture in your home right now. If the centre of it is at 120 or 125
centimetres, which it probably is, you have found one of your three or four errors, and the
fix costs one nail hole and four minutes.

  [ ONE HONEST EXCEPTION ]
Art hung above a sofa or a console follows the furniture, not the room — leave roughly 15 to
25 cm (6 to 10 in) between the top of the furniture and the bottom of the frame, and let
that decide the height. Page 88 covers the whole business of hanging over furniture,
including what to do when the two rules disagree.

Curtains are the larger prize and they get their own chapter, because moving a curtain pole
upward by twenty centimetres does something to a room that is genuinely difficult to believe
until you have seen it. The window appears taller. The ceiling appears higher. The wall
stops looking like a wall with a hole in it.

ERROR THREE
One texture, repeated
---------------------

This is the error that makes a room full of nice things feel like a hotel lobby.

Stand in the doorway and do not look at colour or at shape. Look only at surface. What is
each thing in this room actually made of, and what would it feel like under your hand?

In a great many rooms the honest answer comes back as one material wearing four costumes. A
grey fabric sofa, a grey fabric armchair, a grey fabric ottoman, and a grey fabric curtain:
four soft things. Or: a glass table, a gloss-painted unit, a mirror, and a chrome lamp —
four hard shiny things. Nothing in either room is ugly. Nothing is even badly chosen. And
the room is lifeless, because a surface only reads as interesting next to a surface that is
not like it.

A hand runs over linen and then over oak and then over unglazed clay and registers three
completely different things. A hand that runs over four woven surfaces registers one thing,
four times. Your eye does exactly the same arithmetic, faster, and without telling you.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | COLETTE’S RULE OF THE FOURTH TEXTURE
  | A room reads as finished when four textures meet: something woven, something wooden,
  | something ceramic or stone, and something living. Three is a showroom. Four is a home.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

This is mine, and it is a rule of thumb rather than a truth — but I have used it for
fourteen years and it has never once let me down in a room that was not working. Count the
families. If you can only find two, you have found another of your errors, and it is almost
always the cheapest one in the book to fix, because the missing family is usually clay or
wood and both of those can be answered from a charity shop for the price of a coffee.


THE FOUR FAMILIES

    FAMILY                                     | READS AS             | WHERE IT USUALLY IS
    ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    Woven                                      | soft, absorbs light  | sofa, curtain, rug,
    Wooden                                     | warm, grained, matte | floor, table, frame,
    Ceramic or stone                           | cool, dense, slightl | almost always the mi
    Living                                     | irregular, moves     | one plant, or branch

Metal and glass are not a fifth family so much as a seasoning. A room can carry a little and
does not need any. If your room is all metal and glass, what it is missing is probably wood.

ERROR FOUR
Nobody has ever looked at the ceiling
-------------------------------------

In French we have a name for the ceiling that English does not have. We call it le cinquième
mur — the fifth wall.

I did not understand what a good name that was until I started working in other people’s
homes and noticed that the ceiling was the only surface in the house that nobody had ever
made a decision about. Every other surface had been considered. Somebody had chosen the
floor. Somebody had chosen, or at least argued about, the colour of the walls. The ceiling
was white because ceilings are white.

Consider what that surface actually is. It is the largest single uninterrupted plane in the
room — larger than any wall, because no window or door is cut out of it. It is the only
surface that no piece of furniture will ever stand in front of, which means you see all of
it, all of the time. And it is the last thing you look at before you go to sleep and the
first thing you look at when you wake up.

One coat of paint. Nobody sells it to you, because there is nothing in it for anyone.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE LAW OF THE FIFTH WALL
  | The ceiling is the largest surface in the room, the only one no furniture will ever
  | hide, and the only one you painted white without once asking why.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


WHAT THE CEILING IS DOING TO YOUR ROOM RIGHT NOW

A pale ceiling appears to recede. A darker ceiling appears to come closer. That is a real
perceptual effect and you can use it in either direction, which is the part almost nobody is
told: a low ceiling is not automatically improved by white paint, and a very high ceiling in
a small room is often improved by a darker one, because it brings the room down to a human
scale and makes it feel occupied rather than echoing.

The choice depends on what the room is for and what is wrong with it, and Part Three gives
you the whole decision. What I want from you here is smaller. I want you to go into the room
you like least in your home, lie on the floor, and look up for thirty seconds.

Most people discover two things. The ceiling is not actually white any more — it has gone
cream, or grey, or yellow near the light fitting. And there is more of it than they
remembered.

HOW TO LOOK
The three questions
-------------------

You now know the four errors by name. This is how you find which of them are in your room.

Do not do this from the doorway. Sit down in the chair you actually use, at the time of day
you actually use the room, with the lights as you actually have them. A room that is being
inspected is not the room you live in.

   1. Where is the light coming from, and how many places is it coming from? Count the
      sources that are switched on. Then look at one solid object — a jug, a lamp base, the
      arm of a chair — and find its shadow. If it does not have one, the room has a single
      source and you have error one.
   2. Where is the highest thing on the wall, and where is my eye? Sit still and look
      straight ahead without tilting your head. Note where your gaze lands on the far wall.
      Now look at the top edge of the highest picture, mirror or shelf. If everything on
      that wall sits below where you were already looking, you have error two.
   3. How many different materials can I count, and how many families do they belong to? Not
      how many objects — how many kinds of surface. Woven, wooden, ceramic or stone, living.
      Fewer than three families is error three.

Then, last, look up. If you cannot remember making a decision about that surface, you have
error four, and you have almost certainly got it, because almost everyone has.

  [ WHAT YOU ARE LIKELY TO FIND ]
Three of the four. That is the ordinary result and it is not a disaster — it is why the room
has been resisting you. Four separate problems, none of which is fixable by buying a
cushion, and nobody ever told you their names.


DO NOT FIX ANYTHING YET

Write down what you found and put the pencil down. The order in which these get corrected
matters a great deal, and doing them in the wrong order is how people spend money twice.
That is the subject of page 15 and it is the most expensive page in this book to ignore.

The page opposite is the audit written out properly, with room to fill in. Photocopy it, or
print that page on its own, and keep a filled copy for every room in the house.

Why your room photographs badly when it looks fine to you
---------------------------------------------------------

Nearly everyone has had this experience, and almost nobody knows what it means.

You stand in the room and it is perfectly pleasant. You take a photograph of it to send to
somebody and the photograph is dreadful. The proportions look wrong, the colours look muddy,
the whole thing looks cheaper than it does in life. So you conclude that you are bad at
photography.

You are not. The camera is telling you the truth and your eye has been protecting you from
it.

Your eye is not a camera. It moves constantly, it adjusts its exposure locally as it moves,
and — this is the important part — your brain edits out what it already knows is there. You
have not properly seen the cable behind the television for two years. Your visual system has
decided it is not information and it stopped sending it to you. A camera has no such filter.
It flattens the room to one plane, fixes one exposure for the whole frame, and reports back
every single thing in it.

So the photograph is a free diagnosis. Use it that way.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | HOW TO USE IT
  | Photograph the room, then look at the photograph rather than the room. The first three
  | things that annoy you in the picture are the three errors you have stopped being able to
  | see.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


TWO REFINEMENTS THAT MAKE IT SHARPER

    - Turn the photograph upside down. This is an old drawing-class trick and it works for
      exactly the same reason: inverted, the image stops being a room you recognise and
      becomes a pattern of shapes and tones. Errors of proportion and balance leap out. It
      is startling how well this works on a wall of pictures.
    - Turn it black and white. With colour removed, what is left is the distribution of
      light and dark, and light and dark is what gives a room depth. A photograph that goes
      to uniform mid-grey is a photograph of a room lit from one height.

Keep those photographs. When you have finished correcting the room, take the same photograph
from the same spot at the same hour. It is the only honest before-and-after there is, and it
will show you things you did not know you had fixed.

THE MOST EXPENSIVE PAGE TO IGNORE
The order of operations
-----------------------

Corrections have to be made in a particular order, because each one changes the answer to
the next.

This is the page that saves you money. Not because anything on it is difficult, but because
the natural order — the order in which people actually do it — is almost exactly backwards,
and doing it backwards means paying twice.

Here is the order. It goes from the thing that nothing depends on, to the thing that depends
on everything.

   1. Clear. Take out of the room everything that is broken, everything you do not like, and
      everything that is there because it has always been there. Do not replace any of it
      yet. An emptier room is easier to read, and a surprising number of rooms are
      substantially fixed at this stage. It costs nothing.
   2. Light. Lamps, bulbs, heights, and what you can stop blocking. This comes before colour
      and it is not negotiable, for the reason on the next page. It is usually the cheapest
      real change in the room and almost always the largest.
   3. Height. Curtain pole up, pictures to 145, shelves and mirrors adjusted. Nail holes and
      an afternoon. Do this before you paint, because you will be filling holes anyway and
      because the new heights change where the eye goes.
   4. Texture. The missing families. Fabric, wood, clay, something living. Usually one
      object, usually cheap, and it is what makes a corrected room feel lived in rather than
      merely tidy.
   5. Colour. Walls, ceiling, woodwork. Last, always — and only now, when you know what
      light the room will actually be living in and what else is already in it. By the time
      you arrive here, a surprising number of rooms no longer need painting at all.

  [ THE EXPENSIVE MISTAKE ]
Buying furniture at any point in this list. Furniture is not a stage. It comes after all
five, if it comes at all, and most of the rooms I have corrected needed none.


[ page 16 ]

Why colour comes fourth and not first
-------------------------------------

Everybody starts with paint. It is the wrong place to start, and here is the reason in one
sentence: a colour is not a property of the paint. It is what happens when that paint meets
the light in your room.

Change the light and you have changed the colour. Not your perception of it — the actual
wavelengths coming off that wall into your eye. The same tin of paint on a north-facing wall
and a south-facing wall is two different colours, and anybody who has painted both will tell
you so.

So when you choose a colour before you have settled the light, you are answering a question
whose terms have not been set yet. Then you fix the lighting afterwards, and the colour you
chose turns out to be wrong, and you paint again. I have watched people do this three times
in the same room.


THE TWO THINGS THAT DECIDE A COLOUR BEFORE YOU HAVE CHOSEN IT

    - Which way the window faces. North light in the northern hemisphere never carries
      direct sun; it is cool and steady all day, and it will push a warm grey towards blue.
      South light is warm and strong and will push a cool white towards yellow. This is the
      first page of Part Two and it is the single most useful fact in the book.
    - What your bulbs are. A bulb has a colour temperature printed on the box and most
      people have never looked at it. Twenty-seven hundred kelvin is warm domestic light;
      four to five thousand is the light shops and offices are lit with. Put a colour chosen
      under one of those under the other and it is unrecognisable.

  [ FROM THE NOTEBOOK ]
“You are not choosing a colour. You are choosing what the light will do to it.” — my father
was a house painter and he mixed his own colours, and this is the only thing he ever said
about paint that I have never had cause to revise.

There is one more reason to leave paint until fourth, and it is the practical one. Paint is
the most disruptive and least reversible thing on the list. You have to empty the room,
cover the floor, and lose two days. Lamps take twenty minutes and can be undone. Curtain
poles take an afternoon. It is simply better sense to exhaust the cheap reversible moves
first — and a great many rooms never need the paint at all once the first three stages are
done.

habit, and the tape costs less than the filler you would otherwise need.

What this book will not do
--------------------------

I would rather tell you now than have you find out two hundred pages from here.


IT WILL NOT MAKE A SMALL FLAT INTO A LARGE ONE

There are real techniques in here for making a small room read as larger and more
deliberate, and they work. None of them adds a square metre. If your flat is too small for
your life, that is a different problem and no book solves it.


IT WILL NOT HELP YOU WITH DAMP, MOULD, LEAD OR ASBESTOS

These come up in old buildings and they are not decorating problems. Damp has a cause and
until the cause is found nothing you put on that wall will stay there. Paint in buildings
from before the late 1970s may contain lead, and disturbing it by sanding is the dangerous
part. Some materials in older buildings contain asbestos and must not be drilled or cut. If
you suspect any of these, the correct next step is a surveyor or a licensed specialist, not
a paintbrush. I will say so again each time it comes up.


IT WILL NOT DO ELECTRICAL, GAS, PLUMBING OR STRUCTURAL WORK

Nothing in this book asks you to. Where a correction would — moving a ceiling fitting,
adding a socket, taking out anything that holds the building up — you are told to stop and
call somebody licensed. There is more good work available to you without touching any of
that than you will get through in a year.


IT CANNOT GET YOU PERMISSION

If you rent, some of this is not available to you, and Part Six is written entirely for that
situation — every correction in it needs no drill, no paint and nobody’s consent. If your
building is listed or protected, check before you change a window, a door or anything on the
outside.


IT IS NOT AGAINST SPENDING MONEY

This is the one people get wrong about me. I am against wasted money, which is a completely
different position. There are things in this book I will tell you to spend on, because the
cheap version is a false economy and you will buy it twice:

    - A good lamp. It will outlive the room.
    - Lined curtains, in enough fabric. The single largest visible difference money makes.
    - A sofa whose cover can be taken off, or which can be reupholstered.
    - Decent bulbs, which cost very little and change everything.

Everything else in the ordinary decorating budget is negotiable, and a good deal of it is
listed on page 189 under the heading of what not to buy.

SIX THINGS YOU HAVE BEEN TOLD
That are wrong — part one
-------------------------


ONE · “PAINT A SMALL ROOM WHITE TO MAKE IT LOOK BIGGER”

This is the most repeated piece of advice in the subject and it is the one I disagree with
most sharply. A small room painted flat white does not read as bigger. It reads as small and
unfinished, because white shows you exactly where the walls are and there is nothing else
for the eye to do. Meanwhile every mark, every radiator, every cable is on full display
against it.

A small room painted a deep, quiet colour reads as deliberate. The corners soften, the walls
stop announcing their distance, and the room feels like a decision rather than a shortage.
This is my position and not a physical law, so I have marked it as mine — but Part Three
gives you the whole argument and the cases where white genuinely is the right answer.


TWO · “GET THE BIGGEST LIGHT YOU CAN FOR A DARK ROOM”

A dark room is rarely short of lumens. It is short of directions. One very bright ceiling
fitting in a dark room produces a bright ceiling and a dim room, because all it can do is
illuminate the plane it is attached to and the tops of things. Three modest lamps at three
heights will make that room feel twice as bright as one powerful pendant, using less
electricity.


THREE · “PUSH THE FURNITURE AGAINST THE WALLS TO MAKE SPACE”

In a small room this is often exactly wrong. Furniture flat against every wall leaves a void
in the middle, and a void does not read as space — it reads as a waiting room. Pulling a
sofa five or ten centimetres off the wall, and letting one chair sit at an angle, gives the
room an inside. The floor you lose is floor nobody was standing on.

  [ WHY THESE PERSIST ]
Not one of the three is a conspiracy. They are all true of showrooms, which are lit from
above, painted pale so the stock reads, and arranged around a central aisle. Advice written
for a shop floor got repeated until it sounded like advice for a home.


[ page 20 ]

That are wrong — part two
-------------------------


FOUR · “AN ACCENT WALL WILL TRANSFORM THE ROOM”

One wall in a different colour does not transform a room. It divides it. The eye reads the
coloured wall as nearer than the others, so a room with one dark wall and three pale ones
feels shorter in that direction, not deeper. If the wall you chose is the one with the
window in it, you have also just put your only source of light in front of your darkest
surface, which is the least useful arrangement available.

There is a version of this that works, and it is not an accent wall — it is taking one
colour all the way round the room and onto the ceiling, so the room reads as a single
volume. Page 62.


FIVE · “MIRRORS DOUBLE THE LIGHT”

A mirror reflects what is in front of it. That is the whole mechanism. A mirror opposite a
window reflects the window and genuinely helps, because it puts a second bright rectangle
into the room. A mirror opposite a blank wall reflects a blank wall, adds nothing, and has
cost you the wall it is hanging on. Before you hang a mirror, stand where it will go and
look at what you would be looking at.


SIX · “YOU NEED A FEATURE, A FOCAL POINT, A STATEMENT PIECE”

A room needs somewhere for the eye to rest, which is not the same thing and is usually
cheaper. A window with the curtains hung properly is a focal point. A fireplace is a focal
point. A bookcase with actual books in it is a focal point. The word statement is doing
commercial work in that sentence, and the thing being stated is usually a price.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | CARRY THIS ONE FORWARD
  | Almost every piece of decorating advice you have absorbed was written to sell something.
  | Ask what each rule would have you buy, and a surprising number of them dissolve.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

That is the end of Part One. You know the four errors, you know how to find them, and you
know the order in which to correct them. Everything after this is detail, and the detail
starts where it has to start.

CHAPTER TWO
Why light comes first
---------------------

You cannot see anything in a room except light. Everything else you think you are looking at
is light coming off a surface.

That sounds like a philosophical remark and it is in fact a completely practical one. The
colour of your wall is light. The texture of your sofa is light — specifically, it is the
pattern of tiny highlights and shadows across a weave, which is why velvet and linen look
different even in the same colour. The apparent size of the room is light, because your eye
judges depth largely from how brightness falls off across a surface.

Change the light and you have changed all three. Nothing else in decorating has that reach.

Which is why it is first, and why the money goes furthest here. Three good bulbs and a lamp
you already own will do more for a room than a new rug at ten times the price. I have seen
it happen in an afternoon more times than I can count, and it still surprises the person who
lives there.


THE TWO HALVES OF THE PROBLEM

A room has daylight, which you did not choose and cannot change, and it has electric light,
which you chose without thinking about it and can change tonight. They have to be handled
differently.

    - Daylight is a fixed fact of the building. What you can do is find out exactly what you
      have been given, stop wasting it, and choose colours that work with it rather than
      against it. That is the first six pages of this part.
    - Electric light is entirely yours. Number of sources, their heights, the colour of the
      bulbs, whether they dim, what the shades do. Almost every error in the fourth error
      list lives here, and almost every one is correctable for under the price of a takeaway
      meal.

  [ WHY I KNOW THIS ]
The building I grew up in was put up for silk looms, and the looms needed north light —
light with no direct sun in it, that does not move across the day and does not glare, so a
weaver could match a thread at nine in the morning and at four in the afternoon and get the
same answer. Two hundred years later I am telling you the same thing about your sitting
room. The weavers were right.

So we start with the window, because the window sets the terms for everything else.


[ page 23 ]

Which way does your window face?
--------------------------------

If you take one thing from this book, take this. Almost nobody knows the answer for their
own home, and every colour decision depends on it.


HOW TO FIND OUT, THREE WAYS

   1. The compass on your phone. Stand at the window looking out. The bearing it shows is
      the direction the window faces. Thirty seconds, and it is the only thing your phone is
      better at than a tape measure.
   2. Midday. In the northern hemisphere the sun is due south in the middle of the day.
      Stand at the window at noon. If the sun is in front of you, the window faces roughly
      south. Behind you, roughly north.
   3. Watch it for a day. Direct sun in the early morning means east. Direct sun in the
      evening means west. Direct sun in the middle of the day means south. No direct sun at
      all, ever, at any hour, in any season — north.

Everything in this part and in Part Three assumes the northern hemisphere. If you are south
of the equator, swap north and south throughout and leave east and west as they are.

Write the answer down for every room in the house. Put it on the audit sheet. You will use
it on the paint page, on the bulb page, on the curtain page, and every time you are standing
in a shop wondering whether a colour will work.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | COLETTE’S FIRST LAW
  | Light before colour. You cannot choose a colour until you know which way the window
  | faces.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


[ page 24 ]

NORTH-FACING
Cool, steady, and unfairly maligned
-----------------------------------

A north-facing room receives no direct sunlight at any point in the day or the year. Estate
agents treat this as a defect. Painters and weavers have always treated it as the best light
there is.

Both are right about different things. What north light is, precisely, is light from the
whole dome of the sky rather than from the sun. That gives it two properties, and
understanding them is the whole of managing a north room.

It does not move. There is no patch of sun crossing the floor, no hour when the room
transforms, no glare. The light at nine is the light at four. This is why every proper
painter's studio and every weaver's atelier in Europe faces north, and why my grandmother
would only match a thread at a north window.

It is cool. Skylight is blue-biased, so everything in a north room is being lit by very
slightly blue light, all day, forever.


WHAT THIS DOES TO YOUR COLOURS

It pushes them cold. A warm grey turns blue. A greige goes grey. A white with any blue in it
goes clinical — and a great many fashionable whites have blue in them, which is exactly why
they look so unpleasant in north rooms and so crisp in the showroom photograph that was
taken in a south room.


WHAT TO DO ABOUT IT

    - Choose colours with warmth in them, and more warmth than you think. A colour that
      looks slightly too yellow or too pink on the chip will look correct on the wall. Part
      Three gives you the specific whites.
    - Do not fight it with brightness. Adding more cool light to a cool room makes it
      colder. Add warmth instead: 2700 K bulbs, and lamps rather than the ceiling.
    - Lean into it. A north room will never be a sunny room, and a north room pretending to
      be sunny is the saddest thing in decorating. Deeper, warmer, quieter colours make it
      feel intentional. This is the room to be brave in.
    - Use the steadiness. It is the one room in the house where a colour will look the same
      in the morning and the evening. If you have art you care about, or you sew, or you
      paint, this is where it goes.

  [ THE TEST ]
Hold a sheet of plain white paper up in your north room at midday, then carry it to a south-
facing window. The same sheet of paper. The difference startles people — and it is applied
to every surface in both rooms, permanently.


[ page 25 ]

SOUTH-FACING
Warm, strong, and harder to handle than people expect
-----------------------------------------------------

A south-facing room gets direct sun for most of the day. Everyone wants one. Nobody warns
you that it is the most difficult light to decorate for.

The difficulty is that it changes. Direct sunlight is enormously stronger than skylight, and
it arrives, crosses the room, and leaves. So a south room is really three rooms: a bright
one in the morning, a blazing one at midday, and a normal one after the sun has moved past.
A colour that is handsome at ten can be bleached to nothing at two.

It is also warm. Direct sun is yellow-biased, which means a south room flatters warm colours
and can turn a cool colour muddy. And in summer it is a heat problem as much as a light
problem.


WHAT TO DO ABOUT IT

    - You can afford cool colours here, and you generally should. This is the room where a
      grey-green, a slate blue, or a proper cool white works — the sun supplies the warmth
      so the paint does not have to. In a south room, warm colours on top of warm light can
      tip into orange.
    - Test at the worst hour, not the best. Judge a colour in a south room at full midday
      sun. If it survives that, it will be fine the rest of the day. Judge it at six in the
      evening and you will choose wrong.
    - Give yourself a way to soften the sun. A sheer panel behind the main curtain, or a
      blind you can drop at midday, turns direct sun into diffused light without turning the
      room dark. This is what shutters do in Mediterranean buildings, and it is the single
      most useful thing you can add to a south room.
    - Expect fading, and plan for it. Strong sun over years will lighten textiles, paper and
      some woods. Rotate cushions. Do not put a watercolour you love on the wall the sun
      crosses. This is not pessimism, it is just what ultraviolet does.

  [ THE ONE THAT CATCHES PEOPLE ]
Gloss and satin finishes in a south room. Direct sun across a shiny wall shows every roller
mark, every filled hole, every unevenness in the plaster, in a way that no other light does.
In a strong south room, keep walls matte and save the sheen for woodwork.


[ page 26 ]

EAST AND WEST-FACING
The rooms with two personalities
--------------------------------

An east or west room has direct sun for part of the day and skylight for the rest. So it has
two characters, and the decision you have to make is which one you are decorating for.

That decision is not a matter of taste. It is a matter of when you are in the room.


EAST

Direct sun from sunrise until late morning, then cool skylight for the rest of the day. The
sun is low and the light is clear and slightly golden, and then at some point mid-morning it
simply goes, and from then on the room behaves like a north room.

Decorate it for the afternoon, because the afternoon is most of the room's life. Which means
treating it much like a north room: warmth in the colours, 2700 K bulbs, lamps for the
evening. The morning sun will be a gift on top of that rather than something you have to
accommodate.

Best use: kitchens and breakfast rooms, and bedrooms for people who like to wake with the
light.


WEST

Cool skylight all morning, then direct sun from mid-afternoon until sunset — and evening sun
is the warmest, most orange light of the day, arriving at a very low angle, straight into
the room and straight into your eyes.

Decorate it for the evening if that is when you use it, which for a sitting room it usually
is. West rooms can carry cooler colours than you would expect, because that evening light is
so warm it will pull everything back. But you will need something to cut the glare — a
sheer, a blind, a shutter.

Best use: sitting rooms and dining rooms, where the golden hour lands exactly when you want
it.


IF YOUR ROOM HAS WINDOWS ON TWO SIDES

Then you are fortunate and you have a slightly more interesting problem. Cross-lit rooms are
the easiest rooms in the world to make look good, because everything in them has a lit side
and a shaded side without you doing anything at all — the two errors of flatness and of
single direction are solved by the architecture.

Work out which window is dominant, by sitting in the room at the hour you use it and seeing
which one is actually doing the lighting. Decorate for that one. Treat the second window as
modelling light and do not block it, ever, with a piece of furniture or a plant.

ELECTRIC LIGHT
The three heights
-----------------

Here is the rule stated plainly, and then the reason, and then the numbers.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | COLETTE’S RULE OF THE THREE HEIGHTS
  | Light must arrive at three heights — overhead, table, and floor. A room lit from one
  | height is a room lit like a corridor.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

This is mine and I will not dress it up as physics. But the reason underneath it is physics,
and the reason is modelling. An object looks solid when one side of it is lit and another is
not. That is the only cue your eye has for three-dimensionality in a still scene, and it
requires light to arrive from more than one direction.

Put light at three different heights and every object in the room is caught from three
angles. The jug has a bright side, a mid side and a shadow. The sofa arm has a top, a face
and an underside. The room acquires depth, which is the quality people are describing when
they say a room feels “warm” or “inviting” and cannot say why.


THE THREE, AND WHAT EACH ONE IS FOR

    HEIGHT                                     | ROUGHLY              | ITS JOB
    ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    Overhead                                   | ceiling              | General fill. The le
    Table                                      | 100–150 cm           | The workhorse. Sits
    Floor                                      | below 60 cm          | Depth and atmosphere

Three sources, three heights, three switches. If you do nothing else in this book, do this.


[ page 29 ]

HEIGHT ONE
Overhead, and what it is good for
---------------------------------

I have spent a page telling you that the ceiling light is the problem. Now let me be fair to
it, because there are three jobs it does that nothing else can.

    - Finding things. You want a lot of even light when you are looking for a dropped
      earring or cleaning the room. That is a task, it lasts four minutes, and it is exactly
      what a ceiling fitting is for.
    - Safety. Stairs, thresholds, anywhere someone might trip. Overhead light shows the edge
      of a step better than a lamp does.
    - Work. A kitchen counter and a bathroom mirror need light from above and in front. In a
      kitchen this is not a compromise, it is correct.

What the ceiling light is not for is being the light you sit in. That is the whole error. It
is a tool, not a setting, and the mistake is leaving the tool switched on all evening.


IF YOU ARE STUCK WITH A SINGLE CENTRAL FITTING

Most rented flats have exactly one, in the middle of the ceiling, and you cannot move it
without an electrician. Three things you can do without touching the wiring:

   1. Change the shade so it throws light sideways and up, not straight down. An open-topped
      drum or a fabric shade will bounce a good deal of its output off the ceiling, and
      light that has bounced off a surface is soft, directionless and much kinder than light
      straight from a bulb. This turns your worst fitting into something closer to indirect
      light, for the price of a shade.
   2. Reduce it and warm it. Put in a lower-output 2700 K bulb than you think you need. You
      are no longer asking it to light the room; the lamps are doing that.
   3. Put it on a dimmer if the fitting and bulb allow. See page 37 — and check that the
      bulb says dimmable, because most do not.

  [ NEVER ]
A bare bulb in a ceiling fitting, in a room anybody sits in. It is the single harshest light
source available in a home: a small, very bright point directly overhead, producing hard
shadows in every eye socket in the room. If a shade is missing, a paper lantern costs almost
nothing and is a real improvement.

One honest exception: a beautiful pendant, hung low over a dining table, is one of the great
pleasures of a room. But look at what it is doing — hung that low it is no longer overhead
light, it is table-height light with a cable. The height is the point.


[ page 30 ]

HEIGHT TWO
Table height — the one that does the work
-----------------------------------------

If you may only have one lamp, it goes here. This is the height that makes a room feel like
somewhere people live.

The reason is that it is at the height of a seated person's eye. Light arriving from roughly
your own eye level does two things at once: it lights faces from the front rather than the
top, which is enormously more flattering, and it puts a bright object in your field of view
without glaring, which reads as warmth.


THE NUMBER THAT MATTERS

    Bottom of the shade, above the floor, for a lamp beside a chair or sofa
                                                                       100–115 cm · 40–45 in
    Which for a table at typical side-table height means a lamp of about
                                                                         45–60 cm · 18–24 in
    Bottom of the shade, for a lamp on a desk or console you stand at
                                                                       125–135 cm · 49–53 in
This is why so many lamps are the wrong size — not the wrong style, the wrong size. A lamp
is chosen in a shop, at standing height, on a display plinth. Then it goes onto a low side
table beside a low sofa, and the shade ends up at chest height, and the room is lit from the
chest up only.

  [ HOW TO CHECK A LAMP BEFORE YOU BUY IT ]
Take two numbers with you: the height of the table it will stand on, and your own seated eye
height (sit in a dining chair and have somebody measure from the floor to your eye — for
most adults it lands between 110 and 125 cm). Table height plus lamp height to the bottom of
the shade should land in that range. This is the only measurement anybody needs to buy a
lamp, and no shop displays it.


WHERE THEY GO

Not side by side. Two lamps at table height on the same wall light one half of a room twice.
Put them diagonally opposite, or at least on two different walls, so their light crosses.
Two lamps on two walls is a lit room; two lamps on one wall is a lit wall.


[ page 31 ]

HEIGHT THREE
Floor level — the one nobody does
---------------------------------

This is the height that almost no ordinary home has, and it is the one that makes a room
look like a photograph.

Think about where the light is in your room. It is all in the top half. Ceiling fitting at
two and a half metres, perhaps a lamp at a metre. Everything below knee height is lit only
by whatever falls down there, which is very little, because light falls off quickly with
distance — double the distance from a source and you have a quarter of the light.

So the bottom third of the room sits in gloom, and because the bottom third of the room is
where the floor is, the room reads as sitting in a dark pool. Adding a single low source
transforms this, and it costs almost nothing because the fitting can be very modest.


FIVE WAYS TO DO IT, CHEAPEST FIRST

   1. A small lamp on the floor behind a chair or a plant. Aimed up a wall or into the
      leaves. You cannot see the source, only the glow. This is the cheapest genuine
      transformation in the whole of this book — any small lamp you already own, put on the
      floor.
   2. A lamp on the lowest surface in the room. A hearth, a stack of books, a low stool, the
      bottom shelf of a bookcase.
   3. An uplighter in a corner. A canister that sits on the floor and throws light up the
      two walls of a corner. The corner glows, the ceiling picks it up, and the room gains a
      soft general light with no visible source.
   4. Candles. Not a joke and not a decoration. A candle is a light source at table or floor
      height with the warmest colour temperature in the house, and four of them grouped is a
      real light. Never left unattended, never near a curtain.
   5. Lit shelving. A strip tucked under a shelf, invisible, washing down the books below.
      The most expensive option here and the most permanent.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | WHY IT WORKS
  | Light from below reverses every shadow in the room. Nothing else you can do in twenty
  | minutes changes a room this much.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

One caution, since the effect is strong: low light aimed at a face is theatrical and
unflattering — it is how a horror film is lit. Aim it at a wall, a corner, a plant or a
bookshelf, never across the seating.

How many lamps, and where they go
---------------------------------

More than you have. Almost certainly more than you think is reasonable.


THE COUNT

    Minimum separate light sources in any room anybody sits in ........................... 3
    A sitting room, comfortably ........................................................ 4–6
    Of which on the ceiling .......................................... 1, off in the evening
    Of which below 60 cm ........................................................ at least 1

WHERE THEY GO — THE FOUR RULES

   1. Spread them around the perimeter, not the middle. Light on the walls makes a room feel
      larger, because the walls are what define its size and a lit wall reads as further
      away. Light in the middle does the opposite.
   2. Get one into every corner you can. Dark corners make a room feel smaller and slightly
      unresolved. Not every corner — one should stay dark, see page 102 — but the others
      want something.
   3. Put one where you sit and one where you look. A lamp beside your chair for reading,
      and a lamp at the far end of the room so there is something lit to look towards. A
      room where all the light is behind you feels like a cinema.
   4. Never point one at the television. Or at a mirror, or at any glass, from opposite. You
      will spend the evening looking at the reflection of a bulb.

  [ THE SWITCH PROBLEM, WHICH NOBODY MENTIONS ]
Five lamps means five switches in five awkward places, and a system you have to walk around
the room to operate will be abandoned within a fortnight. Solve it before you build it: put
lamps on a single switched extension where you can, or use plug adaptors you can control
together. A lighting scheme that takes one action to turn on is a lighting scheme you will
actually use, and that matters more than any individual fitting.

Seventy-five to ninety centimetres above the tabletop looks far too low when the room is
empty. It is correct when people are sitting down — it lights the food and the faces and not
the ceiling, and you can see across the table underneath it. Hang it, then sit down before
you judge it. Everyone who raises it wishes they had not.

THE NUMBER ON THE BOX
Colour temperature
------------------

There is a number on every bulb box that decides more about how your home feels than the
price of your furniture, and most people have never read it.

It is printed in kelvin, with a capital K, and it describes the colour of the light rather
than how much of it there is. The scale is confusing because it runs backwards from how you
would expect: a low number is warm and orange, a high number is cool and blue.


THE WHOLE RECOMMENDATION

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | COLETTE’S RULE OF THE WARM BULB
  | Twenty-seven hundred kelvin, or nothing. Everything above three thousand is shop
  | lighting, and a shop wants you to look at objects, not live among them.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

Two thousand seven hundred kelvin is the colour of the old incandescent bulbs everybody grew
up with, and it is the colour every human being alive associates with evening and with being
at home. It is warm without being orange. It flatters skin. It makes wood look like wood.

Three thousand is acceptable and very slightly cooler; some people prefer it in a kitchen or
bathroom. Four thousand belongs in a garage or a shop. Five thousand and up is sold as
“daylight” and has no business in a room where anyone relaxes, because it tells the human
body it is the middle of the day.


THE OTHER NUMBER WORTH FINDING

CRI, the colour rendering index, on a scale to one hundred. It measures how faithfully a
light source shows colours compared with a reference light. A cheap bulb may sit around
eighty, and under it your red cushion is a slightly brown cushion and your skin looks grey.
Ninety or above is what you want, and it is often printed very small or as “CRI 90+”. It
costs a little more and it is the difference between your colours working and not working
after dark.


[ page 35 ]

THE QUIET PART
Why every shop you buy from is lit wrong
----------------------------------------

Walk into any large homeware shop and look up. The light is cool, it is bright, and there is
a great deal of it, coming from directly overhead across the whole ceiling.

Every single thing about that is the opposite of what this chapter has told you to do. And
it is not a mistake. It is correct — for a shop.

Cool bright overhead light makes goods read clearly. It shows edges and colours at a glance,
it reaches into every aisle without shadows anyone could trip in, it keeps people alert and
moving, and it is cheap to run and easy to maintain at scale. A shop is a place where
objects are examined. It is lit for examining objects.

Your sitting room is not a place where objects are examined. But here is the consequence,
and it is worth sitting with for a moment:

Every purchase you have ever made for your home, you made under light that your home will
never have.

You chose the grey of that sofa at four thousand kelvin from above. You will live with it at
two thousand seven hundred from the side. Those are genuinely different colours. This is the
real reason the cushion looked one way in the shop and another way at home, and why people
assume they have been somehow deceived. Nobody deceived you. Two different lights.


AND THE ECONOMICS UNDERNEATH IT

Nobody decided this in a room somewhere. It is quieter and sadder than that.

There is no money in telling you the answer is a two-dollar bulb. There is a great deal of
money in a shop floor arranged so that the object looks its absolute best under lighting
conditions that you will never reproduce, and in the next purchase you make when the first
one disappoints you at home. Nobody has to intend that for it to be how the whole thing
works.

So the fix is not a boycott, it is a habit:

    - Never make a final colour decision in a shop. Bring the sample home.
    - Judge fabric and paint in the room they are going into, at the hour you use it, with
      your own lamps on.
    - Change your bulbs before you change anything else, so that the light you are judging
      in is the light you will keep.

What to actually buy
--------------------

This page is the cheapest correction in the book. An afternoon, a handful of bulbs, and the
light in your home stops being wrong.


THE SPECIFICATION, EVERY TIME

    - 2700 K. Sometimes sold as “warm white”, but the words on the front vary between brands
      and the number does not. Find the number.
    - CRI 90 or above if it is stated. If nothing is stated, assume it is not.
    - Dimmable, if there is any chance the fitting is ever on a dimmer. It must say so
      explicitly.
    - The output you want, in lumens — not watts. Watts describe how much electricity it
      uses, which used to be a rough proxy for brightness and is not any more.


LUMENS, ROUGHLY

    FOR                                        | LUMENS               | OLD MONEY

    A table lamp doing real work               | 400–700              | 40–60 W

Under a fabric shade, buy the cheapest thing that meets the specification above — nobody

  [ DO IT IN ONE GO ]
Walk round the house with a bag and count every socket, including the ones in fittings you
never use. Buy them all at once, in the same specification, and change them in one
afternoon. Mixed colour temperatures in one room is worse than either temperature alone — it
is the only genuinely unfixable lighting error, because the eye cannot settle on a white
point. If you do nothing else: make every bulb in a single room match.


[ page 37 ]

Dimming, which matters more than brightness
-------------------------------------------

A room needs different amounts of light at four in the afternoon and eleven at night. A room
with one setting is wrong twice a day.

This is the part of lighting that people skip because it sounds like a luxury, and it is
closer to a necessity. Your eye adapts: at eleven at night, with your pupils wide, the light
you wanted at four in the afternoon is uncomfortably bright. And the amount of light in a
room is one of the strongest signals your body uses to decide whether it is time to wind
down.


FOUR WAYS, IN ORDER OF COST

   1. Separate switches. The cheapest dimmer in the world is having five lamps on five plugs
      and turning two of them off. No wiring, no cost, and it is genuinely most of the
      benefit. Two lamps at eleven at night is a different room from five.
   2. An in-line dimmer on a lamp flex. A small wheel that sits on the cable. Inexpensive,
      plugs in, nothing to install. The bulb must say dimmable.
   3. A plug-in dimmer or a smart plug. Sits between the plug and the socket. Works with a
      remote or a phone. More expensive, no tools, and it lets you dim several lamps
      together.
   4. A wall dimmer. Replaces the light switch. This is electrical work behind a faceplate
      and it needs an electrician — and if you rent, it needs permission. Do not do this one
      yourself.

  [ THE DIMMABLE LED PROBLEM, WHICH CATCHES NEARLY EVERYONE ]
Most LED bulbs are not dimmable, and putting a non-dimmable LED on a dimmer gives you
flicker, buzzing, a bulb that cuts out before the bottom of the range, or a shortened life.
It has to say dimmable on the box. And even dimmable LEDs vary in how well they behave with
any given dimmer, so if you are fitting several, buy one and try it before buying ten.


ONE THING BETTER THAN A DIMMER

A dimmed bulb gets dimmer but it does not get much warmer. Real evening light — a fire, a
candle, a setting sun — gets warmer as it gets lower, and that is the quality a dimmer
cannot reproduce on its own.

Which is why a candle or two, at the point in the evening when you have dimmed everything
else, does something a dimmer cannot. It is not decoration. It is the only source in the
house at around two thousand kelvin, and it is the reason a room with candles in it feels
late in a way that a dimmed room does not.

[ page 39 ]

The room that gets no sun
-------------------------

A basement flat. A ground-floor room with a wall two metres away. A north room on a dark
street. I have worked in a great many of these and I am not going to pretend they are the
same as a room with a view.

Here is the honest position, and then what genuinely helps.

The honest position: a room with no useful daylight cannot be made to feel like a room with
daylight. Every technique for “brightening” such a room is really a technique for making it
feel intentional instead of deficient. That is achievable, and it is a different goal from
the one the magazines imply.


WHAT DOES NOT WORK, WHICH IS MOST OF THE USUAL ADVICE

    - Brilliant white. The most common advice and the worst. White needs light to be white;
      without it, white goes grey and dead. You have painted the room the colour of the
      problem.
    - More lumens. A dark room flooded with cool light becomes a lit dark room — gloomy and
      glaring at once.
    - A mirror on a blank wall. It reflects the blank wall. Page 20.


WHAT WORKS

   1. Stop trying to be bright and commit to being warm. This is the whole strategy. A deep,
      warm, enveloping colour in a sunless room reads as a decision — a study, a snug, a
      dining room for the evening. The same room in pale grey reads as a mistake. Deep
      green, warm brown-red, ink blue, a proper dark plaster colour. Be braver than feels
      sensible.
   2. Four or five small warm sources, none overhead, with one dark corner left alone. You
      are building an evening room, so light it like an evening.
   3. Every texture you can find. Texture is what gives a dark room something to do — light
      catching a coarse weave, a worn timber, a matte glaze. This is the room for the
      roughest, most tactile things you own.
   4. Something living, realistically. Choose the tolerant plants and accept slow growth, or
      use branches in water.
   5. Take back whatever daylight exists. Page 41. Here every centimetre of glass matters,
      and it is usually half blocked.

  [ FROM THE NOTEBOOK ]
My grandmother made curtains for a dressmaker whose courtyard workroom never saw the sun.
She lined its walls in a dark red and put six lamps in it. Everyone who came in said it was
the loveliest room in the building. It was the darkest.


[ page 40 ]

Corridors, landings and internal rooms
--------------------------------------

Nobody decorates a corridor and everybody walks through one several times an hour. It is the
most neglected surface area in a home.

A corridor has a particular problem: it is long, narrow, usually windowless, and lit by one
or two ceiling fittings that produce bright patches with gloom between them. So you walk
down a tunnel of alternating light and dark, and the corridor feels longer and meaner than
it is.


THE CORRECTION

    - Light the walls, not the floor. Two or three wall lights along one side, centred
      around 150 to 165 cm, will do more than any number of ceiling fittings. Lit walls make
      a narrow space feel wider; a lit floor makes it feel like a route.
    - If you cannot add wiring, use the surfaces. A small lamp on a narrow console, or a
      shelf at the far end with a lamp on it, gives you a lit destination. A corridor with
      something lit at the end of it stops being a tunnel.
    - Hang things at the right height, all the way along. A corridor is the easiest place in
      the house to hang pictures, because you walk past rather than sitting, so the standard
      145 cm centre is exactly right. A run of small pictures at a consistent height turns
      dead space into the best wall in the flat.
    - Do not paint it the same white as everything else. A corridor is a transition and can
      take a stronger colour than a room you sit in, because you are only in it for four
      seconds. It is the cheapest place to be brave, because it is small and nobody lives in
      it.


INTERNAL ROOMS AND WINDOWLESS BATHROOMS

Treat these as the sunless room on page 39, with two additions. First, a windowless bathroom
has a humidity problem as well as a light problem, and the extract fan matters more than
anything decorative — if it does not clear the mirror within a few minutes of a shower, that
is a ventilation job and worth sorting before you paint.

Second, these are the rooms where mirror placement is genuinely powerful, because a mirror
opposite a lit wall or a lamp will double what little light there is and give the eye some
depth. Put the light source and the mirror opposite each other, and stand where you will
stand to check what you will be looking at.

  [ BATHROOMS AND ELECTRICITY ]
Lighting and sockets in a bathroom are governed by specific rules about how close a fitting
may be to water, and those rules exist for an extremely good reason. Anything beyond
changing a bulb in an existing fitting is an electrician's job. There is no version of this
where doing it yourself is sensible.


[ page 41 ]

The daylight you are throwing away
----------------------------------

Before you buy a single lamp, go and get back the light you already have. It is free, it
takes an hour, and almost every home I have been in was wasting between a fifth and a third
of it.


GO AND LOOK AT YOUR WINDOW. ALL OF IT.

   1. What is standing in front of it? A tall plant, a lamp, a drying rack, a chair with
      coats on it, a stack of boxes that has been there since you moved in. Anything in the
      lower third of a window is taking light from the whole room.
   2. Where do the curtains sit when they are open? This is the big one. In most homes the
      open curtains cover fifteen to twenty centimetres of glass down each side, because the
      pole is barely wider than the window. That is a tenth of your window, permanently, in
      every room, for years. The fix is a wider pole — it should extend 15 to 25 cm beyond
      the frame on each side so the curtains stack on the wall rather than on the glass.
   3. Is the blind fully up? A roller blind left a third down is invisible to you after a
      week. Go and look. Roll it right up.
   4. Are the nets or sheers doing anything? Permanent net curtains on a window that nobody
      can see into are costing you light for no reason. If privacy is only needed in the
      evening, a blind you drop is better than a sheer you never move.
   5. Is the glass clean, inside and out? Unglamorous and genuinely significant on a window
      that has not been washed in a year or two, especially on a street.
   6. What colour is the reveal? The little returns of wall at the sides of the window. If
      they are a dark colour, or grubby, they are absorbing light at the exact point it
      enters the room. Painting the reveal the palest thing in the room is a trick worth
      knowing.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | WORTH SAYING ON ITS OWN
  | Most people's curtains, when open, cover a tenth of their window. Nobody notices,
  | because the curtains have always been there.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

Do all six of those before you spend anything. Then reassess the room. A surprising number
of “dark” rooms are ordinary rooms with a blocked window, and you will have spent nothing.

WHAT NOT TO BUY
Lighting
--------

Six things this industry sells constantly that will not solve the problem you bought them
for.


COLOUR-CHANGING SMART BULBS, FOR A SITTING ROOM

The colour-changing is not the problem; the problem is that they are usually bought instead
of a second lamp. A single ceiling fitting that can turn pink is still a single ceiling
fitting. If you want smart bulbs, put them in three lamps at three heights and you have
something genuinely good. One is a novelty.


“DAYLIGHT” BULBS FOR A DARK ROOM

Sold specifically at the problem and it makes it worse. Five or six thousand kelvin in a
room with no daylight gives you a cold blue room. The word on the box is doing the selling.


A BRIGHTER CEILING FITTING

The instinct when a room is gloomy, and it is treating a direction problem as a quantity
problem. You will get a bright ceiling and the same gloomy room.


SPOTLIGHT TRACKS AND DOWNLIGHT GRIDS, RETROFITTED

Often sold as a modern fix for a dull room. What they produce is a ceiling of small bright
sources all pointing straight down — error one, multiplied. They have real uses over a
worktop or aimed at art. As general lighting for a room where people sit, they are the most
expensive way to get a corridor.


VERY CHEAP MULTIPACKS OF BULBS

Where the colour temperature and the CRI are not printed, both are usually bad. Your colours
will not work after dark and you will not know why. A bulb is the highest-leverage cheap
thing in a house; buy the ones with the numbers on.


A DIMMER SWITCH, BEFORE YOU HAVE CHECKED YOUR BULBS

Fitting a dimmer to non-dimmable LEDs produces flicker and buzz, and people conclude the
dimmer is faulty. Check the bulbs first. And a wall dimmer is electrical work behind a
faceplate, which is an electrician's job.

  [ WHAT TO BUY INSTEAD, IN ORDER ]
2700 K bulbs for the whole house. Then one lamp for the floor, if you do not already own
something that will do. Then an in-line dimmer for the lamp you sit next to. That is a
complete lighting correction for a room, and in most homes it comes to less than the cost of
a single ceiling fitting.

CHAPTER THREE
Colour is not a property of paint
---------------------------------

This is the sentence that makes the rest of this part make sense, so I am going to spend a
page on it.

When you look at a painted wall, what reaches your eye is not the paint. It is light that
has hit the paint and bounced off it, having lost the wavelengths the pigment absorbed. So
the colour you see is the product of two things: what the paint absorbs, and what the light
was made of before it arrived.

Change either one and the colour changes. Not your impression of it — the actual light
entering your eye.

This is why the same tin produces two different colours on a north wall and a south wall in
the same flat. It is why the colour you chose under the shop's lights is not the colour you
have at home. And it is why so many people conclude they are bad at choosing colours, when
what actually happened is that they chose correctly for a light they do not own.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE FIRST LAW AGAIN, BECAUSE NOW YOU CAN SEE WHY
  | Light before colour. You cannot choose a colour until you know which way the window
  | faces.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


THE NAME FOR THIS, SINCE KNOWING IT HELPS

When two colours match under one light and stop matching under another, the effect is called
metamerism. It is the reason a cushion and a wall that agreed in the shop can disagree in
your sitting room, and it is not anybody's fault. Two different pigment mixes can reflect
the same overall colour under one light source and separate completely under another.

You cannot prevent it. You can only ever judge a colour in the place and the light it will
live in — which is the entire method of the next four pages.


WHAT THIS PART WILL AND WILL NOT DO

It will give you a way of testing colour that removes almost all of the risk, a working
understanding of undertone, the handful of whites that are worth knowing by behaviour, and a
clear position on ceilings, woodwork and sheen.

It will not give you a palette to copy. I do not know your windows, and anybody who offers
you a colour scheme without knowing which way your room faces is selling you something.


[ page 47 ]

How to actually test a colour
-----------------------------

One method. It takes two days of waiting and about twenty minutes of work, and it is the
difference between choosing a colour and gambling on one.

   1. Buy sample pots, not chips. Two or three shortlisted colours. A chip is printed ink on
      card; a sample pot is the actual paint.
   2. Paint two large sheets of paper per colour, not the wall. A2 or larger — the biggest
      cheap paper you can find. Two coats, edge to edge, and let it dry fully. Paper rather
      than wall for three reasons: you can move it, the colour underneath is not showing
      through, and you are not left with test patches to cover later.
   3. Leave a white border, or cut the sheet to size. Colour is judged against whatever
      surrounds it. A patch of colour on an existing wall is being read against that wall
      and will look wrong in a way that tells you nothing.
   4. Move the sheets around the room and leave them for two days. On the wall facing the
      window. On the wall opposite it. In the darkest corner. Beside the curtain. Next to
      the sofa. Tape them up and go about your life.
   5. Look at them at four moments — morning, the middle of the day, dusk, and at night with
      only your own lamps on. A colour that survives all four is a colour you can commit to.
      Most shortlists lose a candidate at dusk.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | WHY PAPER AND NOT THE WALL
  | A patch painted on a wall is being judged against the colour beside it. A sheet you can
  | move is being judged against the room.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


THE COST OF SKIPPING THIS

A sample pot and two sheets of paper cost a few dollars. Repainting a room because the
colour was wrong costs a weekend, the paint, and the furniture moving — twice. I have never
met anybody who regretted the two days.


[ page 48 ]

Why a two-centimetre chip tells you nothing
-------------------------------------------

Every paint brand hands you a fan of small printed rectangles and invites you to choose a
wall from one. It cannot be done, and here are the four reasons.


ONE · IT IS INK, NOT PAINT

A printed chip is a reproduction. It has none of the paint's actual pigment, none of its
finish, and none of the way real paint sits and scatters light on a surface. It is a
photograph of a colour.


TWO · SCALE CHANGES WHAT YOU SEE

A colour covering a whole wall reads as considerably more saturated and usually darker than
the same colour on a small card. This is partly the eye adapting and partly light bouncing
between walls, each bounce deepening the colour. A colour that looks gentle on a chip can be
startling at four square metres. **Choose one or two steps paler than the chip you liked.**


THREE · IT IS SURROUNDED BY OTHER COLOURS

On a fan deck your chip sits between two neighbours, and your eye reads it against them.
Pull it out and lay it on white paper and it is a different colour. This is simultaneous
contrast and you cannot switch it off; you can only control what surrounds the sample.


FOUR · YOU ARE LOOKING AT IT UNDER SHOP LIGHT

Which is roughly four thousand kelvin from overhead and has nothing to do with your home.
Page 35.

  [ THE ONE THING ON A PAINT CARD WORTH READING ]
Many brands print an LRV — Light Reflectance Value — a number from 0 to 100 describing how
much light the colour bounces back, where 0 is a theoretical black and 100 a theoretical
white. It is the single most useful figure on the card, because it predicts how a colour
will behave in a dark room better than the colour itself does. Anything above about 70 is
behaving as a pale colour. Below about 25 it is behaving as a dark one.

Use the chips to narrow fifty colours to three. Then stop using them and paint the paper.


[ page 49 ]

Reading the sheets once they are up
-----------------------------------

You have three painted sheets taped around the room and two days have passed. This is how to
read them, because most people look and cannot say what they are seeing.


ASK FOUR QUESTIONS, IN THIS ORDER

   1. At the darkest moment of the day, is it still a colour? Look at dusk, before the lamps
      go on. Many pale colours collapse to grey at this hour. If it goes dead, it will be
      dead every evening of the year.
   2. In the brightest light it gets, does it go chalky or garish? Direct sun on a pale
      colour can bleach it to nothing; direct sun on a saturated one can make it shout.
   3. Under your own lamps at night, has it changed hue? Not just darkened — changed. Greens
      go grey, greys go lilac, whites go yellow. This is the test that catches the most
      mistakes, and it is the one nobody runs.
   4. Against the things that are staying in the room — the floor, the sofa, the curtain,
      the door — does it sit comfortably or does one of them start to look wrong? Move the
      sheet next to each in turn.


WHAT A GOOD ANSWER LOOKS LIKE

The colour you want is the one that is slightly less interesting than its rivals at every
hour, and never actively wrong at any of them. Colours that are thrilling at one particular
moment are usually exhausting to live with, because the other twenty-three hours are the
compromise.

  [ FROM THE NOTEBOOK ]
My father used to say that the colour you notice is the colour you will get tired of. He
chose for the light, not for the card, and he always looked at a colour last thing at night
before he would agree to mix a full tin of it.


IF TWO SURVIVE

Take the paler one. Every colour goes a little deeper once it covers a whole room and the
light starts bouncing between the walls, and almost nobody in the history of decorating has
wished their walls were darker after the second coat.


[ page 50 ]

Undertone, and why nobody explains it
-------------------------------------

This is the concept that separates people who can choose colours from people who cannot, and
it is usually described so vaguely that it sounds like intuition. It is not. It is a simple
thing.

Almost no paint colour is pure. A grey is never only grey; there is a little blue in it, or
a little green, or a little violet, or a little brown. That trace is the undertone, and it
is what the colour reveals when it is put next to something else or lit by something else.

You can ignore a colour's undertone right up until the moment it meets another colour. Then
it is the only thing that matters. A grey with a blue undertone beside a grey with a green
undertone will look dirty, and neither is a bad colour. They simply disagree.


WHY THIS RUINS SO MANY ROOMS

Because most rooms are not one colour. They are a wall colour, a ceiling white, a woodwork
white, a floor, a sofa and a curtain — six things, each with an undertone, chosen at
different times by different logics. If four of them lean warm and two lean cool, the room
will feel subtly wrong and nobody will be able to say why. Everything in it is perfectly
nice.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE PRACTICAL RULE
  | You do not need to know which undertone a room has. You need all of them to be the same
  | one.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


THE SHORTCUT, IF YOU TAKE NOTHING ELSE FROM THIS PAGE

Decide once, for the whole flat, whether you are warm or cool. Then choose everything on
that side — the whites, the greys, the timber, the metals. A consistently warm room and a
consistently cool room are both beautiful. A room that is half of each is the one that reads
as cheap.

Which one should you pick? Look at what you cannot change. The floor, usually. A honey oak
floor has decided that your flat is warm, whatever you were planning. Fighting the largest
surface in the room is a losing argument.


[ page 51 ]

How to see an undertone
-----------------------

You cannot see an undertone by staring at a colour on its own. You can only see it by
comparison, and once you know the trick you will never lose it.


THE WHITE PAPER TEST

Take a sheet of ordinary printer paper — which is a very cool, very bright white — and hold
your sample against it.

    - If the sample suddenly looks yellow, pink or brown, it is warm.
    - If it looks blue, grey or slightly violet, it is cool.
    - If it looks green, it is green, and greens behave as their own family — see below.

That is the whole test. It takes four seconds and it works on paint, fabric, tile and timber
equally.


THE THREE-SAMPLE TEST, WHICH IS BETTER

Put your candidate between two colours you already know. If you have a warm white and a cool
white to hand, lay the sample between them. The one it resembles is the side it is on. This
is more reliable than the paper test because it gives your eye two references instead of
one.


THE UNDERTONES YOU WILL MEET MOST OFTEN

    FAMILY                                     | WARM VERSION LEANS   | COOL VERSION LEANS
    ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Beiges and taupes are the hardest colours in the shop, because a great many of them have a

[ page 52 ]

There is no such thing as white
-------------------------------

Every paint brand sells between forty and a hundred whites. That is not cynicism. It is
because white is not a colour but a whole family of them, and the differences are the entire
point.

A white paint is a white pigment with a small amount of something else added. What that
something else is decides everything about how the colour behaves on your wall.


THE THREE THINGS THAT DIFFER BETWEEN WHITES

    - The undertone. Yellow, red, blue, green or grey, in a very small quantity. This is
      what decides whether your room feels warm or cold.
    - How much of it there is. A white with a trace of yellow is a warm white; a white with
      a good deal of yellow is cream, which behaves as a colour and not as a white.
    - Whether any black or grey has been added. A white knocked back with grey is softer,
      less reflective, and much easier to live with than a brilliant white — and most of the
      whites that decorators use are of this kind.


THE ONE TO BE CAREFUL OF

Most brands sell a brilliant white or pure white which has optical brighteners in it, making
it the coldest and most reflective thing you can put on a wall. It is designed to read as
clean and it does. It is also unforgiving: it shows every flaw in the plaster, it goes blue
in a north room, and it makes everything you put against it look slightly grubby, including
other whites.

It has real uses — a gallery, a workshop, a ceiling in a room that needs every photon it can
get. It is almost never the right white for a room where people sit.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | IF YOU REMEMBER ONE THING ABOUT WHITE
  | A white with a little grey in it looks expensive. A brilliant white looks like a rental.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

That is a judgement rather than a fact, and it is one I will defend. Brilliant white is what
a landlord reaches for because it is cheap, fast and neutral to every future tenant. Your
eye has learned that association, and it applies it to your own walls.


[ page 53 ]

THE SIX WHITES
Described by behaviour, not by brand
------------------------------------

I am not going to give you product names, because they change, they differ by country, and a
name tells you nothing about what a colour will do in your room. I am going to describe six
whites by how they behave. Every brand sells all six.


ONE · BRILLIANT WHITE

No undertone, often optically brightened. LRV near 90 or above.

The coldest and most reflective. Shows every imperfection. Goes blue in north light. Makes
adjacent whites look dirty.

Use it for: a ceiling in a genuinely dark room where reflectance matters more than warmth. A
workshop. Almost nothing else.


TWO · SOFT WHITE — WHITE WITH A LITTLE GREY

Grey undertone, LRV roughly 80 to 88.

The most useful white there is. Reads as white but has enough body to stop it glaring, and
it is forgiving of imperfect plaster. Works in more rooms than anything else in this list.

Use it for: walls in almost any room, ceilings in most rooms, and woodwork if you want it
quiet. If you cannot decide, this is the answer.


THREE · WARM WHITE — WHITE WITH A LITTLE YELLOW

Yellow undertone, LRV roughly 80 to 88.

Gentle, slightly sunny, flatters skin and timber. In strong south light it can tip towards
cream, which some people love and some find dated.

Use it for: north-facing rooms, which need the warmth; rooms with honey or oak floors;
bedrooms.

  [ READING THE NUMBERS ]
LRV figures here are the ordinary ranges these whites fall into, not a rule. Use them to
compare candidates on the same card: a white at 84 will behave noticeably differently in a
dark room from one at 74, and the number will tell you that before your eye does.


[ page 54 ]

The six whites, continued
-------------------------


FOUR · COOL WHITE — WHITE WITH A LITTLE BLUE OR VIOLET

Blue undertone, LRV roughly 80 to 88.

Crisp and slightly austere. In a south-facing room it is genuinely beautiful, because the
warm sunlight cancels the blue and leaves something very clean. In a north-facing room it is
bleak.

Use it for: south and west rooms, bathrooms, kitchens with a lot of daylight. Never a north
room.


FIVE · GREIGE — WHITE KNOCKED WELL BACK WITH WARM GREY

Brown-grey undertone, LRV roughly 60 to 75.

No longer really a white; it is a very pale colour. Reads as soft, quiet and slightly
expensive, and it is extremely forgiving of poor light and poor plaster.

Use it for: the room you cannot make work. Hallways, north rooms, rooms with a lot of
mismatched furniture that needs calming down.


SIX · CREAM — WHITE WITH A GOOD DEAL OF YELLOW

Strong yellow undertone, LRV roughly 70 to 84.

Behaves as a colour, not a white. Warm, soft, and strongly associated with older interiors;
whether that reads as handsome or dated depends entirely on what you put with it. It is
unbeatable against dark timber, old brass and worn stone.

Use it for: period rooms, rooms with substantial woodwork, anywhere with an open fire.

  [ THE MOST COMMON WHITE MISTAKE ]
Using two different whites in the same room without meaning to — a cool white on the ceiling
and a warm white on the walls, or trade white on the woodwork and something chosen on the
walls. The eye reads the mismatch as dirt rather than as a decision. Either use one white
throughout, or make the difference large enough to be obviously deliberate.

[ page 56 ]

Where white fails
-----------------

White is the default, which means it is chosen far more often than it is decided. Here are
the five rooms where it is the wrong answer, and what to do instead.


ONE · THE ROOM WITH NO DAYLIGHT

Covered on page 39 and worth repeating here: white needs light to be white. In a sunless
room it goes grey and dead, and a grey dead room announces what it is missing. Go warm and
deep instead.


TWO · THE SMALL ROOM YOU WANT TO FEEL FINISHED

See the next page, which argues this one out properly.


THREE · THE ROOM WITH BAD PLASTER

White, and especially brilliant white, is the least forgiving finish there is. Every ripple,
patch and filled hole reads. A colour with some depth in it, in a matt finish, hides a
surprising amount. If your walls are old and uneven, paint is doing repair work as well as
decoration.


FOUR · THE TRANSITIONAL SPACE

Hallways, landings, corridors, cloakrooms. You are in them for seconds, so they can carry a
much stronger colour than a room you sit in — and because they are small and dark they tend
to make white look grubby. This is the cheapest place in the house to be brave.


FIVE · THE ROOM THAT IS ALREADY FULL OF WHITE THINGS

A white kitchen with white units, white tiles and white appliances does not need white walls
as well. At some point the room stops reading as clean and starts reading as unresolved,
because there is nothing for the eye to measure the whites against.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE TEST
  | Ask whether you chose white or defaulted to it. If you cannot say what the alternative
  | was, you defaulted.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

None of which is an argument against white. Half the rooms I have worked in ended up white,
and page 55 exists because choosing the right one matters. It is an argument against white
as a way of avoiding the decision.


[ page 57 ]

MY MOST UNPOPULAR POSITION
A small room painted pale looks smaller
---------------------------------------

I want to mark this clearly: this is my professional opinion and not a law of physics. It is
also the piece of advice I have most often been argued with about, and most often been
thanked for later.

The standard advice is that pale colours make a small room feel larger. The reasoning is
that pale walls reflect more light, and a brighter room reads as bigger. The first half of
that is true — a high-LRV colour genuinely does bounce more light. The conclusion does not
follow.


WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENS IN A SMALL PALE ROOM

    - You can see exactly where the walls are. A pale wall is legible. Every corner, every
      junction, every edge is clearly drawn, and your eye measures the room accurately —
      which in a small room is not what you want.
    - Every flaw is on display. The radiator, the cables, the patched plaster, the
      mismatched skirting. In a small room these are close to you and there is nowhere for
      them to hide.
    - It reads as unfinished. Because the association is with rooms that have not been
      decided about: rentals, new builds before anyone moves in, rooms between tenants.


WHAT HAPPENS IN A SMALL DARK ROOM

The corners soften and the eye loses the exact edges of the space, so the room stops
announcing its dimensions. The flaws recede. And most importantly it reads as deliberate —
nobody paints a room deep green by accident, so the room is immediately understood as a
choice rather than a shortage.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | COLETTE’S RULE OF THE SMALL PALE ROOM
  | A small room painted pale does not look bigger. It looks small and cheap. A small room
  | painted deep looks deliberate.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


WHERE I CONCEDE

If the small room is also the only room, and you are in it all day, and it has one small
window, a deep colour can genuinely be oppressive. And if you are selling the flat, pale is
the commercially safe answer, because buyers are told the same advice I am arguing with.
Both of those are real. Neither is about how the room looks.


[ page 58 ]

What a deep colour actually does
--------------------------------

If you are going to take the argument on the last page seriously, you need to know what you
are buying. Four things happen, and the third surprises people.


ONE · THE EDGES OF THE ROOM STOP BEING LEGIBLE

In a dark room, the corner where two walls meet carries very little contrast, so your eye
cannot place it precisely. The room's boundary becomes approximate. This is the whole
mechanism behind “it feels bigger”, and it is the opposite of the reason usually given.


TWO · EVERYTHING IN FRONT OF THE WALL GAINS CONTRAST

A pale lamp against a dark wall has enormous presence. A picture frame reads. A plant reads.
A face reads. In a pale room all of these are competing with a bright background and lose;
in a dark room they are lit objects against a quiet ground, which is how a painting is hung
in a gallery and why it works.


THREE · THE LIGHTING STARTS DOING MUCH MORE WORK

This is the one people are not warned about. A dark room absorbs light rather than bouncing
it, so a single ceiling fitting will not carry it — the room will simply be gloomy. Deep
colour and the three heights from Part Two are a package. If you paint a room dark and light
it from one point, you will hate it, and you will blame the colour.


FOUR · THE ROOM ACQUIRES A TIME OF DAY

A dark room is at its best in the evening, under lamps, and is frankly a little sombre at
ten in the morning. That is fine for a sitting room, a dining room or a study. It is a real
consideration for a room you work in all day.

  [ BEFORE YOU COMMIT ]
Paint the sheets, as always, but for a deep colour paint four and put them on all four walls
at once, because a dark colour on one wall of a pale room tells you nothing about a dark
room. And check page 61 first: in most cases a deep colour wants the ceiling and woodwork to
go with it, and that changes the size of the job.


[ page 59 ]

How dark is dark enough
-----------------------

The commonest failure with a deep colour is not going far enough. A mid-tone is the one
place you genuinely should not stop.

There are broadly three places a wall colour can sit, and the middle one is the trap.

    ROUGHLY                                    | READS AS             | VERDICT
    ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    LRV 70–90                                  | Pale. The wall is a  | Works. Commits to be
    LRV 35–70                                  | Mid-tone. Too dark t | The trap. Often read
    LRV under 25                               | Deep. The wall reced | Works. Commits to be

These bands are a working guide rather than a standard, and the boundaries are soft — a
saturated mid-tone in a well-lit room can be excellent. But if you find yourself choosing a
colour because it seems like a sensible compromise between pale and dark, that is usually
the signal to go one way or the other.


WHY THE MIDDLE FAILS

Because both mechanisms need commitment. A pale colour works by reflecting light and staying
out of the way; a deep colour works by absorbing light and softening the edges. A mid-tone
does neither. It is bright enough that you still read every corner, and dark enough that the
room is noticeably dimmer. You have paid the cost of the dark room and bought none of the
benefit.

  [ FROM THE NOTEBOOK ]
A client in Lyon asked my grandmother what colour to paint a small dining room. She said:
either a colour you could read a book against, or a colour you could not. Anything in
between and you will repaint it within two years. She was right about that woman, who
repainted it within two years.


THE PRACTICAL VERSION

Choose your candidate. Then find the version of it two or three steps darker on the same
strip and test that one too. Nine times out of ten the darker one is the answer.

THE FIFTH WALL
What to do with a ceiling
-------------------------

You met le cinquième mur on page 11. Here is the decision, and there are only three answers.


ONE · A WHITE, BUT CHOSEN RATHER THAN ASSUMED

The default, and correct more often than not — but not brilliant white, and not a different
white from the walls. The usual best answer is the wall colour mixed with white at about a
quarter strength, which most merchants will do for you. It reads as white, it belongs to the
room, and the junction where wall meets ceiling stops being a hard line.


TWO · THE SAME COLOUR AS THE WALLS

Wall colour taken straight across the ceiling. This is the one that surprises people, and in
the right room it is the best thing in this part of the book. What it does is remove the
boundary between wall and ceiling, so the room reads as a single volume rather than a box
with a lid.

Best in: small rooms, rooms with awkward angles or sloping ceilings, box rooms, and any room
you want to feel enclosing rather than tall. It is also the answer to a ceiling covered in
pipework or patches, because it stops advertising them.


THREE · DARKER THAN THE WALLS

A deliberate move for a room with a high ceiling that feels cold or cavernous. A darker
ceiling appears to come closer, which brings a tall room down to a human scale.

Best in: high-ceilinged period rooms — a Haussmannian apartment runs between two point eight
and three point two metres, and a room like that can carry a dark ceiling easily. A dining
room. Anywhere you want the evening to feel intimate.

  [ THE ONE THING NOT TO DO ]
Leave the old brilliant white ceiling in place while painting the walls a considered colour.
It is the most common half-finished job there is: a careful colour on four surfaces and a
cold builder's white on the fifth, meeting it in a hard line all the way round the room.
Whatever you choose, choose it.


[ page 62 ]

One colour, everywhere
----------------------

Walls, ceiling, woodwork, skirting, door, window frame — all in the same colour. It sounds
extreme. It is the single most reliable way to make an ordinary room look considered.


WHY IT WORKS

Every junction in a room is a place where the eye stops: wall to ceiling, wall to skirting,
wall to architrave, architrave to door. An ordinary room has dozens, and each one is drawn
in high contrast because the woodwork is white and the wall is not. Your eye spends the
whole time tracing an outline of the room's plumbing.

Take the contrast out of those junctions and they stop being events. What is left is a
single quiet volume with your furniture in it — and the furniture, the art and the people
become the things you look at, because they are now the only things with contrast in the
room.


WHERE IT EARNS ITS KEEP MOST

    - Rooms with ugly or excessive woodwork. Boxed-in pipes, a radiator, four doors, a fuse
      box. Painting all of it out is the only cheap fix that genuinely works.
    - Small rooms and awkward shapes. Attic rooms with sloping ceilings, box rooms, under-
      stair spaces.
    - Hallways, which are almost entirely made of junctions.


HOW TO DO IT PROPERLY

Use one colour in different finishes, not one finish everywhere: matt on the walls and
ceiling, eggshell or satin on the woodwork. The colour reads as continuous, but the woodwork
is durable and washable, and the slight difference in sheen gives the room a subtle life
that a single flat finish does not have. Page 65 covers sheens.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | WHY THIS IS NOT JUST A TREND
  | Before white paint was cheap, rooms were routinely finished in one colour throughout,
  | because mixing several was expensive. What looks like a bold modern choice is mostly a
  | return to the ordinary.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


[ page 63 ]

Skirtings, architraves and doors
--------------------------------

Woodwork is white because woodwork is white. It is the same unexamined default as the
ceiling, and it is doing more harm in more rooms.

Add up the woodwork in an ordinary room: skirting all the way round, architrave round the
door and the window, the door itself, perhaps a picture rail. It is a considerable area, and
in most homes it is painted the brightest white in the house, in the shiniest finish in the
house, outlining every edge of the room in high contrast.


THE FOUR OPTIONS, FROM QUIETEST TO LOUDEST

   1. The same colour as the walls. Everything disappears. See the previous page. The best
      answer in most small and awkward rooms.
   2. A shade or two deeper than the walls, same colour family. The woodwork reads as a soft
      frame rather than a bright outline. Elegant, easy, and almost impossible to get wrong.
   3. A soft white or greige rather than brilliant white. If you want the contrast of white
      woodwork without the glare. This is the standard decorator's answer and it suits
      period rooms with good mouldings that deserve to be seen.
   4. A deliberately contrasting colour. A deep green skirting under pale walls, a black
      door. A real move with real presence, and the one most likely to go wrong. Commit to
      it across the whole room or it looks like an unfinished job.


THE DOOR IS WORTH ITS OWN THOUGHT

A door is a large flat rectangle at eye level, and painting it the wall colour makes it
vanish — which is usually what you want in a small room with several doors. Painting it a
deep colour when the walls are pale makes it an object, which is worth doing when the door
is handsome and there is only one of them.

  [ OLD PAINT, AND PLEASE READ THIS ONE ]
Woodwork in older buildings can carry many layers. In the United States, lead-based paint
was banned for residential use in 1978; other countries have their own dates, generally in
the same era. Sanding old woodwork is what makes lead dangerous, because it puts it into the
air. If your building predates that and you are planning to sand woodwork back, get the
paint tested first. This is not a job to guess at, and it is the one place in this book
where I would rather you did nothing than proceed uncertain.


[ page 64 ]

The accent wall, properly examined
----------------------------------

I dismissed this in one paragraph on page 20. It is common enough to deserve the full
argument, and there is a version that works.


WHAT GOES WRONG

    - It shortens the room. A darker surface is read by the eye as nearer. Paint one wall
      dark and three pale, and the room appears shorter in that direction. People almost
      always paint the far wall, and the far wall is the one whose distance they were hoping
      to increase.
    - It divides rather than transforms. The room now has two characters and a hard line
      between them, and the line is the thing you see.
    - It is often the window wall, which is the worst choice available: you have put your
      darkest surface immediately around your only light source, so the contrast between the
      bright window and the dark wall is at its most severe and the room reads as gloomy.
    - The junctions get worse, not better. Now there are two colours meeting at two corners,
      plus white woodwork, plus a white ceiling. More edges, not fewer.


THE VERSION THAT WORKS

Not an accent wall — an accent volume. Take the colour round an entire part of the room that
is architecturally separate: an alcove and the shelves in it, a chimney breast including its
returns, the inside of a bookcase, a bay, a small connected room seen through a doorway.

The difference is that you are colouring a thing rather than a surface. An alcove painted
deep green is an alcove. The far wall painted deep green is a wall that has been painted a
different colour from the other three.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE TEST BEFORE YOU PAINT ONE WALL
  | Ask whether the surface you are about to colour is a thing or a side. Paint things.
  | Leave sides alone.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

And if what you actually want is for the room to feel different — the thing an accent wall
promises — the answer is on page 62. One colour on all four walls and the ceiling changes a
room far more than one wall in five ever will, and takes about the same amount of paint.


[ page 65 ]

Sheen, which is half the decision
---------------------------------

Two tins of the identical colour in different finishes are two different colours on the
wall. Sheen is not a detail at the end of the process; it changes what you see.

Sheen is how much light a finish reflects directly back at you rather than scattering it. A
flat finish scatters almost everything, so the colour looks deep, soft and even. A gloss
finish bounces a good deal straight back, so the colour looks lighter, harder and more
variable depending on where you stand.

A USEFUL STORY
The most copied French colour is a mistake
------------------------------------------

There is a grey that the design world has been copying for two hundred years, and
restoration has now established that it was never the original colour at all.

It is called gris Trianon — Trianon grey. It appears as a named court colour in Versailles
inventory documents of the 1770s and 1780s, and it is associated with the Temple of Love at
the Petit Trianon, the small palace in the grounds of Versailles. It is a soft dove grey
that reads almost as cement in poor light and as a pale barley-taupe in good light, and if
you have ever seen a photograph of a beautiful French room with grey panelling, you have
seen its descendants.

What restoration work at the Trianon has revealed is that the famous grey is a nineteenth-
century repaint. Underneath it, the original finish was a pale green.

So the colour that the whole world understands as the authentic colour of eighteenth-century
French elegance is a Victorian decorator's decision, applied over the real thing, and copied
ever since by people who believed they were being faithful.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | WHY I PUT THIS IN A BOOK ABOUT YOUR FLAT
  | There is no authentic colour to be faithful to. There is only what works in the light
  | you have.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


AND IT IS STILL A VERY GOOD GREY

That is the other half of the point. Knowing it is a repaint does not make it worse. It is a
warm grey with enough green in it to stay soft under electric light, which is exactly why it
has survived two centuries of copying — it behaves well. The nineteenth-century decorator
who chose it knew what they were doing.

If you want a grey that does not go cold at night, look for one with a trace of green rather
than blue in it. That is the whole technical content of this famous colour, and it costs
nothing to apply.


[ page 68 ]

OLD WALLS
Badigeon à la chaux — limewash
------------------------------

If your building is old and has solid walls, this page may save you a great deal of trouble,
and it is the one my father would have wanted in the book.

Limewash is one of the oldest wall finishes there is — lime finishes go back to Greek and
Roman building. It is made of three things: slaked lime, water, and natural pigment. That is
the whole recipe. It dries to a chalky, matte, faintly variegated surface with a quality of
depth that modern paint cannot reproduce, because you are looking slightly into it rather
than at a film on top.


THE PROPERTY THAT MATTERS MORE THAN THE LOOK

Limewash is breathable. It is porous, so moisture in a wall can pass through it and
evaporate away.

Old buildings with solid walls and no damp-proof course are designed to work this way. They
take up moisture and release it again, constantly, and they stay sound as long as nothing
stops them. A modern plastic-based paint forms an impermeable film, which traps that
moisture in the wall. The result is damp that appears after redecorating, blown plaster, and
paint that lifts off in sheets — and the owner concludes the wall has a damp problem, when
what it has is a paint problem.

  [ TWO REAL CAUTIONS ]
Wet lime is caustic. It has a very high pH and it will burn skin and eyes. Gloves and eye
protection, every time, and this is not a place to be relaxed about it.

And limewash will not adhere to a wall that is already covered in modern emulsion. It needs
a porous surface — bare plaster, lime plaster, brick or stone. Painting it over vinyl
emulsion will simply produce dust.


WHO THIS IS ACTUALLY FOR

If you live in a modern building with plasterboard walls, none of this applies to you and
ordinary paint is correct. If you live in something old with thick solid walls, it is worth
finding out what is already on them before you add another layer, because the wrong finish
on an old wall is a slow and expensive mistake.


[ page 69 ]

How many colours a home should have
-----------------------------------

Fewer than you think, and repeated more than you think. This is the page that makes a flat
feel like one home rather than a series of decisions.


THE WORKING SCHEME

   1. One white. The same white on every ceiling and every piece of woodwork in the flat,
      unless a room is being drenched in its own colour. One tin, one decision, permanent
      consistency.
   2. One quiet colour that carries the circulation — hall, landing, stairs, and any room
      you have not made a specific decision about. This is the flat's ground note.
   3. Two or three room colours, each belonging to a room with a distinct character. The
      bedroom, the sitting room, perhaps a study or dining room.
   4. One deeper accent that appears in small quantities across several rooms — a door, an
      alcove, a bookcase interior. This is the thread that stitches the flat together.

Five or six colours for a whole home. Most flats I have worked in had eleven or twelve, all
chosen separately, none of them wrong, and the whole never adding up.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | WHY REPETITION MATTERS MORE THAN VARIETY
  | A colour used once is a decoration. The same colour used three times in three rooms is a
  | scheme.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


THE DOORWAY TEST

Stand in any doorway where you can see into two rooms at once, which in most flats is the
hall. Everything visible from that spot has to agree, because you will see it together
several times a day.

This is where the undertone rule from page 50 does its real work: not within a room, but
across a threshold. Two rooms that are lovely on their own and disagree at the doorway is
the commonest whole-flat error there is, and it is invisible until you go and stand there.

And the practical benefit: one white and one circulation colour means you own two large tins
that cover nine-tenths of the surfaces in the flat. Touching up becomes trivial, and it
costs less.


[ page 70 ]

The floor has already voted
---------------------------

The largest continuous surface in your home is the one you almost certainly cannot change.
Every colour decision above it is a response to it, whether you meant it to be or not.

A floor is typically a quarter of the visible surface of a room, it runs under everything,
and it is lit from above all day, which means it is bouncing its own colour onto the bottom
of everything in the room. A warm honey oak floor is putting a warm cast on your walls
continuously.


READ THE FLOOR FIRST

    ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    Honey or golden oak                        | warm, quite strongly | warm whites, greige,
    Mid brown, walnut                          | warm, moderately     | almost anything warm
    Grey or limed timber                       | cool                 | cool whites, blue-gr

IF YOU ARE FIGHTING THE FLOOR

You will lose. A cool grey wall above an orange-toned floor is a fight that happens in every
room of the flat, all day, and no amount of styling settles it. The honest options are to
work with the floor's temperature, to cover a good deal of it with a rug in your chosen
temperature, or to change the floor — which is expensive and is the only place in this book
where I would say the expensive answer is often correct.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE FLOOR RUNS THROUGH
  | One continuous floor through connected rooms makes a small flat read as one home. A
  | change of flooring at every threshold chops it into cupboards.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

This is mine and it is about layout as much as colour. If you are ever in a position to
choose flooring for more than one room, choose the same one and run it through. It is the
single most effective thing you can do to make a small flat feel generous.


[ page 71 ]

Matching is a trap
------------------

The instinct, once you have chosen a wall colour, is to find a cushion in the same colour.
Resist it. Matching is what makes a room look like it came in a box.

Two things prevent an exact match from ever working. The first is that the same colour in
two different materials is not the same colour — paint on plaster and dye in wool reflect
light completely differently, so a perfect match on paper reads as a near-miss in the room,
and a near-miss is worse than a difference. The second is that a room where everything
agrees exactly has nothing for the eye to do.


WHAT TO DO INSTEAD

    - Stay in the family, change the depth. If the walls are a soft green, the cushions can
      be a much deeper green or a much paler one. Same undertone, different value. This
      always works.
    - Repeat the colour in a different material. The wall colour appearing again in a
      ceramic, a book spine, a painting — small, and in something with a different surface.
      The eye enjoys finding it.
    - Let one thing disagree on purpose. A room that is entirely harmonious is slightly
      dead. One object in a colour that has no relationship to the scheme is what stops it
      feeling assembled.


THE ONE THING THAT GENUINELY SHOULD MATCH

Whites. If you have white woodwork, white ceilings and white curtains, those should be the
same white or obviously different whites, never nearly-the-same whites. Near-misses in white
read as dirt. This is the exception to everything on this page.

  [ FROM THE NOTEBOOK ]
My grandmother would not sell a client a fabric that matched their wall. She said a room
should look as though it had been arrived at over years, not delivered on a Tuesday. She had
been making curtains for thirty years by then and could afford to say so.


[ page 72 ]

Colour when you cannot paint
----------------------------

Part Six is written entirely for rented rooms. This page is the colour half of it, because
everything above assumes a paintbrush you may not be allowed to pick up.


FIRST, CHECK WHETHER YOU ACTUALLY CANNOT

A great many tenancies permit redecoration with written permission, and a landlord who has a
white magnolia flat is often perfectly happy for you to improve it at your own cost if you
agree to return it to neutral. It is worth one email. The worst answer is no, which is where
you are already.


IF THE ANSWER IS NO, THE ORDER CHANGES

Without paint, the largest colour surfaces available to you are, in order of area:

   1. Curtains. Floor to near-ceiling, across a whole wall, in a colour you chose. This is
      by a wide margin the biggest colour decision available to a tenant, and Part Four
      covers how to hang them so they read as architecture rather than as fabric.
   2. A large rug. The second largest surface, and the one that can override an unpleasant
      floor's temperature. Bigger than feels sensible — page 97.
   3. The bed, in a bedroom. A bed dressed in one considered colour is most of the visual
      field in a small bedroom.
   4. Bookcases and their contents, which are a wall of colour you already own.
   5. Large art or a hanging textile. One big thing beats six small ones.


THE WHITE-WALL STRATEGY

If you are stuck with landlord white, stop treating it as a background to improve and start
treating it as a gallery wall. White walls are genuinely excellent behind strong colour and
strong texture — it is only as a whole scheme that they fail. Go deeper and warmer in
everything you can control, and let the walls be the quiet part.

  [ ONE THING WORTH KNOWING ]
Magnolia and builder's white are usually a cheap vinyl matt, which marks easily and cleans
badly. If you are allowed to repaint before you leave, budget for it — a flat returned in
fresh white is often the difference between getting a deposit back and arguing about it.


[ page 73 ]

MISTAKES TO AVOID
The six colour errors I saw most often
--------------------------------------


ONE · CHOOSING THE COLOUR BEFORE FIXING THE LIGHT

The error this whole book is ordered around. You choose for a light you are about to change,
and then you have chosen wrong. Bulbs first, always — it is an afternoon and it decides
everything.


TWO · TESTING ON THE WALL INSTEAD OF ON PAPER

A patch painted on an existing wall is judged against that wall, and the old colour around
it biases everything you see. It also means you cannot move it to the dark corner, which is
the position that matters most.


THREE · TWO WHITES THAT ARE NEARLY THE SAME

Ceiling in one white, woodwork in another, walls in a third, none of them chosen together.
The eye cannot read the difference as a decision so it reads it as dirt. One white through
the whole flat solves it permanently.


FOUR · THE MID-TONE COMPROMISE

Page 59. Choosing something halfway between pale and deep because it feels like the sensible
option gets you the disadvantages of both. Commit in one direction.


FIVE · IGNORING WHAT IS OUTSIDE THE WINDOW

A large tree throws green light into a room from spring to autumn. A red brick wall a few
metres away throws warm light in all year. Both will shift a wall colour more than the
compass direction does, and neither shows up on any paint card.


SIX · BUYING THE PAINT BEFORE THE SHEETS HAVE BEEN UP TWO DAYS

Because the shop is open now and the weekend is free. This is how almost every repainted
room happens. The two days cost nothing and they are the entire method.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | WHAT ALL SIX HAVE IN COMMON
  | Not one of them is a mistake of taste. Every one is a mistake of sequence — deciding
  | something before the thing it depends on was settled.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


[ page 74 ]

TROUBLESHOOTING
“I painted it and I hate it”
----------------------------

Before you repaint, work out which of these it is. Three of the five are not solved by a
different colour, and repainting would waste a weekend.


IT LOOKS MUCH DARKER THAN THE SAMPLE

Expected, and usually not a colour problem. A whole room reads deeper than a sample because
light bounces between the walls, each bounce deepening it. Give it a week before you judge —
your eye adapts considerably. If it is still too much, the fix is often lighting, not paint:
the room may simply have gone dark, which is page 58.


IT LOOKED FINE BY DAY AND IS HORRIBLE AT NIGHT

A bulb problem, not a paint problem. You have almost certainly got mixed colour temperatures
or bulbs above 3000 K. Change every bulb in the room to 2700 K, with a CRI of 90 or above,
and look again the following evening. This fixes it far more often than repainting does.


IT HAS GONE A COMPLETELY DIFFERENT COLOUR FROM THE SAMPLE

Check three things in order: whether the finish differs from what you tested (satin reads
lighter than matt), whether it has had two full coats, and whether the wall underneath was a
strong colour showing through. A deep colour over a dark wall can need three coats before it
is itself.


IT IS FINE BUT IT FIGHTS THE FLOOR OR THE SOFA

An undertone clash, page 50. Nothing is wrong with the colour and repainting in the same
family will not help. Either change the smaller thing, or put a rug between them in a colour
that belongs to both.


IT IS SIMPLY FLAT AND LIFELESS

Usually not colour at all. Check the four textures on page 10 and the three lighting heights
on page 28. A correct colour in a one-texture room lit from the ceiling will always look
dead, and painting it a second colour will not change that.

  [ BEFORE YOU REPAINT ANYTHING ]
Live with it for two weeks. Colour adaptation is real and substantial — a room that feels
overwhelming on day one often feels correct by day ten. I have talked more people out of
repainting than into it, and almost none of them came back.


[ page 75 ]

The order of painting a room
----------------------------

Not a decorating manual — there are good ones and this is not trying to be one. But the
sequence saves a day and almost nobody is told it.

   1. Empty the room, or as near as you can get. Everything into the middle, covered. The
      time you spend here you get back twice in not working around things.
   2. Fill, sand, and wash the walls. Fill every hole including the ones you are about to
      make again. Sugar soap or a cloth and warm water on anything greasy, especially above
      a radiator and around light switches, because paint does not stick to grease.
   3. Ceiling first. Always. It spatters, and everything below it will be painted
      afterwards.
   4. Walls second.
   5. Woodwork last. Skirting, architrave, doors, windows. Woodwork paint is slower, more
      precise, and the thing you most want to avoid rushing — so it goes when nothing else
      is waiting on it.
   6. Two coats of everything. Including the thing you think looks fine after one. A single
      coat on a wall reads patchy in raking light for as long as you live there.


THE TWO THINGS WORTH SPENDING MONEY ON

    - A decent brush for the woodwork and the cutting-in. A cheap brush sheds bristles into
      the wet paint and leaves a visibly worse edge, and you will be looking at that edge
      every day.
    - Enough paint. Running out two-thirds of the way through a wall and finishing from a
      second tin mixed at a different time is how you get a visible join. Buy the whole
      quantity at once, and if it is a mixed colour, check the tins are from the same batch.

  [ VENTILATION, AND THE ONE REAL HAZARD ]
Keep windows open and the room aired while you paint and for a day after. If the building
predates the late 1970s and you intend to sand old paintwork rather than just paint over it,
stop and read page 63 first — sanding is what makes old lead paint dangerous.

A WORKED EXAMPLE
One room, from start to finish
------------------------------


THE ROOM

A sitting room of about sixteen square metres in a 1930s flat. One window facing north-east,
used mostly in the evenings. Honey oak floorboards. A mid-grey sofa. Landlord white walls, a
brilliant white ceiling, white woodwork, and one ceiling pendant with a 4000 K bulb in it.
The owner described it as “fine but depressing” and wanted to paint it a pale grey.


THE AUDIT

    - Light: one source, one height, cool bulb. Error one, plus the wrong colour
      temperature.
    - Height: pictures centred at about 122 cm, curtain pole 4 cm above the frame, curtains
      6 cm off the floor. Error two, comprehensively.
    - Texture: fabric sofa, fabric curtain, fabric cushions, painted surfaces. Two families.
      Error three.
    - Ceiling: brilliant white, never considered. Error four.
    - Orientation: north-east, used in the evening — so, per page 26, decorate it as a north
      room.


WHAT WAS DONE, IN ORDER

   1. Every bulb changed to 2700 K, CRI 90. Two lamps added — one at 105 cm on a side table,
      one on the floor behind a chair. The pendant now stays off in the evening.
   2. Curtain pole moved to 12 cm below the ceiling and widened so the curtains stack on the
      wall. Curtains re-hung to the floor. Pictures re-hung to 145 cm.
   3. Only then, colour. The proposed pale grey was tested and abandoned: it went violet
      under the new warm bulbs and fought the honey floor. A soft warm white was chosen for
      the walls, the same white at quarter strength for the ceiling, and eggshell in the
      same white for the woodwork.
   4. A wool rug in a deep olive, an oak stool, and a large unglazed pot. Four textures.


THE POINT OF THE EXAMPLE

The owner asked for a colour. Colour was the fourth thing done, it was not the colour
requested, and the largest single change in the room was two lamps and a packet of bulbs.

WHAT NOT TO BUY
Paint and colour
----------------


A COLOUR FAN DECK

Two thousand chips makes the decision harder, not easier, and every one of them is printed
ink viewed under shop light. You need three candidates and two sheets of paper. The fan is a
merchandising object.


“ONE COAT” PAINT

It is thicker, it costs more, and it still wants two coats to look even in raking light. You
are paying a premium for a claim you will not act on.


PAINT-AND-PRIMER-IN-ONE, FOR A NORMAL REPAINT

Genuinely useful on bare plaster, new plasterboard or a drastic colour change. Painting a
similar colour over sound existing paint, which is most repaints, does not need it.


PEEL-AND-STICK WALLPAPER AS A CURE FOR RENTED WHITE

Sold directly at tenants and it is a recurring purchase dressed as a solution. It is
expensive per square metre, it is difficult to hang without visible seams, it frequently
does not survive removal without taking paint with it, and it solves a problem that curtains
and a rug solve better and cheaper. Page 72.


A COLOUR CONSULTATION THAT ARRIVES WITHOUT SEEING YOUR ROOM

Anybody choosing your colours from photographs is guessing, because a photograph has an
automatic white balance and has already destroyed the information about your light. If you
pay for advice, pay for someone who stands in the room at the hour you use it.


TESTER POTS IN MORE THAN THREE COLOURS

Buying eight is a symptom of not having narrowed the decision. Do the orientation table on
page 55, get to three, and test those properly.

  [ WHAT TO BUY INSTEAD ]
Three sample pots. Four large sheets of paper. One good brush. Enough paint for two coats,
in one batch. That is the entire shopping list for a room, and it is less than most people
spend on the things above.

THE QUIET PART
Who profits from the colour of the year
---------------------------------------

Every autumn, several large paint companies announce a colour of the year. It is reported as
news. It is worth looking at what it is for.

A colour of the year does two things at once. It gives magazines and shops something to
write about and arrange displays around, which is ordinary commerce and nobody's business
but theirs. And it establishes, by implication, that there is a colour which is current —
which necessarily means the colour on your walls is last year's.

That is the whole mechanism. Paint does not wear out. A well-painted wall in a room nobody
kicks will look perfectly good in fifteen years. So an industry selling a product that does
not expire has to supply a reason to replace it, and taste is the only reason available.

Nobody decided this in a room somewhere. It is quieter and sadder than that.

There is no money in telling you that the soft warm white you chose three years ago, for the
north-facing room you still live in, is still exactly the right colour for it. There is a
great deal of money in a calendar that quietly makes every room in the country a year out of
date each October. Not one person has to intend it for that to be how the whole thing works.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE TEST THAT SURVIVES ANY TREND
  | A colour chosen for the direction your window faces cannot go out of fashion, because
  | your window will not move.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


WHAT THIS IS NOT

It is not an argument that the paint companies make bad paint. They make very good paint,
the colours in their ranges are carefully developed, and the LRV figure on the card is one
of the most useful pieces of information in this whole subject. Use the product. Ignore the
calendar.

And if you like this year's colour — genuinely like it, in your own room, at dusk, under
your own lamps — then paint your room with it. Liking something is a perfectly good reason.
Being told it is current is not.


[ page 80 ]

QUESTIONS I AM ASKED
Eight short answers
-------------------


CAN I PAINT OVER WALLPAPER?

Sometimes, if it is sound, well stuck and not textured or vinyl. Test a small area first —
paint wets the paste and can lift a seam. If any edge is loose, it will get worse. Stripping
is more work and the right answer more often.


DO I NEED TO PRIME?

Bare plaster, new plasterboard, bare wood or a drastic colour change: yes. Sound existing
paint in a similar colour: no.


HOW MUCH PAINT WILL I NEED?

Measure the walls and check the tin, because coverage varies by brand and colour. Then buy
somewhat more than the tin claims. Deep colours and porous surfaces both drink more, and
running out mid-wall is the one thing you cannot recover from neatly.


IS EXPENSIVE PAINT WORTH IT?

Often, for a deep colour, where the pigment load genuinely shows in depth and coverage and
may save you a coat. Much less so for a white ceiling. Spend on the colour that people will
look at and economise on the ones they will not.


CAN I USE EXTERIOR PAINT INSIDE?

No. It is formulated for weather and flexibility rather than for scrubbing and low odour,
and some exterior products are not intended for enclosed spaces. Buy the right one.


WHAT ABOUT A RADIATOR?

Use a radiator-grade paint; ordinary white paint yellows with heat. Paint it cold, and leave
it a good while before turning the heating on.


SHOULD THE INSIDE OF MY FRONT DOOR MATCH THE OUTSIDE?

No, and it almost never should. The inside belongs to the hall's scheme, the outside belongs
to the street. They are two different rooms.


DOES A DARK CEILING REALLY MAKE A ROOM FEEL LOWER?

It makes it feel closer, which in a high room reads as intimate and in a low room reads as
oppressive. Page 61. Above roughly two point seven metres it is an option worth having;
below that, be careful.

THE HONEST PART
Where I might be wrong
----------------------

I will not sell you a fantasy, and that is the whole reason you can trust the rest of this.
Four places where what I have told you is opinion, or is limited, or where I know good
decorators who disagree with me.


THE SMALL PALE ROOM

Page 57 is my most contested position and I have marked it as mine throughout. Plenty of
excellent designers hold the opposite view and produce beautiful pale small rooms. What I
will defend without hesitation is the narrower claim: brilliant white in a small dark room
is a mistake. The argument about pale versus deep is a real argument.


THE WHOLE BOOK ASSUMES THE NORTHERN HEMISPHERE

Every orientation rule in Parts Two and Three assumes you are north of the equator. South of
it, swap north and south and leave east and west as they are. Near the equator the whole
framework weakens, because the sun passes much closer to overhead and the differences
between orientations narrow considerably.


LRV BANDS ARE GUIDANCE, NOT A STANDARD

The numbers on page 59 are working ranges I use, not an industry classification. A saturated
mid-tone in a well-lit south room can be excellent and would fall squarely in the band I
called a trap. Use them to compare candidates, not to rule one out.


SOMETIMES THE EXPENSIVE ANSWER IS THE RIGHT ONE

I have spent this part explaining how to avoid wasting money on paint. But if your floor is
fighting every colour in the flat, page 70, the honest answer is that changing the floor is
the real fix and no amount of clever colour work substitutes for it. I would rather say so
than sell you a workaround that leaves you dissatisfied for ten years.

The colour weekend
------------------

If you have read this far and want a plan rather than a philosophy, here it is. Two weekends
and one week of waiting in between.


WEEKEND ONE — ABOUT TWO HOURS OF ACTUAL WORK

   1. Change every bulb in the room to 2700 K. Before anything else, so that every judgement
      afterwards is made in the light you will keep.
   2. Find your orientation and write it down. Page 23.
   3. Decide warm or cool for the whole flat, by looking at the floor. Page 70.
   4. Narrow to three candidates using the table on page 55, and buy three sample pots and
      four large sheets of paper.
   5. Paint the sheets, two coats, and tape them up in three positions.


THE WEEK IN BETWEEN

Do nothing. Look at the sheets in passing — morning, midday, dusk, and at night under the
lamps. Move them once, halfway through. Let one colour lose.


WEEKEND TWO

   6. Fill, sand, wash. Every hole, including the ones you will re-drill.
   7. Ceiling, walls, woodwork, in that order, two coats of each. Page 75.
   8. Re-hang at the right heights while the walls are empty and you have the ladder out —
      pictures to 145 cm, the curtain pole as high and as wide as it will go. Part Four.
   9. Put back less than you took out.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE ONE THAT IS NOT OPTIONAL
  | Change the bulbs on the first morning, before you look at a single colour. Every other
  | step in this plan depends on it.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

And do one room. Not the flat. Finish it, live in it for a month, and you will make much
better decisions about the second room than you could possibly make today.

CHAPTER FOUR
The cheapest correction in this book
------------------------------------

Nothing else in decorating gives you this much for this little. A packet of picture hooks, a
longer curtain pole, and an afternoon.

You met the error on page 9: almost everything that hangs in an ordinary home hangs lower
than it should, because you hang things at the height where it is comfortable to hold them
against the wall, and that is a good deal lower than where they needed to go.

What I want to give you in this part is the set of numbers that replaces that instinct. Not
because numbers are more tasteful than your eye, but because your eye has been trained by
rooms that made the same mistake, and it will keep making it until something external
interrupts.


WHAT HAPPENS WHEN YOU CORRECT THE HEIGHTS

    - The room gets taller. Not metaphorically — the apparent height of the wall changes,
      because your eye reads the topmost object as the top of the usable space and
      everything above it as ceiling.
    - The furniture stops looking cheap. A sofa under art hung at the right height reads as
      a considered arrangement. The same sofa under art hung twenty centimetres too low
      reads as a sofa with a picture over it.
    - The window becomes architecture. This is the largest single effect in the part, and it
      is what the curtain pages are about.
    - Everything you already own looks better. Nothing has been bought.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE HABIT THAT PREVENTS EVERY ERROR IN THIS PART
  | Before you put a hole in anything, mark the height with masking tape and leave it up for
  | a day. Walk past it. Sit down and look at it.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


A WORD ON THE NUMBERS

Everything in this part is a working figure from the trade, not a law. Where a number is a
genuine standard — the gallery hanging height, for instance — I say so. Where it is what I
use and what most decorators use, I say that instead. Rooms differ, and a number that fights
your room is wrong for your room.


[ page 87 ]

THE ONE NUMBER TO REMEMBER
One hundred and forty-five centimetres
--------------------------------------

Art hangs with its centre at 145 cm — about 57 inches — from the floor. Not the top, not the
bottom, not the hook. The centre.

This is the standard hanging height used in galleries and museums, and it is not a matter of
taste. It puts the middle of the work at the eye level of an average standing adult. It is a
fact about where human eyes are, and it is why gallery walls feel comfortable and most
domestic walls do not.


HOW TO FIND THE HOOK POSITION

You want the centre of the picture at 145. The hook goes above that, and where exactly
depends on how far the wire drops when it takes the weight.

   1. Measure the picture's height. Halve it. Call that H.
   2. Hang the wire on your finger as if it were the hook, pull it taut, and measure from
      there down to the top of the frame. Call that D.
   3. Hook height = 145 + H − D.

For a picture 60 cm tall whose wire pulls up to 8 cm below the top of the frame: 145 + 30 −
8 = 167 cm for the hook. Do the arithmetic on paper rather than in your head at the top of a
ladder.

  [ IF YOUR CEILINGS ARE VERY LOW OR VERY HIGH ]
Under about 2.3 m, drop to roughly 140. Above about 3 m — a Haussmannian room runs 2.80 to
3.20 — you can lift to 150 or so, and larger work will want it. But 145 is right far more
often than people expect, including in tall rooms, because eyes do not get higher when
ceilings do.


[ page 88 ]

When the wall rule and the furniture disagree
---------------------------------------------

Art hung above a sofa, a bed, a console or a fireplace stops following the room and starts
following the furniture. Here is how to resolve it.


THE FURNITURE RULE

    Gap between the top of the furniture and the bottom of the frame
                                                                          15–25 cm · 6–10 in
    Width of the art, as a share of the furniture below it .............. roughly two thirds

WHEN THE TWO RULES CONFLICT

A tall sofa back plus a 15 cm gap can put the picture's centre well above 145. A low bed
with a small picture can put it well below. Which wins?

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE RESOLUTION
  | Furniture wins. A picture above furniture belongs to that furniture, and the eye reads
  | the pair, not the wall.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

Take the 145 rule as governing anything hung on open wall — a corridor, a stairwell, a blank
wall, a chimney breast with nothing under it. Take the furniture rule as governing anything
hung above something. Almost all the disagreement disappears once you decide which case you
are in.


ABOVE A FIREPLACE

A third case, and the one people get wrong most. A mirror or picture over a mantel follows
the mantel, not the room: leave roughly 10 to 15 cm above the shelf. And keep it narrower
than the mantel — art wider than the fireplace opening below it makes the fireplace look
shrunken.

  [ THE ONE TO BE CAREFUL ABOUT ]
A television over a fireplace is almost always too high for comfortable viewing, whatever it
does for the wall. If the room's main use is watching it, that is a real cost and worth
being honest with yourself about before the bracket goes in.


[ page 89 ]

Pairs, rows and gallery walls
-----------------------------

Several pictures stop being several pictures and become one object. That is the whole
principle, and every rule below follows from it.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE PRINCIPLE
  | A group of pictures is a single shape. Hang the shape at 145, not each picture in it.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

So for a group, the centre of the whole arrangement sits at 145. Individual frames within it
will sit higher and lower, and that is correct.


THE SPACING

    Gap between frames in a group .......................................... 5–8 cm · 2–3 in
    Same gap, used consistently throughout ................ yes — this is what makes it read

THREE ARRANGEMENTS THAT WORK

    - A grid. Identical frames, identical gaps, equal rows and columns. Formal, calm, very
      easy to get right, and the best answer for a set of related images.
    - A shared line. Mixed sizes, but all frames aligned along one horizontal — either their
      tops, their bottoms, or their centres. The shared line is what holds it together.
    - A block. Mixed sizes filling a rectangle, with the outer edge of the whole arrangement
      forming a clean rectangle even though the interior is irregular. The hardest of the
      three and the most rewarding.


THE PAPER METHOD, WHICH REMOVES ALL THE RISK

Cut a piece of paper to the size of every frame. Tape them to the wall and move them around
until the arrangement is right. Mark the hook position on each piece of paper while it is
still on the wall, then drill through the paper. You will get it right the first time, and
the only holes in your wall will be the ones you meant.


[ page 90 ]

Mirrors, and what they are actually for
---------------------------------------

A mirror reflects what is in front of it. That is the entire mechanism, and almost every
mirror mistake comes from forgetting it.

You met the short version on page 20. Here is the working method.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | BEFORE YOU HANG ONE
  | Stand where the mirror will go and look at what is directly opposite. That is what you
  | are about to put on your wall.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


WHAT IS WORTH REFLECTING

    - A window. The best reflection there is. A mirror opposite or adjacent to a window puts
      a second bright rectangle into the room and genuinely increases the light.
    - A lamp or a lit corner. Especially in a dark room or a hallway.
    - A long view — down a corridor, through a doorway into another room. Depth is what a
      mirror is best at.
    - Something you like looking at. A bookcase, a plant, a good painting.


WHAT IS NOT

A blank wall. The back of a door. A radiator. The kitchen bin. A television, which is a
black rectangle for most of the day and a distraction for the rest. And — the one people
forget — the ceiling, which is what a mirror hung too high above a fireplace usually shows
you.


HEIGHTS

    Decorative mirror on open wall — centre ................................. 145 cm · 57 in
    Over a mantel — above the shelf ...................................... 10–15 cm · 4–6 in
    Over a basin or console — above the surface .......................... 15–20 cm · 6–8 in
    A full-length mirror — lean it, or fix it ............................ top at 175–185 cm
  [ TWO PRACTICAL POINTS ]
A large mirror is heavy and heavier than it looks. Fix it into something solid, not into
plasterboard alone, and use the fixings rated for the weight — this is one of the few
genuinely dangerous things in this book.

And a leaning full-length mirror must be secured to the wall behind it. They tip.


[ page 91 ]

Shelves, and the rail you already own
-------------------------------------

If your home has a picture rail, you have a hanging system already installed and you are
almost certainly not using it.

A picture rail is the moulded strip running round a room at or near the head height of the
doors. It exists because plaster walls were once difficult to fix into, so pictures hung
from cords or chains dropped from the rail. In older buildings it is still there, painted
over, ignored.


WHY IT IS WORTH USING

    - No holes. Which makes it the single most useful thing in a rented flat with period
      features — and it is the first thing I look for in one.
    - Everything is adjustable. Move a picture in thirty seconds, with no filler.
    - The drop itself reads well. Two visible cords running down to a frame is a handsome,
      deliberate detail, not a compromise.

Hooks that slip over the rail cost very little. Use two cords rather than one — a single
cord makes a triangle and the picture will swing; two parallel drops hang straight and stay
put. And the 145 rule still applies: adjust the cord length so the picture's centre lands
there, rather than hanging everything from the rail at the same drop.


SHELVES

    Lowest shelf of an open unit, above the floor ........................ at least 25–30 cm
    Highest shelf you will use daily ................................. no higher than 180 cm
    Between shelves, for ordinary books ........................................... 28–32 cm
    A single floating shelf, above a sofa or desk ................... treat as art — page 88

THE THING ABOUT BOOKCASES

A bookcase that stops two-thirds of the way up a wall leaves an awkward band of empty wall
above it and makes the ceiling look lower. A bookcase that runs to the ceiling — or has
something on top that closes the gap — reads as architecture. If you are buying one, measure
the wall first and buy the tallest that fits.


[ page 92 ]

THE LARGEST PRIZE IN THIS PART
The curtain pole
----------------

Moving a curtain pole upward and outward changes a room more than any other twenty-minute
job in this book. Most people have never considered that it could move at all.

The default installation puts the pole a few centimetres above the window frame and barely
wider than it. Both are wrong, and both are wrong in the same direction.

    Pole height — above the top of the window frame ...................... 15–20 cm · 6–8 in
    Or, better, below the ceiling or cornice .............................. 8–15 cm · 3–6 in
    Pole width — beyond the frame, each side ............................ 15–25 cm · 6–10 in

WHAT EACH ONE BUYS YOU

Height makes the window taller. The eye reads the whole curtained area as the window, so a
pole near the ceiling produces an apparently floor-to-ceiling opening rather than a hole in
a wall with fabric above it.

Width gives you back your daylight. Open curtains stack onto the wall instead of onto the
glass — which, as page 41 said, is typically a tenth of your window returned to you
permanently.


[ page 93 ]

The drop, and the four inches that matter
-----------------------------------------

A curtain goes to the floor. Almost the only exception is a radiator, and even that one is
arguable.

A curtain that stops short — at the sill, or a hand's width above the floor — draws a
horizontal line partway down the wall, and the eye reads that line as the bottom of the
room. The ceiling comes down with it. This is the same mechanism as the pole height working
in reverse, and it is the most common single error in the whole of window dressing.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | COLETTE’S RULE OF THE DROPPED CURTAIN
  | A curtain that stops above the floor makes the ceiling lower. Hung close to the ceiling
  | and dropped to the floor, it makes the window taller than it is.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


THE THREE FINISHES AT THE FLOOR

    FINISH                                     | CLEARANCE            | CHARACTER
    ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    Floating                                   | 1 cm above           | Crisp, practical, mo
    Kissing                                    | just touching        | The most elegant and
    Breaking                                   | 2–5 cm on the floor  | Soft, slightly langu

A deeper puddle than about 5 cm collects dust, trips people and needs arranging every time
it is moved. It photographs beautifully and lives badly.


THE RADIATOR QUESTION

A full-length curtain in front of a radiator traps the heat behind it and sends it up the
window. Three honest options: stop the curtain at the sill and accept the proportional cost;
hang full-length curtains that you draw only when the heating is off; or fit a blind or
shutter for warmth and hang the curtains as decorative panels that never close. The third is
what I do most often, and it keeps the proportions.

  [ MEASURE IN THREE PLACES ]
Floors are not level, especially in old buildings. Measure the drop at the left, the centre
and the right, and work to the shortest. A curtain cut to the longest measurement will drag
at one end.


[ page 94 ]

Fullness — how much fabric
--------------------------

This is the number that decides whether a curtain looks expensive, and it has nothing to do
with what the fabric cost.

Fullness is the ratio between the total width of fabric and the width of the pole it hangs
on. A curtain with little fullness hangs flat and reads as a sheet. A curtain with generous
fullness falls in deep, even folds and reads as a curtain.

    Bare minimum, will look thin .......................................... 1.5 × pole width
    Standard, and what most ready-made curtains give you ............................... 2 ×
    Generous — what makes the difference ............................................. 2.5 ×
    For sheers and very light fabric ............................................... 2.5–3 ×

WHY THIS IS THE HONEST PLACE TO SPEND MONEY

Between a cheap fabric at 2.5 times fullness and an expensive fabric at 1.5, the cheap one
looks better every time. Fullness is visible from across the room; fibre content is not. If
your budget is fixed, buy more of a plainer cloth rather than less of a finer one.

  [ FROM THE NOTEBOOK ]
My grandmother would not make a curtain under two and a half times. If a client could not
afford the fabric she would suggest a cheaper cloth rather than less of it, and she would
explain why. Forty-one years and she never once lost that argument, because she would hold
the two up side by side and let the client see it.


WORKING OUT WHAT TO BUY

   1. Measure the pole, not the window.
   2. Multiply by your fullness — use 2.5 unless there is a reason not to.
   3. Divide by the fabric width, and round up to whole widths. This gives the number of
      fabric widths.
   4. Multiply the number of widths by the drop, plus an allowance for hems and heading,
      plus a pattern repeat if the fabric has one.

The worksheet on page 96 does this arithmetic for you, with space for the measurements. If
you are having curtains made, take it with you.


[ page 95 ]

Headings, and what each one costs you
-------------------------------------

The heading is how the curtain attaches to the pole. It decides how the folds fall, how much
of the pole you see, and how much fabric you need.

    HEADING                            | FULLNESS        | READS AS        | NOTE
    ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    Pencil pleat                       | 2–2.5×          | Soft, gathered, | The commonest r
    Pinch pleat double or triple       | 2–2.5×          | Structured, tai | The most elegan
    Wave                               | 2–2.2×          | Modern, even, c | Needs its own t
    Eyelet                             | 1.5–2×          | Casual, deep so | Cheapest to mak
    Tab top or ties                    | 1.5–2×          | Rustic, light,  | Hard to draw —


THE PRACTICAL HIERARCHY

If the curtain is decorative and never drawn, any of them will do and you should choose on
looks. If it is drawn every evening, pinch pleat or wave will run smoothly and stay handsome
for years; tab top will annoy you within a month.

  [ LINING, WHICH MATTERS MORE THAN THE HEADING ]
A lined curtain hangs heavier, falls in better folds, blocks more light, insulates the
window and lasts far longer, because the lining takes the sun damage instead of the face
fabric. An unlined curtain is a panel of cloth. If you are choosing between a better fabric
unlined and a plainer fabric lined, take the lining every time. This is one of the four
things in this book I will tell you to spend on.

Interlining — a soft layer between face and lining — is a further step again, and it is what
gives the heavy, quiet, expensive drape you see in old houses. It roughly doubles the making
cost and it is genuinely transformative on a tall window.

THE MOST EXPENSIVE COMMON MISTAKE
The rug is too small
--------------------

Nearly every rug in nearly every home is one size too small, and a rug is one of the more
expensive things in a room. It is worth getting right once.

It happens for an understandable reason. A rug is bought in a shop, where it is hanging on a
rail or stacked in a pile, and at that moment it looks enormous. It arrives home, goes down
on the floor, and becomes a mat floating in the middle of the room with all the furniture
standing around it on bare boards.


WHAT A RUG IS ACTUALLY DOING

A rug is not floor covering. It is a boundary. It draws the edge of a zone and tells your
eye that everything standing on it belongs together. That is why a too-small rug is so
damaging: instead of grouping the furniture, it puts an object in the middle of the group
and pushes everything outward.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE TEST
  | If the furniture is standing around the rug rather than on it, the rug is too small. It
  | is doing the opposite of its job.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


THE THREE LEGITIMATE ARRANGEMENTS

   1. All legs on. Every piece of seating sits entirely on the rug, with a margin beyond.
      The most generous and the most expensive, and unbeatable in a large room.
   2. Front legs on. The front legs of every sofa and chair sit on the rug, the back legs on
      the floor. The standard answer for most sitting rooms, and it works because the rug
      still reaches under and connects everything.
   3. Deliberately floating — but sized to the furniture. A rug entirely inside the seating
      arrangement, but wide enough that it visibly relates to the sofa rather than looking
      dropped there. It should be at least as wide as the sofa.

What is not on that list is the common accident: a small rectangle under a coffee table
only, touching nothing else in the room.


THE MARGIN

Leave a border of bare floor between the rug and the walls, and keep it roughly even —
somewhere between 20 and 45 cm in a normal room. A rug that runs almost to the skirting
reads as failed fitted carpet.

buying a standard size and hoping.

Sixty to seventy-five centimetres beyond the table on every side is not aesthetic advice. A
chair whose back legs drop off the edge of a rug when someone sits down catches, tips, and
damages the rug. If you cannot fit the margin, do not put a rug under a dining table.

Before buying, mark the size out on the floor with masking tape and live with it for two
days. The same habit as page 86, and it costs nothing.

The heights that have to agree
------------------------------

Furniture is bought one piece at a time, over years, and nobody measures. Then the pieces
stand next to each other and quietly disagree.

These are the relationships that matter. Not absolute heights — relationships. A coffee
table is not too tall in the abstract; it is too tall for that sofa.

    RELATIONSHIP                                                      | SHOULD BE
    ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    Coffee table top vs. sofa seat height                             | level, or up to 5 cm
    Coffee table distance from the sofa                               | 35–45 cm — close eno
    Side table top vs. sofa arm                                       | level, or slightly b
    Dining chair seat vs. table top                                   | 28–32 cm between the
    Bedside table top vs. mattress top                                | level, or up to 5 cm
    Desk height, seated                                               | 72–76 cm
    Walkway between furniture                                         | at least 75 cm — 90
    Between sofa and television                                       | roughly 1.5–2.5 × th


THE THREE THAT CAUSE THE MOST COMPLAINTS

    - The coffee table that is too tall. Modern sofas have got lower and coffee tables have
      not. A table well above the seat height means you reach down and then up for a cup,
      and it blocks the view across the room.
    - The side table that is too low. If it sits well below the sofa arm you cannot put a
      glass down without leaning, and a lamp on it will be at the wrong height — page 30.
    - The walkway that is too narrow. Under about 75 cm people turn sideways without
      noticing they are doing it, and the room feels cramped for a reason nobody can name.

  [ BEFORE YOU BUY ANYTHING ]
Take three measurements with you: your sofa seat height, your sofa arm height, and the
narrowest walkway in the room. Almost every furniture-buying mistake is prevented by those
three numbers, and no showroom will give them to you.


[ page 100 ]

Pull it off the wall
--------------------

The instinct in a small room is to push every piece of furniture flat against a wall to
create space in the middle. It produces the opposite of space.

You met this as myth three on page 19. Here is what to do about it.

A room with all its furniture against the walls has a void in the centre, and a void does
not read as generous — it reads as a waiting area. Nothing is happening in the middle of the
room, and the middle of the room is what you see first.


THE FLOAT

    Pull a sofa off the wall by ........................................... 5–15 cm · 2–6 in
    Which costs you, in usable floor .......................... nothing you were standing on
Five to fifteen centimetres is enough. It is not about creating a walkway behind the sofa —
it is that the shadow gap between the furniture and the wall tells your eye the piece is a
free-standing object in a room, rather than built into its edge. The room acquires an
inside.


THREE MORE MOVES IN THE SAME FAMILY

    - Turn one chair off-axis. A single armchair at an angle to the grid breaks the sense
      that everything has been pushed to the perimeter. One is enough; two looks accidental.
    - Let something stand in front of a window. Not blocking the lower third — page 41 — but
      a low chair or a small table in front of glass is normal in French apartments and
      reads as confident. A window is not an altar.
    - Put the sofa across the room rather than along it, where the proportions allow. A sofa
      that divides a space defines two zones; a sofa against the long wall defines none.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE PRINCIPLE UNDERNEATH ALL FOUR
  | Furniture pressed flat to the walls describes the edges of a room. Furniture pulled
  | inward describes the room itself.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

Doors, thresholds and the vertical line
---------------------------------------

A room has a horizontal rhythm that most people never notice, and once you see it you cannot
stop.

Stand in a doorway and look along a wall. There is a line running round the room at the head
height of the doors — the top of the door frame, the top of the window frame, the picture
rail if there is one, often the top of a fitted cupboard. In a well-proportioned room these
align, or nearly do, and the room feels settled without anybody being able to say why.

In most homes something breaks that line: a shelf at an odd height, a wall cupboard, a
boxed-in pipe, a television bracket. The line is what your eye was using to organise the
wall, and the break is read as disorder.


HOW TO USE IT

    - Align the tops of things, not the bottoms. Two bookcases of different heights look
      like an error; two units whose tops align look like a decision, even when they are
      different widths.
    - Put nothing new above the door line unless it goes all the way up. A shelf floating
      just above door height is the single most disruptive thing you can add to a wall.
      Either keep below the line, or run to the ceiling.
    - Use the line for the curtain pole where you can. If the pole lands close to the door
      head, everything in the room agrees at once.


THE THRESHOLD

Where one room meets another, the floor either continues or it changes. A change of flooring
at every doorway chops a small flat into a series of cupboards, which is the law on page 70.
If you cannot change the floors, you can at least keep the colour continuous across the
threshold and let one run of skirting carry through.

  [ THE DOORWAY VIEW ]
Every doorway frames a view. Stand in each one in your home and look at what it frames. In
most flats at least one doorway is aimed squarely at the side of a wardrobe or the back of a
television. Moving one piece of furniture so that a doorway frames something worth seeing is
free and it is the sort of thing decorators are paid for.


[ page 102 ]

THE ONE I AM ASKED ABOUT MOST
Every room needs one empty corner
---------------------------------

I have been promising this page since Part One. It is the shortest rule in the book and the
one people find hardest to obey.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | COLETTE’S RULE OF THE EMPTY CORNER
  | Every room needs one corner where nothing happens.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

One corner with no furniture, no lamp, no plant, no stack of anything. Ideally unlit, or lit
only by whatever spills into it. Genuinely empty.


WHY

Because a room in which every corner is doing something has nowhere for the eye to rest.
Every direction you look, there is an object asking to be considered. It is tiring in a way
that is difficult to identify, because nothing in the room is wrong — there is simply no
pause in it.

The empty corner is the pause. It is also what makes everything else in the room read as
chosen: when three corners are occupied and one is not, the occupied ones look deliberate.
When all four are occupied, they look like the places things ended up.


THIS IS ALSO WHY I TOLD YOU TO LEAVE ONE CORNER DARK

Page 32 said to get light into every corner you can, except one. Same rule, seen from the
lighting side. A room lit evenly into all four corners is a lit box; a room with three lit
corners and one in shadow has depth, because the eye has something to read the light
against.


WHAT COUNTS AS EMPTY

Bare floor and bare wall meeting. A single tall plant in an otherwise unused corner is a
borderline case and I would allow it — it is one object, it moves, and it does not ask to be
considered. A chair with a coat on it, a drying rack, a stack of boxes, a floor lamp and a
side table: not empty.

  [ FROM THE NOTEBOOK ]
My grandmother called it le coin qui respire — the corner that breathes. She said you could
tell how a household was doing by whether it still had one.

CHAPTER FIVE
Why texture comes last, and decides everything
----------------------------------------------

Texture is fifth in the order of operations because it depends on everything above it. It is
the first thing anybody notices when they walk in.

That is not a contradiction. The order of operations is about what you fix first; it says
nothing about what a visitor perceives first. And what a visitor perceives first, before
they have registered a single colour or noticed a lamp, is whether the room feels hard or
soft, warm or cool, dressed or bare. That reading happens in well under a second and it is
almost entirely a reading of surface.


WHY IT HAS TO BE LAST

Because texture is read by light. The whole visible difference between velvet and linen is
how each one handles a beam: velvet has a pile that absorbs light down its length and
catches it across, linen has a slubbed weave that throws tiny highlights and shadows in
every direction. Change the light and you have changed the texture, exactly as you changed
the colour in Part Three.

So a room lit from one height has, in a real sense, no texture at all. Flat overhead light
strikes every surface at the same angle and produces almost no shadow, which is precisely
the information your eye uses to tell one material from another. This is why a room can be
full of beautiful fabrics and still look like a catalogue page: the light is not letting you
feel any of them.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | WORTH KNOWING BEFORE YOU SPEND ANYTHING
  | Texture is invisible without raking light. A fifteen-dollar lamp on the floor will do
  | more for the linen you already own than a better linen will.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


WHAT THIS PART IS CAREFUL ABOUT

This is where the money goes. Sofas, rugs, curtains, upholstery — the big purchases all live
in this part, and most of them are made once a decade. So I am going to be specific about
which of them reward spending and which do not, and page 122 lists what I would not buy at
all.

WHY FOUR AND NOT THREE OR FIVE

This is my rule and not a law, so here is the reasoning rather than an assertion. Two

different years, and four different families is the minimum that convincingly suggests that
history.

Five and six are fine. Nothing goes wrong above four. The rule is a floor, not a ceiling,
and the page that matters is the one after next — because the missing family is nearly
always the third.

WEIGHT MATTERS MORE THAN FIBRE

A heavy cloth hangs in deep folds and reads as substantial; a light cloth of the same fibre
hangs limp and reads as cheap. This is why a thin linen curtain can look worse than a heavy
cotton one despite costing three times as much.

When you are choosing fabric, hold a length of it up and let it fall. If it drops into a few
deep folds it is heavy enough for curtains. If it clings to itself in many small creases, it
is a dress fabric.

  [ CONTRAST WITHIN THE FAMILY ]
The cure for a four-soft-things room is not fewer soft things. It is soft things that
disagree: a smooth linen against a nubbly wool against a coarse jute. Same family, three
completely different surfaces. Page 115.


[ page 109 ]

Why linen reads expensive
-------------------------

It is worth understanding, because the reason is not the price — and once you know it you
can get most of the effect from cheaper cloth.

Linen is spun from flax. The fibre is not uniform: it varies in thickness along its length,
so a linen yarn has thick and thin places, and a woven linen cloth has a surface of small
irregularities. That irregularity is called slub, and it is the whole thing.

A slubbed surface catches light at hundreds of slightly different angles. So a linen curtain
in raking light is never one flat tone — it is a field of tiny highlights and shadows that
shifts as you move. Your eye reads that variability as depth, and reads depth as quality.

A perfectly uniform synthetic of the same colour reflects light identically across its whole
surface. It has one tone. Your eye reads one tone as flat, and flat as cheap — not because
it knows the price, but because uniformity is what machines produce and irregularity is what
natural fibre produces.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE GENERAL PRINCIPLE, WHICH IS WORTH MORE THAN THE LINEN
  | Irregularity reads as expensive. Uniformity reads as manufactured. This is true of
  | cloth, timber, glaze, stone and plaster equally.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


HOW TO GET THE EFFECT WITHOUT THE PRICE

    - A linen blend. Linen-cotton or linen-viscose keeps most of the slub at a fraction of
      the cost, and creases less.
    - Any irregular weave. Slub cotton, basketweave, herringbone, bouclé. It is the surface
      variation you are buying, not the flax.
    - Raking light. As on page 106 — the effect is created by the light. Cheap cloth lit
      from the side beats expensive cloth lit from above.


AND THE HONEST COST OF LINEN

It creases the moment anyone sits on it, and the creases do not fall out. If you want linen
you have to want the creased version, because that is the only version there is. People who
buy linen and then resent it have bought an idea of linen from a photograph taken before
anybody sat down.


[ page 110 ]

FAMILY TWO
Wood — grain, finish and age
----------------------------

My husband is a joiner, so I have been corrected about wood for twenty years. Three things
matter and the third is the one nobody mentions.


ONE · SOLID, VENEER, OR NEITHER

    - Solid timber can be sanded and refinished, sometimes many times. It moves with
      humidity, which is why solid tabletops are built to allow it. It lasts generations.
    - Veneer is a thin layer of real timber over a stable board. It looks identical when
      new, it is more stable than solid, and it can usually be refinished once or not at
      all. Good veneer on good board is a perfectly honest product.
    - Printed foil or laminate is a photograph of wood. It cannot be repaired, it chips at
      the edges, and once chipped it announces exactly what it is. This is the one to avoid
      in anything you intend to keep.

How to tell: look at an edge and at the end of a leg. Solid shows continuous grain wrapping
round. Veneer shows a fine seam. Print shows a repeat — find the same knot twice and you
have found a photograph.


TWO · THE FINISH DECIDES THE TEXTURE

Oiled timber is matte, open-pored and warm to touch; it marks, and it can be repaired with
more oil. Lacquered timber is sealed and shiny; it resists marks and cannot be locally
repaired. Waxed is between the two. An oiled oak table and a lacquered oak table are,
texturally, two different materials.


THREE · THE THING NOBODY MENTIONS

Mixing wood tones is not a mistake. Almost every guide tells you to match your woods, and it
is the single worst piece of received advice in this family, because it produces exactly the
matched-set problem on page 114.

A room with an oak floor, a walnut table and a pine shelf reads as a room that accumulated.
A room where every timber matches reads as a showroom set, and it is achievable only by
buying everything at once from one place — which is precisely the impression you do not
want.

  [ WHAT TO ACTUALLY MATCH ]
Not the species. The undertone — page 50. Warm timbers together, cool timbers together. An
orange-toned pine beside a grey-limed oak is the clash people mistake for a species problem.


[ page 111 ]

FAMILY THREE
The missing family
------------------

In the great majority of rooms I have worked in, this is the one that was not there. It is
also the cheapest of the four to correct.

Ceramic and stone do something no other family does: they are cool and dense. They take
light and hold it rather than absorbing or scattering it, so their shadows have hard edges.
In a room of fabric and timber — both soft-edged, both warm — a single unglazed pot
introduces a completely different kind of surface, and the whole room sharpens.


WHAT COUNTS

    - Unglazed or matte-glazed pottery. A pot, a jug, a bowl, a lamp base. Matte and
      slightly rough is doing far more work than high-gloss, which reads closer to glass.
    - Plaster. A limewashed wall is this family — page 68 — which is one reason those walls
      feel so different from painted ones.
    - Stone and marble. A worktop, a hearth, a small offcut used as a board or a tray.
    - Terracotta and brick. Exposed brick counts. So does a single terracotta pot, which is
      the cheapest entry into this family there is.
    - Concrete and cement in small quantities.


WHY IT IS THE CHEAPEST FIX IN THE BOOK

Because you need one object, and it can be very small. A single unglazed pot on a shelf
changes the count from three families to four. You do not need a stone worktop. Charity
shops and garden centres are full of this family for the price of a coffee, and a plain
terracotta plant pot — the ordinary kind — is both this family and the container for the
next.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE TEST, FOR ANY ROOM THAT FEELS SOFT AND SLIGHTLY SHAPELESS
  | Count the things in the room that are cold to the touch. If the answer is none, you have
  | found what is missing.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

One caution, since it is the family most often bought badly: high-gloss white ceramic reads
as bathroom fittings rather than as material. Matte, unglazed, or a glaze with some
variation in it — the irregularity rule from page 109 applies here exactly as it does to
cloth.


[ page 112 ]

FAMILY FOUR
Something living, honestly
--------------------------

A plant is the only thing in a room that changes shape over months. That is its entire job,
and it is a job nothing else can do.

Every other object in your home is fixed. The plant grows, leans towards the window, drops a
leaf, needs water. It introduces irregularity that is not designed — which, by the rule on
page 109, is exactly what reads as real.


CHOOSE BY THE LIGHT YOU HAVE, NOT BY THE LEAF YOU LIKE

This is the whole of it, and it is why so many people conclude they cannot keep plants. A
plant sold as an indoor plant is not therefore suited to a north-facing corner three metres
from the window.

    - Bright light, near a south or west window: most of what is for sale will be happy.
    - Moderate light, a few metres back or a north window: choose deliberately — pothos,
      philodendron, spider plant, cast iron plant, dracaena.
    - Genuinely low light, an internal corner: the tolerant survivors — snake plant, ZZ
      plant, aspidistra. Expect slow growth and accept it.
    - No natural light at all: nothing will thrive long-term. Use branches in water and
      change them, or rotate a plant in and out of a brighter room.

  [ THE CLAIM I WILL NOT REPEAT ]
You will read that houseplants purify indoor air. The famous experiment behind that claim
was run in small sealed chambers, and later analysis of what it implies at realistic room
densities suggests the effect is negligible compared with simply opening a window. Have
plants because a room with something alive in it is better than a room without. Do not buy
them as air filtration.


THE CHEAPEST VERSION, WHICH I USE CONSTANTLY

Branches in a jug of water. Bare in winter, in leaf in spring, replaced whenever you like.
They need no light, cost nothing, and satisfy this family completely. My grandmother kept
beech or hornbeam in water on the windowsill from October to March and never owned a
houseplant in her life.

Metal and glass — the seasoning
-------------------------------

These are not a fifth family. They are a seasoning, and the quantity is the whole question.

Metal and glass are both specular — they reflect light directly rather than scattering it.
That gives them enormous visual weight for their size: one brass lamp draws more attention
than a whole wool rug. A little is lively. A lot is a hotel lobby.

SHOULD METALS MATCH?

No — for the same reason woods should not. Two metals in a room is normal and looks

the second appear two or three times, so it reads as a decision rather than an oversight.

What genuinely should agree is temperature: warm metals with warm metals, cool with cool.
Brass and chrome in the same sightline is the combination that looks accidental, and it is
the undertone rule again.

  | THE QUANTITY RULE
  | Metal and glass should be the smallest family in the room by area. If they are not, the
  | room will read as commercial whatever else you do.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

Glass has one additional property worth using: it is the only material that disappears. A
glass table in a small room takes up its volume without taking up its visual weight, which
is occasionally exactly the right answer — though it is also a fingerprint magnet and it
gives you nothing texturally.

THE MOST EXPENSIVE HABIT IN DECORATING
Nothing that came in a set
--------------------------

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | COLETTE’S RULE OF THE MATCHED SET
  | Nothing that arrived as a set will ever look like it was chosen.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

A three-piece suite. A bedroom set. A dining table with its six matching chairs and the
sideboard that goes with them. All bought together, all delivered on the same day.

The problem is not that any individual piece is bad. It is that the set announces its own
origin. Every object in it shares one timber, one finish, one proportion, one design
language, because it was designed to be sold together — and a room furnished this way tells
anyone walking in that all of its decisions were made in a single afternoon by somebody
else.


WHAT A ROOM WITHOUT A SET LOOKS LIKE

A sofa from one place. Two armchairs that do not match each other. A coffee table that was
somebody's grandmother's. A floor lamp bought years later. None of them agree on species,
period or price, and they all agree on temperature and scale — and the room looks as though
it was assembled by a person with opinions over a decade, because it was.


HOW TO BREAK A SET YOU ALREADY OWN

You do not have to replace it. You have to interrupt it.

    - Remove one piece. Take the matching armchair out of the room and put something else in
      its place. One dissenting object breaks the pattern for the whole set.
    - Change what is on it. Different cushions on the sofa from the ones it came with. A
      cloth or a run of books over a matching sideboard.
    - Separate the pieces. A set reads as a set when it is together. Move the sideboard to
      the hall and it becomes a piece of furniture again.
    - Add the two families it lacks. A set is almost always one timber and one fabric.
      Ceramic and something living will do more than any rearrangement.


WHERE I WOULD SPEND, IF YOU ARE STARTING FROM NOTHING

One good sofa, bought alone. Then everything else acquired slowly and separately, secondhand
where possible. A room furnished over three years for the price of one suite will look
better than the suite, and every piece in it will be something you chose.


[ page 115 ]

Contrast inside one family
--------------------------

The four-family rule gets you to a room that works. This page is what separates a room that
works from a room people remember.

Having all four families is a floor, not a ceiling. The next move is contrast within each
one — because the eye can distinguish surfaces far more finely than four categories, and a
room that only satisfies the minimum still reads a little schematic.


WITHIN THE WOVEN FAMILY

The most common failure, because it is where the volume is. A room with four soft things
that are all medium-weight, medium-smooth cotton has one texture repeated four times.

Put a smooth cloth against a coarse one: a fine linen cushion on a nubbly wool sofa, over a
jute rug. Three surfaces from one family, and every one of them feels different under a
hand. That is a richer room than four different families all of the same smoothness.


WITHIN THE WOODEN FAMILY

Oiled against lacquered. Sawn against planed. A smooth walnut table beside a rattan chair
beside a rough-sawn shelf. Page 110 — do not match your woods.


WITHIN CERAMIC AND STONE

Matte against glossy. A rough unglazed terracotta pot next to a smooth glazed bowl is a
genuine contrast, and both are cheap.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE REFINEMENT
  | Four families is the minimum. Two surfaces within each family is the difference between
  | a room that works and a room somebody wants to sit down in.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


THE HAND TEST

Walk round the room and actually touch ten things. Not look — touch. If more than about four
of them feel substantially the same under your palm, the room is texturally thinner than it
looks, and you now know exactly which surfaces to change.

This is the test I use in a room that is bothering me and I cannot say why. It works because
your hand has not been trained by photographs, and your eye has.


[ page 116 ]

The curtain is the largest texture in the room
----------------------------------------------

Part Four treated the curtain as proportion. It is also, in most rooms, the single biggest
area of fabric you own — larger than the sofa.

Measure it. A pair of curtains at two and a half times fullness across a normal window is
several square metres of cloth, hanging vertically, in folds, usually next to your main
light source. Nothing else in the room is that much of one material, that well lit, at that
angle.

Which means the curtain is doing more texturally than anything else in the room, and it is
almost always chosen on colour alone.


WHAT TO CHOOSE IT FOR INSTEAD

    - Weight, first. Page 108 — hold a length up and let it fall. Deep folds or small
      creases tells you everything.
    - Surface, second. A slubbed or irregular weave against a window is the single best-lit
      texture in your home, because it is side-lit for most of the day. This is where linen
      genuinely earns its price.
    - Colour, third. Genuinely third. A curtain in a plain, quiet colour and a beautiful
      cloth beats a striking colour in a flat one, every time.

  [ FROM THE NOTEBOOK ]
My grandmother chose cloth by holding it against the window of the room it was going in,
never in the shop, and never flat — always gathered in her hand, because a curtain is never
seen flat. She said a fabric that is only beautiful lying down is a tablecloth.


THE SHEER, WHICH IS THE UNDERRATED ONE

A fine sheer behind a heavier curtain does three jobs at once: it diffuses direct sun into
soft light — page 25 — it gives daytime privacy without blocking the view, and texturally it
puts a second, completely different surface at the window. A heavy wool curtain over a fine
linen sheer is two contrasting textures in the best-lit place in the room, and it is why the
arrangement has survived for two centuries.

THE HONEST PAGE
Cushions and throws
-------------------

This is the category people reach for when a room is not working, and it is almost never the
answer — but it is not nothing either, so let me be exact.


WHY IT IS THE WRONG INSTINCT

A cushion is small, soft, and belongs to a family the room already has too much of. If your
sitting room is dissatisfying, the cause is on one of the previous four parts' pages — the
lighting height, the hanging height, the missing third family — and a cushion addresses none
of them. This is the purchase that feels like progress and produces none, which is precisely
why it is sold so hard.


WHAT CUSHIONS GENUINELY DO

    - Introduce contrast within the woven family. Page 115. A coarse wool cushion on a
      smooth sofa is real texture work.
    - Break a matched set. Page 114. The cheapest possible interruption.
    - Carry the accent colour into a third place. Page 69.

Upholstery — where the money should go
--------------------------------------

A sofa is the most expensive thing in most sitting rooms and the thing people know least
about buying. Four things decide whether it is still good in fifteen years.


ONE · THE FRAME

Kiln-dried hardwood, jointed and glued, is what lasts. Softwood, stapled, or particle board
will loosen and there is nothing to be done about it. Ask what the frame is made of; a shop
that cannot tell you is telling you.


TWO · WHETHER IT CAN BE RECOVERED

This is the question that decides everything else. A sofa with a good frame can be
reupholstered two or three times across fifty years, and each time it becomes a new sofa for
a fraction of the price. A sofa built so that the cover cannot come off is a disposable
object however much it cost.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE ONE QUESTION TO ASK IN THE SHOP
  | Can this be reupholstered? If the answer is no, you are not buying furniture. You are
  | renting it slowly.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


THREE · THE RUB TEST ON THE FABRIC

Upholstery fabric is tested for abrasion resistance. In Europe the figure is the Martindale
count; in the United States it is Wyzenbeek double rubs. Higher is more durable. As working
bands:

    Light domestic — occasional chair ............................. 10,000–15,000 Martindale
    General domestic — a normal sofa ......................................... 15,000–25,000
    Heavy domestic — family, daily, pets ........................................... 25,000+

FOUR · REMOVABLE COVERS

Washable loose covers on a sofa that gets real use is the difference between a piece you
keep and a piece you replace. It is the single most practical thing on this page.

Patina — the thing you cannot buy
---------------------------------

Everything in this part has been about choosing materials. This page is about what happens
to them afterwards, which is the part that cannot be bought and is most of what you are
actually responding to in rooms you admire.

Patina is what use does to a surface. Brass darkens in the places hands touch it and stays
bright elsewhere. Oiled oak deepens in colour and picks up small marks. Linen softens and
its creases become permanent and soft rather than sharp. Leather lightens where it is sat
on. Stone wears hollow in a doorway over a century.

In every case the material is being changed by its own use, so the marks are a true record
of how the object has been lived with. That is why patina reads as authenticity and why no
manufactured distressing quite convinces — a factory-applied wear pattern is in the wrong
places, because nobody's hand has been there.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | WHICH IS THE PRACTICAL POINT
  | Choose materials that get better as they are used, not materials that only get worse.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


MATERIALS THAT IMPROVE

Unlacquered brass and copper. Oiled and waxed timber. Linen. Full-grain leather. Wool.
Unglazed terracotta. Lime plaster. Stone.


MATERIALS THAT ONLY DETERIORATE

Lacquered and foil-wrapped anything. Chrome. Printed laminate. Polyester velvet. Viscose.
High-gloss paint on woodwork that gets touched. Each of these begins at its best possible
condition, and every subsequent day is a small loss.


WHAT FOLLOWS FROM THIS, PRACTICALLY

It is a strong argument for buying fewer, better, repairable things, and it is the reason
the secondhand version of a good object is frequently preferable to the new one — somebody
else has already done the first ten years of the work for you, and the price reflects the
opposite.

It is also the honest argument against the aesthetic this book is working in. Rooms that
look like this take time as well as decisions, and no amount of correct choosing on a
Saturday produces a surface that has been touched for thirty years. That is not a reason not
to start. It is a reason to buy things that will still be here in thirty years.

WHAT NOT TO BUY
Texture and soft furnishing
---------------------------


A MATCHING CUSHION SET

Four identical cushions in the fabric the sofa came with. It is the matched-set problem
bought deliberately, usually by somebody trying to fix the room. Page 117.


CUSHION COVERS WITHOUT INSERTS

Or worse, covers with the polyester insert that comes free. The insert is the part that
decides whether it looks expensive, and it is the part being economised on. Buy feather
inserts two to five centimetres larger than the cover and keep the covers you already have.


VISCOSE RUGS SOLD AS “ART SILK” OR “BANANA SILK”

Beautiful in the shop, and they mark with a drop of water, show every footprint, and
deteriorate exactly where people walk. This is the only rug fibre I would tell you to avoid
outright.


A SOFA WHOSE COVER CANNOT BE REMOVED OR REPLACED

Page 118. Whatever it costs, it is a disposable object, and a decade from now the choice
will be to live with it or throw it away.


FAUX-DISTRESSED ANYTHING

Factory-applied wear is in the wrong places, because no hand has been there. It reads as
costume next to a single genuinely worn object. If you want patina, buy the secondhand
version of a good thing — page 120.


SCENTED CANDLES AS A DECORATING PURCHASE

Not because there is anything wrong with them, but because they are sold in this aisle as
though they were a texture. They are a consumable. They do not change a room and they run
out, which is the business model.

  [ WHAT TO BUY INSTEAD, IN ORDER ]
One unglazed pot, from a charity shop or a garden centre. Feather inserts for the cushions
you already own. A rug pad for the rug you already own. And then nothing else in this
category until the lighting and the hanging heights are corrected, because a room fails
those two before it ever fails on texture.


[ page 123 ]

THE QUIET PART
Who profits from furniture that cannot be repaired
--------------------------------------------------

Go and look underneath your sofa. Then look at the back of a chest of drawers. You are
looking for the same thing in both places: whether anybody expected this object to be taken
apart.

A hardwood frame with proper joints can be reupholstered indefinitely. A stapled softwood
frame cannot. A drawer on wooden runners can be repaired by anybody; a drawer in a particle-
board carcass whose runners have torn out of the board cannot be repaired by anyone at all,
because there is nothing left to fix it to.

The second kind is not badly made. It is precisely made, to a specification, and the
specification includes the end.

Nobody decided this in a room somewhere. It is quieter and sadder than that.

There is no money in a sofa that a family recovers twice and hands on. There is a great deal
of money in a sofa that is pleasant for six years and impossible to repair in the seventh,
and in the replacement, and in the one after that. No one has to intend it. A market that
competes on the price of the first purchase will arrive here on its own, because the cost of
the second purchase is invisible at the moment of the first.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE TEST THAT COSTS NOTHING
  | Before you buy anything large, ask what would happen if one part of it broke. If the
  | answer is that you would throw the whole thing away, you have learned the price
  | properly.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


WHAT I AM NOT SAYING

I am not saying buy expensive. Some very expensive furniture is built exactly this way, and
a secondhand hardwood-framed armchair with a tired cover is usually cheaper than a new flat-
pack one and will outlive it by forty years. The division is not price. It is whether the
thing was built to be opened.

And if the flat-pack one is what you can afford this year, buy it without apology. Then know
what it is, keep it going as long as you can, and spend differently when you are next able
to. That is a completely respectable way to furnish a home.

AND THE SIX THAT DO NOT NEED THEIR OWN PAGE

    - Texture is invisible without raking light. A lamp on the floor does more for the linen

CHAPTER SIX
What you actually own
---------------------

A rented flat is not a smaller version of the problem. It is a different problem, and it
deserves its own chapter rather than a paragraph of apology at the end.

Here is the difficulty stated plainly. The four corrections in this book are light, height,
texture and colour. In a rented home you generally do not own two of them. The colour on the
walls is not yours. The fittings in the ceiling are not yours. In many flats the floor is
not yours either, and neither is the thing screwed above the window.

So the advice most renters are given is: wait. Wait until you buy something. In the
meantime, live in beige and do not make a fuss.

I think that is a small cruelty, and it is also wrong. I spent fourteen years working almost
entirely in rented flats, and the correction that does the most in any room — light at three
heights, which is all of Part Two — requires no permission from anybody. You plug it in.
Height, which is Part Four, needs one removable fixing and sometimes not even that. Texture,
which is Part Five, is entirely portable and goes with you to the next flat.

That is three of the four corrections available to a tenant on the first evening.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE RENTER’S ARITHMETIC
  | You do not own the paint. You do own the light, the height of everything hung, and every
  | texture in the room. That is three corrections out of four, and the one you are missing
  | is the one that matters least.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

Colour is the missing one, and Part Three already dealt with it honestly on page 72 — a room
can be recoloured entirely in fabric, and in a rented flat that is usually the only sensible
route anyway, because fabric moves house and paint does not.

What follows is the practical version. What to ask for, what to do without asking, what
genuinely works, and — this part matters — what is sold to renters that does not work and
will cost you your deposit.


[ page 127 ]

Read the lease before you despair
---------------------------------

Almost every tenant I worked with believed their lease forbade more than it actually did.
Nobody had read it. They were living by a rumour.

I am not a lawyer and this book is not legal advice, and tenancy law differs in every
country and often in every city. What I can tell you is what I found again and again when a
client finally took the document out of the drawer and we read it together at the kitchen
table.


WHAT THE DOCUMENT USUALLY ACTUALLY SAYS

    - No alterations without consent — which is a rule about consent, not a refusal. Consent
      that is never asked for is never given.
    - Return the property in the same condition, reasonable wear excepted. That is the real
      test in most disputes: did you change it, and can it be put back?
    - Nothing whatsoever about lamps, rugs, cushions, plants, mirrors leaning against a
      wall, or what colour any of it is. This is the important discovery. The largest
      corrections in this book are not mentioned in your lease at all, because they are not
      alterations. They are your belongings.


THE TWO QUESTIONS THAT DECIDE EVERYTHING

   1. Does it make a hole? Holes are the whole argument. Nearly every dispute I ever saw
      between a tenant and a landlord over decoration was about a hole or a mark.
   2. If it does, can it be made invisible in an afternoon by somebody competent? A filled
      and touched-in screw hole in an ordinary painted wall is the most routine repair there
      is. A ripped patch of plaster is not.

One thing I do know, because it is French law and I was asked about it constantly: here a
room only counts as a habitable main room if it has at least nine square metres of floor
with a ceiling of at least 2.20 m, or failing that a volume of at least twenty cubic metres.
A “bedroom” that fails those figures is not a bedroom, whatever the advertisement said. Your
own country will have its equivalent, and it is worth ten minutes of an evening to find out
what it is.

  [ BEFORE YOU DO ANYTHING ]
If your tenancy came with an inventory or a check-in report — in France the état des lieux,
elsewhere a schedule of condition — find it and read the section on walls. That single page
tells you exactly what you will be measured against when you leave, which is more useful
than any opinion of mine.


[ page 128 ]

The five permissions worth asking for
-------------------------------------

Tenants almost never ask. Landlords are almost never asked. I have watched a polite two-
sentence email get a whole flat repainted at the owner’s expense.

Ask for these five, in this order of likelihood. Ask for them in writing, keep the reply,
and ask for one at a time rather than presenting a list that reads like a renovation.

   1. To change the light fittings, keeping the originals. The most likely yes of the five,
      because it costs the owner nothing and the flat is improved. Say that you will store
      the originals and reinstate them.
   2. To put up a curtain pole. Two brackets, four screws. Frame it as improving the
      property, because it does — a pole stays behind and the next tenant inherits it.
   3. To hang pictures with proper fixings. Offer the number. “About six screw holes, filled
      and touched in when I leave.” A number is reassuring; “some pictures” is not.
   4. To repaint, at your own expense, in a colour you name. Attach the colour. Many owners
      say yes to this if you promise white-back at the end; many more say yes and never ask
      for white-back, because a freshly painted flat lets faster.
   5. To replace the floor covering. The least likely, and usually not worth spending your
      credit on. A large rug does most of it. See page 136.


THE EMAIL THAT WORKS

  [ COPY THIS ]
Dear —, I would like to make one small improvement at my own cost: replacing the ceiling
fitting in the sitting room with a plain fabric shade. I will keep the original fitting and
put it back at the end of the tenancy, and there will be no change to the wiring. May I go
ahead?

Three things make it work: it is one thing, not five; it is at your expense; and it is
reversible, and says so. Every refusal I ever saw was a refusal of risk, not of decoration.


[ page 129 ]

Light, which needs nobody’s permission
--------------------------------------

This is the page that changes a rented flat, and it is the reason Part Two came second in
this book and not last.

The single overhead fitting is the defining feature of rented housing everywhere in the
world. It is there because it is cheap to install and impossible to break. It produces
exactly the light described on page 29: one source, at the worst possible height, with
nothing to soften it. Every rented flat I ever walked into was suffering it.

And every one of them could be corrected the same evening, by a tenant, with no tools, no
permission and nothing screwed to anything.


THE PLAN, UNCHANGED FROM PART TWO

    Table height — a lamp at roughly seated eye level ............................ 2 sources
    Floor level — one light low, in a corner, behind something .................... 1 source
    Overhead — off, most of the time ............................................. 0 sources

THE RENTER’S COMPLICATIONS, AND THEIR ANSWERS

    - There are no sockets where the lamps should go. Then the flat is telling you to use a
      floor lamp that can stand behind a chair, and an extension lead run along the skirting
      and taped down. I have never once regretted an extension lead. I have often regretted
      a lamp placed where the socket was.
    - No switch by the door, so you walk into darkness. A plug-in remote socket, or bulbs
      that come on to a timer at dusk. This costs very little and it is the difference
      between using the plan and abandoning it in week two.
    - Nowhere to put a table lamp at all. A clamp lamp on a shelf edge, a bookcase or a door
      frame. It is the trade’s own solution and it is not ugly if the fitting is plain.

Three lamps and a fortnight of switching the big light off will do more for a rented room
than any amount of paint you are not allowed to use.


[ page 130 ]

The bulb is the only free renovation
------------------------------------

If you do one thing in this part, do this one. It takes ten minutes, it costs less than
lunch, it is entirely reversible, and it changes the colour of every surface you own.

Landlords buy bulbs in packs. Nobody selects them. They arrive in the flat as a consequence
of a hardware shop having a special offer, and they are almost always the same: cool,
bright, cheap, and poor at showing colour.

Go round the flat tonight and read what is written on the bulbs. You are looking for two
numbers, both explained on pages 34 and 36.

    Colour temperature, for every room you sit in ................................... 2700 K
    Colour rendering, the number after CRI or Ra ....................................... 90+
    Brightness, in lumens, for a table lamp beside a chair ...................... 400–600 lm

WHY THIS IS THE RENTER’S CORRECTION ABOVE ALL OTHERS

Because you cannot change the colour of the walls, and the bulb changes the colour of the
walls. A landlord white under a 4000 K bulb is the grey-blue that everybody complains about.
The same wall under 2700 K at CRI 90 is a warm, quiet, entirely acceptable white. The paint
did not move. The light did.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | COLETTE’S RULE OF THE RENTED BULB
  | In a flat you may not paint, the bulb is the paint.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


THE BOX

Put the landlord’s bulbs in a box, label it, and keep it at the back of a cupboard for the
whole tenancy. On the last day they go back in. This is the same box that will hold the
original light shades, the original curtain, and anything else you swapped — and it is the
single most useful object in a rented home. Page 142 is about that box.


[ page 131 ]

The ceiling light you may not remove
------------------------------------

Sometimes you are refused, or there is no fitting to change — a bare bulb on a flex, or a
sealed plastic dish, or a row of spotlights you cannot reach.

Take them in order of how much they can be helped.


A BARE BULB ON A FLEX

The easiest of all, because the flex will take any shade with a standard ring and the whole
operation is reversible in a minute. Buy a plain paper or fabric drum, hang it, keep the
packaging. If you want the light to fall down onto a table rather than sideways into
everyone’s eyes, choose one closed at the top; if the room needs bounce off the ceiling,
choose one open at both ends. Page 38 explains which does what.


A SEALED DISH OR A PLASTIC BOWL

You cannot improve its shape, so improve its behaviour. A warm, high-rendering bulb inside
it is the whole correction. Then dim it — a bulb you can dim from a remote or a phone costs
little and needs no electrician, because the dimming happens inside the bulb rather than in
the wall. That single change turns a hard overhead light into a background wash that you can
leave on at a quarter and stop resenting.


A ROW OF DOWNLIGHTS YOU CANNOT CHANGE

These are the hardest, and honesty is better than cleverness: you will not make them
flattering. Warm, dimmable replacements where the fitting allows it, then use the lamps and
leave the row off. Downlights light floors. You do not live on the floor.

  [ THE SAFETY LINE ]
Never put a fabric or paper shade over a bulb that runs hot, and never exceed the wattage
printed inside the fitting. With modern LED bulbs this is rarely an issue because they run
cool, but a halogen or an old incandescent in a close-fitting shade is a genuine fire risk,
not a decorating question. If anything here means touching wiring rather than unscrewing a
bulb, it is an electrician’s job and your landlord’s bill.


[ page 132 ]

Curtains without a drill
------------------------

Part Four called the curtain pole the largest prize in the room. In a rented flat it is also
the hardest, and I am going to be straight with you about what works.


WHAT IS USUALLY ALREADY THERE

Look above the window before you buy anything. Most rented flats have either a track screwed
to the ceiling, a pole at frame height, or a plain batten. A track can be re-hung with
longer curtains. A pole at frame height cannot be moved without holes — but it can be
lengthened at the ends, and page 92 explains why width often matters more than height.


TENSION RODS — THE HONEST ASSESSMENT

A tension rod is held up by friction alone, inside the window reveal. It works for one job:
a light unlined curtain or a café panel across a narrow window. It fails, and falls, with
lined curtains, with a wide span, and with any fabric that is pulled hard twice a day. I
have seen a rod in a reveal hold for years and I have seen one come down in a week. Weight
and width decide it.

    Tension rod, sensible span ................................................ under 120 cm
    Tension rod, sensible curtain ............................................. unlined only

THE COMPROMISE I USED MOST OFTEN

Two screws. That is what a proper pole costs you — two brackets, four small screws, in solid
plaster or a timber frame, filled with a fingertip of filler on the day you leave. It is the
best value in holes anywhere in this book, and it is the permission most worth asking for on
page 128.

  [ FROM THE NOTEBOOK ]
“A curtain hung too low says the room is rented. A curtain hung high says nothing at all —
and saying nothing is the goal.”


[ page 133 ]

Hanging things without nails
----------------------------

Adhesive fixings are the renter’s great modern convenience and the renter’s great modern
disaster, and the difference between the two is entirely in the preparation.


WHAT THEY WILL ACTUALLY CARRY

Read the packet, believe the packet, and then halve it. A set of the largest picture-hanging
strips is typically rated at around seven kilograms — but that rating assumes a smooth,
clean, sealed, fully cured painted surface at room temperature, which is a description of
very few rented walls. My own working rule after a number of expensive lessons:

    Adhesive strips, what I will trust them with ........................... half the rating
    Anything with glass, over a bed or a cot ........................... a screw, or nothing

WHERE THEY ALWAYS FAIL

    - Textured or embossed wallpaper. The adhesive touches perhaps a third of the surface.
    - Limewash, distemper and chalky old paint. The strip holds perfectly. The paint lets go
      of the wall, and takes a patch with it.
    - Fresh paint. Emulsion needs time to cure properly — a week is the usual instruction,
      and longer is better. A strip on week-old paint will pull it off.
    - Bathrooms and kitchens. Steam and heat defeat adhesive. Every time.


THE FIVE MINUTES THAT DECIDE IT

   1. Wipe the spot with rubbing alcohol, not a household spray — sprays leave a film that
      is precisely what the adhesive cannot grip.
   2. Press for thirty seconds, hard, with your thumb.
   3. Wait an hour before hanging anything on it.
   4. To remove: pull the tab straight down, slowly, flat against the wall, until it
      stretches free. Pulling outwards is what takes the plaster with it.

And the fixing nobody thinks of: if your building is older, look for a picture rail near the
ceiling. If there is one, you can hang almost anything at any height, on cord, for the price
of two hooks, and you will not make a single hole.


[ page 134 ]

The wall you may not paint
--------------------------

Landlord white is not a colour. It is the absence of a decision, chosen because it is cheap
by the drum and can be patched by anybody.

You cannot change it. You can stop it being the loudest thing in the room, and there are
exactly four ways, in order of how well they work.


ONE · CHANGE THE LIGHT ON IT

Page 130. A warm bulb at high rendering recolours every wall in the flat for the price of a
sandwich, and it is the only one of the four that is free and invisible. Do this before you
consider any of the others.


TWO · COVER THE RIGHT AMOUNT OF IT

Not a gallery of small frames — page 89 explains why a scatter of small things makes a wall
look busier and emptier at once. One large piece, hung at 145 cm to its centre, takes more
white off the wall than fifteen postcards and costs one fixing instead of fifteen.


THREE · PUT FABRIC ON IT

A quilt, a rug, a length of linen on a slim pole, a curtain along a whole wall rather than
just a window. This is the oldest wall treatment there is and it does three things at once:
it colours, it textures, and it quiets the room acoustically, which nobody expects and
everybody notices.


FOUR · LEAN THINGS AGAINST IT

A large mirror on the floor, a picture ledge, a leaning frame. No fixings at all, and the
diagonal of a leaning object is more interesting than the flat of a hung one.

  [ DO NOT SKIP THIS ]
Anything large and leaning must be secured to the wall or to the furniture behind it, with a
strap or a hook, even though it looks stable. Falling mirrors and toppling furniture injure
small children every year. This is the one place in the book where I will not offer you a
no-fixings option: one screw into a wall, or do not lean it.


[ page 135 ]

THE HONEST PAGE
Peel-and-stick, and what it does to a deposit
---------------------------------------------

Removable wallpaper is sold to renters as the answer to this entire part of the book. I have
used it, I have removed it, and I have twice watched it take a wall with it.


WHAT IT GENUINELY DOES WELL

On a smooth, sound, slightly sheened surface it is excellent, and there are three places I
recommend it without hesitation: the back of a bookcase, the inside of a cupboard door, and
a small panel behind a desk. In all three it is small, it is on a surface nobody inspects,
and if it fails you have lost an afternoon rather than a deposit.


WHERE IT GOES WRONG

    - Flat, matt, cheap emulsion — which is what a landlord’s wall almost always is. Matt
      paint is porous and mechanically weak at the surface. The adhesive often ends up
      stronger than the paint’s grip on the plaster, and the paper takes the paint off in
      sheets.
    - Textured walls or lining paper — it will not seat, and the edges lift within a month.
    - Anywhere warm or damp — above a radiator, in a kitchen, in a bathroom. It curls.
    - Left up for years — the longer it stays, the harder it comes off. The claim of
      removability quietly assumes you remove it reasonably soon.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE TEST PATCH, WHICH IS NOT OPTIONAL
  | Put one piece, the size of a postcard, somewhere hidden — behind a door, low in a
  | cupboard, behind the wardrobe. Leave it two weeks, in the season you are actually in.
  | Then take it off and look at the wall. That piece of paper is the cheapest insurance
  | policy in this book.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

If the test patch lifts even a fleck of paint, the answer for your flat is no, and you have
learned it for the price of one postcard instead of one room. Go back to page 134 and use
fabric instead. Fabric has never taken a wall off in the history of decorating.


[ page 136 ]

A floor you did not choose
--------------------------

The two rented floors are grey laminate and beige carpet, and they are the same problem in
two costumes: a large, flat, uniform surface with no texture and no interest, laid by
somebody buying by the square metre.

Page 70 said the floor has already voted. In a rented flat it voted without you, and the
correction is the same as it was there: do not fight it, cover the part of it that matters.


THE RUG IS THE FLOOR NOW

All of the sizing from page 98 applies unchanged, and the mistake is the same — too small.
In a rented sitting room, the rug should be large enough that the front legs of every seat
stand on it. That single rule turns a collection of furniture stranded on somebody else’s
laminate into a room.

    Sitting-room rug, the minimum that works ................................. front legs on
    Clear walking width left around it ............................................ 60–70 cm

A RUG ON CARPET, WHICH EVERYBODY ASKS ABOUT

Yes, and it is one of the most useful things a tenant can do — it is how you get texture
onto a floor that has none. Two conditions. Use a pad made for laying rug over carpet, not
the ordinary sort for hard floors, or it will creep and ruck within a week. And choose a rug
with some weight and a low, dense pile; a thin flat-weave over a soft carpet feels like
walking on a dropped tea towel.


ON STICK-DOWN VINYL PLANKS

I understand the temptation and I will still say no. On a rented floor they can bond far
harder than the packet suggests, the seams trap damp, and lifting them off a landlord’s
vinyl or a varnished board can take the surface with it. A rug is reversible. That is worth
more than a better-looking floor for the eighteen months you will live there.


WHAT TO LOOK FOR, SINCE THE RUG IS DOING SO MUCH

Wool if you can afford it, because it takes weight and recovers; a flat wool or a wool-and-
jute blend if you cannot. Avoid the very thin printed sort entirely — it reads as a picture
of a rug, and it will curl at every corner within a month. And buy the pad. A rug without a
pad wears out from underneath, slides, and feels cheap underfoot however much it cost.


[ page 137 ]

The kitchen you cannot change
-----------------------------

Nobody rents a kitchen they chose. The doors are laminate, the worktop is a pattern
pretending to be stone, and there is one fluorescent-looking fitting in the middle of the
ceiling.

Five corrections, none of them structural, in the order I would do them.

   1. Light the worktop, not the room. A plug-in strip or a pair of small lamps under the
      wall cupboards is the single biggest change available here. You currently work in your
      own shadow, because the only light is behind you. This is not decoration; it is the
      difference between a kitchen that is pleasant to cook in and one that is not.
   2. Change the handles, keep the originals. Most cupboard handles are two screws. A plain
      metal handle on a poor door reads as a plain door; a shiny moulded handle reads as a
      cheap one. Twenty minutes, entirely reversible, and the originals go in the box on
      page 142.
   3. Clear the worktop to two things. A rented kitchen is usually small and always looks
      worse cluttered. Everything that is not used daily goes into a cupboard. This costs
      nothing and it is the largest visual improvement on the list.
   4. Add the missing family. Page 111. A kitchen is laminate, steel and glass — hard,
      smooth, cold. A wooden board left standing, a ceramic jug, a stone mortar, a linen
      cloth on the rail: the four families arrive on one worktop for very little money, and
      the room stops reading as a fitted unit.
   5. Something growing. Herbs if the window faces anywhere but north, and see page 112 for
      what to do if it does.

  [ FROM THE NOTEBOOK ]
“A rented kitchen cannot be made beautiful. It can be made kind to cook in, and that is
nearly the same thing by seven in the evening.”


AND WHAT I WOULD NOT BOTHER WITH

Stick-on tile film over the splashback, adhesive worktop wrap, and anything that promises to
make laminate look like marble. All three fail in the same place — at the edges, near heat
and water — and all three can leave a residue that costs you far more at the end of the
tenancy than the cupboard doors were ever worth.


[ page 138 ]

The bathroom you cannot change
------------------------------

The smallest room, the worst light, and the one place where the corrections cost almost
nothing because there is so little surface to correct.


THE LIGHT, WHICH IS THE WHOLE PROBLEM

A bathroom is nearly always lit from directly overhead, which puts every face into the
shadow pattern that makes people believe they look tired. The professional answer is light
beside the mirror at roughly face height, not above it, so that both sides of the face are
lit. In a rented bathroom you often cannot add a fitting, so the two things you can do are:

    - Change the bulb to 2700–3000 K at CRI 90 or better. A bathroom under a cheap cool bulb
      is the origin of a great deal of unnecessary unhappiness at eight in the morning.
    - If there is a socket, a small lamp on the windowsill or a shelf, on in the evening. A
      bathroom lit low at night is a different room.

  [ THE SAFETY LINE ]
Electricity and water are governed by real rules, and they differ by country — in many
places sockets are not permitted in a bathroom at all, and fittings must carry a moisture
rating. Whatever you plug in, do not improvise around this. Nothing in this book is worth a
shock.


THE OTHER FOUR, IN ONE PARAGRAPH

A heavier shower curtain, hung high and long, is the largest piece of fabric in the room and
behaves exactly like a curtain elsewhere — page 116. Two good towels of the same colour
instead of six of different ones. A plant that likes low light and damp air, which is a
short and forgiving list. And nothing on any surface except what is used every day, because
in a room this small, clutter is the decoration.


THE THREE-MINUTE VERSION

Change the bulb. Take everything off the surfaces except what you use daily. Hang two towels
of the same colour. That is a bathroom corrected, and it costs the price of one bulb.


[ page 139 ]

Furniture that moves with you
-----------------------------

A tenant buys furniture twice: once for this flat, and once again when it does not fit the
next one. The second purchase is avoidable, and avoiding it is how a renter ends up with
good things.


THE THREE QUESTIONS BEFORE ANYTHING LARGE IS BOUGHT

   1. Will it go through a standard door and round a standard turn? Measure the narrowest
      point of the route, not the room — the front door, the stairwell, the turn at the top.
      A sofa that fits the room and not the stair is a sofa you will sell at a loss.
   2. Does it depend on this wall? Anything built to fit one alcove is bought for one flat.
      Freestanding beats fitted for anybody who moves.
   3. Can it be repaired, and would I still want it in ten years? Page 118 and page 123,
      unchanged. This is more important for a renter than for anybody, because your
      furniture is the only part of your home that is actually yours.


WHAT IS WORTH SPENDING ON, IN A RENTED FLAT

    Lamps — they go everywhere, forever .............................................. spend
    One good chair, one good table ................................................... spend
    Rug, linen, ceramics — portable texture .......................................... spend
    Anything fitted, cut down, or sized to this room ................................ do not

THE SIZE RULE FOR PEOPLE WHO MOVE

Buy slightly smaller than this flat allows. Rooms shrink more often than they grow, a two-
seat sofa fits nearly everywhere, and a three-seat sofa is the piece I saw abandoned on more
landings than any other object in Lyon.


WHERE TO BUY IT

Secondhand, for anything made of solid wood or with a frame worth having. A renter’s budget
goes furthest in the market for good old furniture, because the things that survived forty
years are by definition the things built to. Page 120 is about what that does to how a room
looks; this page is only about what it does to what you can afford.


[ page 140 ]

Storage, without building anything
----------------------------------

Rented flats are short of cupboards, and the shortage does more visible damage than any
colour on any wall, because a room with nowhere to put things is a room that is always
covered in things.

I put this in Part Six rather than in a chapter of its own because it is almost entirely a
renter’s problem. People who own their homes build a cupboard. You cannot, so you must be
cleverer.


THE FOUR PLACES A FLAT IS HIDING

    - The top thirty centimetres of every wall. Nothing is ever up there. A shelf at 200 cm,
      running the length of a wall, holds everything you own but rarely use, and reads as
      architecture rather than storage. Page 91.
    - Under the bed. Flat boxes, lidded, on the floor — and if the bed is too low, bed
      risers are cheap and invisible under a valance.
    - Behind a curtain. A rail across an alcove with a linen curtain in front of it is a
      wardrobe, costs almost nothing, comes down in ten minutes and is what half of Paris
      does.
    - The back of every door. Over-door hooks make no holes and take coats out of the room
      entirely.


THE RULE THAT KEEPS IT FROM LOOKING LIKE A STOREROOM

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | COLETTE’S RULE OF VISIBLE STORAGE
  | Visible storage must be one material and one colour, or it stops being storage and
  | becomes clutter with a lid on.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

Six identical boxes read as a system. Six different boxes read as a mess, even if they hold
exactly the same things. This is the cheapest tidying in the book: not buying more storage,
but making the storage you already own match.


TWO CAUTIONS

    - A high shelf must be fixed into something structural — that is a screw, not an
      adhesive strip, and it is one of the few places in this part where I will tell you to
      spend a permission on it. A shelf that comes down at 200 cm is not a decorating
      mistake.
    - Do not store anything in the room you actually live in if there is any alternative.
      Storage in a sitting room is the reason that sitting room never feels calm, whatever
      colour the walls are.


[ page 141 ]

THE QUIET PART
Who profits from a temporary home
---------------------------------

Ask why a renter is told to wait, and who is standing at the other end of the waiting.

There is an entire category of furniture that exists because people believe their home is
temporary. It is light, it is cheap, it is designed to be assembled once, and it is very
difficult to repair — the fixings are one-way, the material at the joint is often compressed
board that cannot take a screw twice, and the covers are not made to be replaced. That is
not a moral failing on anybody’s part. It is a product designed honestly for a customer who
has said, in effect, that they only need it for now.

But look at what happens over a decade of renting. You buy the temporary thing. It half
survives one move. You buy another. Over ten years and four flats, a person can quite easily
spend more on furniture that was never meant to last than they would have spent on a few
things that were — and end up at the end of it owning nothing.

Meanwhile the walls stay landlord white, because the tenant has been told that changing
anything is not their place. Magnolia and off-white are not chosen for beauty. They are
chosen because they can be patched by any decorator from any tin, which is a perfectly
rational decision for a person who owns twelve flats and a slightly sad one for the person
living in this one.

  [ SAY THIS PLAINLY ]
This is not a conspiracy. Nobody sat in a room and decided that renters should live badly.
It is what happens when one party buys for turnover and the other buys for eighteen months,
and nobody in the chain is buying for ten years. I am only pointing out that you can step
out of that pattern on your own, this week, without anybody’s permission.

Buy few things, buy them to last, and let them move house with you. The flat is temporary.
You are not.


THE FAIR OBJECTION, WHICH I WILL ANSWER

Somebody will say: cheap furniture is how a person on very little money furnishes a flat at
all, and a lecture about buying well is a lecture from somebody with savings. That is true,
and I mean it. So let me be precise about what I am actually asking: not that you spend more
in total, but that when you do buy, you buy fewer things and buy the ones that will move
house. Three lamps and one good chair, bought over two years, cost less than a room of new
furniture bought in one afternoon.


[ page 142 ]

Day one, and the last day
-------------------------

Everything in this part depends on being able to put the flat back exactly as you found it.
That is a practice, and it starts on the day you get the keys.


DAY ONE, BEFORE A SINGLE BOX IS UNPACKED

   1. Photograph every wall, every floor, and every existing mark, with the date on the
      file. Not a general view — go close on the chips, the scuffs, the stained corner of
      the carpet. This takes fifteen minutes and it is the only evidence that will exist.
   2. Photograph every light fitting before you touch it, so that reinstating them at the
      end is a matter of copying a picture.
   3. Find out what the walls are painted with. Ask, or look in the cupboard under the sink
      where half-used tins live. A tin of the landlord’s own paint is worth more to you at
      the end of the tenancy than anything you will buy.
   4. Start the box. Page 130. One box, labelled, at the back of a cupboard, for every
      original thing you remove: bulbs, shades, handles, the shower curtain, the plastic
      dish from the ceiling.


THE LAST DAY, WHICH IS NOW ONE AFTERNOON

Take down what you put up. Fill the screw holes with a fingertip of filler, let it dry, sand
it flat with a piece of fine paper, and touch in with the landlord’s paint using a small
brush and a dabbing motion rather than a stroke. Put the box back where it came from.
Photograph the walls again.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | COLETTE’S RULE OF THE FILLED HOLE
  | A hole you can fill is not damage. Take the photographs on day one, keep the originals
  | in a box, and every correction in this part costs you nothing when you leave.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

I have handed back a great many flats this way, and the honest truth is that the ones I had
corrected came back cleaner and better documented than the ones I had not.

    - Read the lease, then ask. Most refusals are refusals of risk. One thing at a time, at

CHAPTER SEVEN
The order, applied to any room
------------------------------

Every room in this part is corrected in the same order, and the order is the reason this
works. Doing the right things in the wrong sequence is how people spend a great deal of
money and change nothing.

   1. Empty what does not belong. Before anything is assessed, take out everything that is
      in the room by accident rather than by decision. Half of what is wrong with most rooms
      is not a fault of design; it is three objects that ended up there.
   2. Light. Three heights, warm, dimmable, big light off. Part Two. This costs least,
      changes most, and must come first because every other judgement you are about to make
      is a judgement made under this light.
   3. Height. Curtains up, pictures to 145 cm, furniture off the walls. Part Four. An
      afternoon, and usually free.
   4. Texture. Count the four families, add the missing one. Part Five.
   5. Colour. Last, always. Part Three. By the time you arrive here, half the rooms that
      thought they needed painting no longer do — which is the whole argument of this book
      and the reason I have made you wait four steps to hold a brush.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | COLETTE’S RULE OF THE ORDER
  | Light, height, texture, colour — in that order, in every room, every time. Anyone who
  | sells you the last one first is selling, not correcting.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


HOW TO READ THE PAGES THAT FOLLOW

Each room gets the same treatment: the fault I found most often, then the corrections in the
order above, then the numbers. Numbers given earlier are repeated here rather than cross-
referenced, and all of them are collected on page 165.

[ page 147 ]

The sitting room, and its four faults
-------------------------------------

This is the room I was called to most, and the complaint was always the same sentence: it
does not feel finished. It was almost never the room they thought it was.


FAULT ONE · EVERYTHING IS PUSHED TO THE WALLS

The commonest arrangement in the world, and it comes from a reasonable instinct — to keep
the middle clear. But a ring of furniture round an empty carpet is not a room; it is a
waiting area. Pull the seating in. Even five or ten centimetres off the wall changes how the
room photographs and how it feels, and this is page 100 in action.


FAULT TWO · THE SEATS ARE TOO FAR APART TO TALK

People buy the largest furniture the room will hold and then arrange it at the edges of the
space, and the result is a room in which two people must raise their voices slightly. Nobody
ever names this, and everybody feels it.

    Furthest comfortable distance between two seats facing each other ................ 2.4 m

FAULT THREE · ONE LIGHT, IN THE CEILING

Part Two, all of it. Three lamps, three heights, warm, and the overhead off. If you do only
this, most people will tell you the room has been painted.


FAULT FOUR · THE RUG IS TOO SMALL

The most expensive of the four to fix and the most common of all — page 97. A rug that
floats in the middle with every leg off it makes the furniture look temporary.

Not finished, in nine cases out of ten, means: lit from one place, arranged round the edges,
and standing on a rug that is one size too small.


THE ORDER TO FIX THEM IN

Light first, because it is free and it changes how you judge the other three. Then pull the
furniture in, which is also free. Then look again — genuinely look, in the evening, with the
lamps on and the overhead off. About half the sitting rooms I was called to needed nothing
further, and the rug was a decision for the following month rather than an emergency.


[ page 148 ]

The sitting room, in numbers
----------------------------

These are the figures I worked to. They are not laws of physics; they are the distances at
which a room stops being slightly uncomfortable in a way nobody can name.

    Sofa to coffee table .......................................................... 35–45 cm
    Coffee table height, against the seat cushion ...................... level or just below
    Walking route through the room, clear width ................................... 75–90 cm
    Between a seat and a side table, so a cup can be set down ........... within arm’s reach
    Rug — front legs of every seat standing on it .................................. minimum
    Television centre, seated eye height ...................................... about 100 cm

TWO THAT NOBODY GIVES YOU

    Side table top, against the arm of the chair beside it .................... level, ±5 cm
    Floor lamp, bottom of the shade, against seated eye level ................... 105–120 cm

THE TELEVISION, SINCE SOMEBODY HAS TO SAY IT

It is usually hung too high, because it was hung above a fireplace or a radiator rather than
at the height of the people watching it. A screen mounted above a mantelpiece asks everyone
in the room to sit with their chin up for two hours. If it cannot be lowered, at least stop
treating it as the thing the room faces: turn the seating to face the seating, and let the
screen be watched at an angle.

  [ FROM THE NOTEBOOK ]
“A sitting room is furniture arranged around a conversation. If it is arranged around an
appliance, that is what you will have.”

One more, which costs nothing: give the room somewhere to put a cup down within reach of
every seat. A guest with nowhere to set a glass never relaxes, and they will never tell you
why.


[ page 149 ]

The bedroom is lit for lying down
---------------------------------

Every other room is lit for standing or sitting. The bedroom is the only room in the house
you use with your head on a pillow, and almost nobody lights it that way.

Lie down on the bed tonight with the lights as they are and look straight up. If you are
looking directly into a bulb, you have found the fault, and it is the one that makes people
call their own bedroom restless without knowing why.


THE THREE SOURCES, BEDROOM VERSION

    - Beside the bed, one each side, with the bottom of the shade at about the height of
      your eyes when you are sitting up. Higher and it shines in your face; lower and you
      cannot read.
    - Low, across the room — a lamp on the floor or a low chest, which is the light you
      leave on while the room is being used for anything other than sleeping.
    - Overhead, off. Or fitted with the dimmest warm bulb in the flat, for finding things.

    Bedside table top, against the mattress top .............................. within 5–8 cm
    Bedside lamp, bottom of shade above mattress .................................. 50–60 cm
    Colour temperature, bedroom, everywhere ............................... 2700 K or warmer

THE SWITCH THAT MATTERS MORE THAN THE LAMP

You must be able to turn the light off without getting up, and on without finding it in the
dark. A bedside lamp with a switch on the flex halfway down the wall is a lamp that will be
left on or left off. This is a two-euro problem that ruins a fifty-euro lamp, and a plug-in
switch at the pillow solves it.

And curtains: this is the one room where the question is not only how they look. Lined or
interlined, hung wide enough to clear the glass entirely when open, and long enough that
light does not run out along the floor.


THE TWO LIGHTS NOBODY COUNTS

The standby lamp on a television or a charger, and the screen of a telephone at one in the
morning. Both are small, both are cold in colour, and both are in a room you have just
carefully warmed. Turn the first to the wall or cover it; charge the second in another room.
It is not decorating, but it is the last two per cent of a bedroom that is genuinely dark.


[ page 150 ]

The bed wall, and what goes on it
---------------------------------

The bed is the largest object you own and it sets the proportion of the room single-handed.
Nearly every bedroom fault is a fault of what is above it or beside it.


WHERE THE BED GOES

Against the longest uninterrupted wall, ideally with a view of the door and never with the
head under a window if it can be avoided — not for any mystical reason, but because a window
above a bed is cold, draughty, and impossible to curtain properly. Leave room to walk both
sides if the room allows it; if it does not, accept one side against a wall rather than
leaving a corridor of 30 cm that nobody can use.

    Clear space each side of a double bed, where possible ......................... 60–70 cm
    Space at the foot, to walk past .................................................. 75 cm
    Rug, extending beyond the sides of the bed .................................... 45–60 cm

ABOVE THE BED

The rule from page 87 changes here, because the bed governs. Anything hung above a bed is
centred on the bed, not on the wall, and its width should be somewhere between half and two
thirds of the bed’s width. Hang it so there is a hand’s span or a little more between the
bottom of the frame and the top of the headboard — close enough that the two read as one
composition.

  [ THE ONE GENUINE SAFETY RULE IN THIS BOOK ]
Whatever hangs above a bed should be light, and should be fixed properly — a screw into
something solid, not an adhesive strip. If the only fixing available is adhesive, hang
nothing there. A mirror above a sleeping person is not worth one night’s worry.

If the bed has no headboard, the wall behind it is doing that job, and it is the single best
place in a rented flat for a large piece of fabric — page 134.


[ page 151 ]

The dining table, and the light above it
----------------------------------------

There is exactly one light in a home that everybody agrees should hang from the ceiling, and
it is this one. It is also, in my experience, the most reliably hung wrong.

A pendant over a table is the only overhead light that works, and the reason is on page 29:
it is low enough to be below eye level, so it lights the table and the hands and the food
without lighting the tops of everybody’s heads. It only works at the right height, and the
right height is lower than people dare.

    Bottom of the shade above the table top ....................................... 75–90 cm
    Shade width, against the table width ............................. one third to one half
    Chair pulled out, clear space behind ............................................. 90 cm
    Table edge to wall, to walk past a seated person ................................ 100 cm

THE THREE MISTAKES

    - Too high. At 120 cm above the table it has become an ordinary ceiling light and lost
      every advantage it had.
    - Not centred on the table. It must be centred on the table, not on the room, and if the
      table cannot go under the existing fitting, the answer is a hook in the ceiling and a
      length of flex swagged across — an old, honest, entirely respectable solution.
    - Not dimmable. A table light at full strength is a kitchen. At a third it is a dinner.

If the shade is open at the bottom, the bulb will be visible to seated guests: use a frosted
or opal bulb, or one of the shades that carries a small diffuser. A bare filament at eye
level across a dinner table is the one fashionable lighting choice I have never been able to
forgive.


IF A PENDANT IS IMPOSSIBLE

Then light the table from the sides at about the same height instead: two lamps on a
sideboard, or wall lights, or — the oldest answer and still the best one — candles, which
sit exactly where a pendant would and put the light precisely where the food is. A table lit
low is the one piece of theatre in this book that I recommend without any reservation at
all.


[ page 152 ]

The kitchen, as a room
----------------------

A kitchen is designed by people who think about workflow and bought by people who think
about doors, and it is finished by nobody at all.


LIGHT, WHICH IS TWO JOBS AND NOT ONE

A kitchen needs task light on the surfaces — under the wall cupboards, over the hob, on the
sink — and it needs a soft light for the hours when nobody is cooking. Almost every kitchen
has the first badly and the second not at all. Under-cupboard light is the correction that
people thank me for most, because standing in your own shadow while chopping is a genuine
daily irritation that everyone has stopped noticing.

    Task light on worktops ................................................. 3000 K, CRI 90+
    The evening light — a lamp or a dimmed pendant .................................. 2700 K
    Worktop height, standard ...................................................... 90–92 cm
    Gap between facing runs, for two people ......................................... 120 cm

TEXTURE, WHICH IS THE WHOLE OF THE REST

A fitted kitchen is one material repeated forty times. That is why it reads as a showroom
rather than a room, and the correction is on page 111 — wood, ceramic, stone, linen,
something growing. A wooden board standing against the wall, a stone mortar, a ceramic jug
of utensils, a linen cloth over the rail. This is not styling. It is the same four families
you would count in a sitting room.


COLOUR, IF IT IS YOURS TO CHANGE

The inside of a kitchen window reveal, the wall above the tiles, and the ceiling. Those
three surfaces take colour without touching a single cupboard door, and they are usually the
only unfitted surfaces left.


THE ONE THING A KITCHEN NEEDS THAT IT NEVER HAS

Somewhere to sit. Even a single stool changes what a kitchen is for: somebody can keep you
company while you cook, which is the whole reason people gather there. If the kitchen is too
small for a chair, a stool that tucks fully under the worktop is worth more than any other
object you could put in that space.


[ page 153 ]

The first three metres
----------------------

The entrance is the room nobody decorates and everybody judges — including you, four times a
day, in the second before you take your coat off.

It is also, in most flats, the worst-lit space in the home: no window, one overhead fitting,
and a wall of coats. Three corrections, and it is usually the cheapest room in the book to
fix.


ONE · A LIGHT THAT IS NOT OVERHEAD

A small lamp on any surface, or a wall light if one exists. If there is genuinely nowhere, a
shelf at 110 cm with a lamp on it creates the surface and the light in one move. An entrance
lit at head height instead of from the ceiling is the difference between arriving somewhere
and arriving in a corridor.


TWO · A PLACE FOR THE FOUR THINGS

Keys, post, shoes, coat. If the hall does not hold those four, they will be held by the
kitchen table for the rest of your life. A bowl, a hook, a basket, a shelf.

    Coat hooks, adult height .................................................... 165–175 cm
    Second row, for children ........................................................ 110 cm
    Shelf for keys and post ..................................................... 100–110 cm

THREE · A MIRROR, AND WHERE

Not opposite the front door, where it will only ever reflect the door. On the wall you pass,
so that it reflects the room beyond and lengthens the hall — page 90. Hang it so that the
top is above the head of the tallest person who lives there, or it will cut them off at the
forehead every morning for years.

Three metres, one lamp, one hook, one mirror. No room in this book returns so much for so
little.


IF THERE IS NO HALL AT ALL

Many flats open straight into the sitting room, and the correction is to build the first
three metres anyway: a rug at the door, a hook on the wall beside it, a small table or shelf
with a lamp and a bowl. Those four objects, standing together, create an entrance where the
architecture did not provide one — and they stop the sitting room being the place where
coats live.


[ page 154 ]

The bathroom, quickly
---------------------

Small, hard, bright and reflective — every quality that makes a room unforgiving. It is also
the fastest room in the house to correct, because there is so little of it.


THE LIGHT, WHICH IS THE WHOLE COMPLAINT

Light from directly overhead falls into the eye sockets and under the chin, and produces the
face that makes people say they look terrible in this bathroom. They do not. They are lit
badly.

The correction is light beside the mirror at about face height rather than above it, so that
both sides of the face receive light. Where that is not possible, warm the bulb and add a
second small source anywhere else in the room — two poor lights are dramatically kinder than
one.

    Mirror lights, either side, centre at ..................................... about 160 cm
    Bathroom colour temperature ................................................ 2700–3000 K
    Colour rendering, where you will look at your face ............................. CRI 90+
  [ THE SAFETY LINE, AGAIN ]
Bathroom electrics are regulated, and the rules differ by country: what may be installed,
and how close to water, is not a matter of taste. Anything beyond changing a bulb belongs to
a qualified electrician.


AND THE REST, IN ONE PARAGRAPH

The shower curtain is the largest textile in the room and behaves like any other curtain —
weight first, length second, colour last. Towels: fewer, and matching, because six
mismatched towels in a small room are six decorative decisions you did not make on purpose.
A plant that tolerates low light and damp air. And a stool or a small chair if there is any
room at all, which turns a functional cubicle into somewhere you might sit and read.


THE MIRROR, WHILE WE ARE HERE

Bigger than you think, and as wide as the basin below it if the wall allows. A small mirror
centred over a wide basin is the single commonest proportion error in a bathroom, and it
makes an already small room read as a cupboard.


[ page 155 ]

The desk, and which way it faces
--------------------------------

More homes contain a desk now than at any point in my working life, and almost every one I
have seen was placed by where the socket was.


THE WINDOW GOES TO THE SIDE

There are three places a desk can face, and only one of them is right.

    - Facing the window — you look into bright sky all day, and anything on the desk sits
      against glare. Tiring within an hour.
    - Back to the window — the light falls over your shoulder onto the screen, which is now
      a mirror, and on a call your face is a silhouette.
    - Window to the side — daylight across the surface, nothing behind the screen, no
      reflection in it. This is the answer, and it has been the answer since people wrote
      with ink.

If you write or draw by hand, put the window on the side opposite your writing hand, so your
hand does not cast a shadow across its own work. That is the oldest rule in this book and it
comes from the workrooms of the Croix-Rousse.

    Desk height, standard ......................................................... 72–75 cm
    Top of the screen, against your eyes .................................. at or just below
    Screen distance ........................................................... about an arm
    Task lamp, from the side, never behind the screen ............................... 3000 K

AND ONE THING THAT IS NOT ERGONOMIC AT ALL

A desk in a room you live in must be able to stop being a desk at six o’clock. A lamp you
switch off, a lid you close, a basket the papers go into. Otherwise the work is the last
thing you see every evening, and the room never becomes a home again.


[ page 156 ]

One room that does four jobs
----------------------------

A studio is not a small flat. It is a sitting room, a bedroom, a kitchen and an office asked
to occupy the same floor at the same time, and the mistake everybody makes is trying to make
it look like one thing.


DO NOT BLUR IT. DIVIDE IT.

The instinct is to keep everything matching and low and pale so the room reads as one space.
It does — as one undifferentiated space, which is why studios feel like waiting rooms.
Divide it instead, with the four tools you already have:

   1. Light. The strongest divider there is, and the only one that takes no floor. Each zone
      gets its own light and its own switch. When the bed corner is lit and the desk is
      dark, the room is a bedroom; at nine in the morning it reverses. One room, four rooms,
      no walls moved.
   2. The floor. A rug under the sitting area and bare floor elsewhere draws the line more
      clearly than any screen.
   3. A back. The back of a sofa, a low bookcase, an open shelf unit — anything with a back
      to it makes a boundary at waist height without blocking the light.
   4. Fabric. A curtain on a ceiling track across the bed is the oldest studio solution in
      Europe, costs very little, and disappears in the day.


THE ONE RULE I WOULD NOT BREAK

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | FOR ONE-ROOM HOMES
  | The bed should not also be the sofa. If there is any way at all to have a real bed and a
  | separate seat, take it — even if both are small. A home where you must dismantle where
  | you sleep in order to sit down is a home that is never at rest.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

And storage, more than anywhere: page 140, and the rule that visible storage must be one
material and one colour. In a studio, everything is visible from everywhere.


[ page 157 ]

THE AWKWARD ROOMS
The long, narrow one
--------------------

The corridor room: twice as long as it is wide, one window at the far end, and furniture
down both long walls because there is nowhere else to put it.

That last decision is what makes it a corridor. A room lined on both long sides has
announced its own proportion, and everything that follows exaggerates it.


FOUR CORRECTIONS, IN ORDER

   1. Break the length into two rooms. Not physically — by use. Sitting at one end, table or
      desk at the other. Two centres are read by the eye as two spaces, and two short spaces
      are better proportioned than one long one.
   2. Turn something across the width. A sofa with its back to the middle of the room, a rug
      running across rather than along, a bench at right angles. Every horizontal line that
      crosses the room cuts the length.
   3. Light both ends. The tunnel effect is mostly a lighting fault: a bright window at the
      far end and darkness at the near end makes the room read as longer than it is. A lamp
      at the dark end collapses the distance immediately.
   4. Put something substantial at the far end. A large picture, a tall cupboard, a heavier
      colour on that one wall — the end wall is the one place where an accent wall is
      genuinely doing work rather than decorating, because it stops the eye.


WHAT NOT TO DO

Do not run a long thin rug down the middle. It is the commonest attempt and it is the one
thing guaranteed to make a long room longer, for the same reason a runner makes a hallway
read as a hallway.

A narrow room is cured across, never along.


IF THE WINDOW IS ON A LONG WALL INSTEAD

Then you are luckier than you think, and the room is easier: put the main seating facing
that wall rather than down the length, so that the room is used across its short dimension.
A room is as wide as the way you sit in it.


[ page 158 ]

THE AWKWARD ROOMS
The one with five doors
-----------------------

Old flats were divided and redivided, and the result is a room with a door to the hall, a
door to the kitchen, a pair of glazed doors, a cupboard and a radiator — and about two
metres of usable wall.


MEASURE THE SWINGS FIRST

Before deciding anything, open every door fully and mark on the floor where it lands. The
room you actually have is what is left. People try to furnish the plan and then spend two
years edging round a door.


THEN USE WHAT IS LEFT PROPERLY

    - The one good wall gets the one large thing. Sofa, bed, or cupboard — whichever is the
      largest object. Do not divide your only wall between two medium objects.
    - Float the rest. With no walls left, the furniture must stand in the room, and it needs
      a rug under it to explain why it is there.
    - Quiet the doors. Five doors in five different finishes are five loud objects. Painting
      doors and architraves the same colour as the walls — page 63 — makes them recede, and
      it is the single most effective thing available in a room like this.
    - Use the leaf. The back of a door is a wall you are not using: hooks, a narrow shelf, a
      mirror.


IF A DOOR IS NEVER USED

Say so out loud, and then commit. A door that is never opened can be curtained over or have
a piece of furniture in front of it, and you have bought yourself a wall — provided it is
not a fire escape route or the only way out of a room, which is a real consideration and not
a decorative one.

  [ FROM THE NOTEBOOK ]
“Count the doors before you fall in love with the sofa.”


[ page 159 ]

THE AWKWARD ROOMS
Open plan, without walls
------------------------

A wall was removed to make one big room, and what was gained in light was lost in
definition. The complaint is always the same: it is lovely, but it feels like a hall.

The correction is not to put the wall back. It is to give each part of the room the three
things a room has — its own floor, its own light, and a back.

    Its own floor .................................................... a rug, sized properly
    Its own light .................................................... own lamps, own switch
    A back ............................................................ sofa, shelf or bench

THE LIGHTING IS THE WHOLE THING

In an open-plan room the lighting must be able to do what a wall used to do: switch a part
of the room off. If the kitchen end and the sitting end share one circuit, you will always
be sitting in kitchen light, which is bright, high and working. Separate switching — even if
it is only lamps on plugs — is what lets the room become an evening room at seven.


THE SEAM BETWEEN COOKING AND SITTING

The hardest part of open plan is that the kitchen is always on show, including the washing
up. Two practical answers, both better than pretending: let the sitting area face away from
the kitchen, so that the back of the sofa is the boundary; and make the light in the kitchen
end switchable down to almost nothing, so that after dinner the whole zone simply goes dark
and stops existing.

Sound matters here more than in any other room, and it is the reason open-plan spaces feel
tiring. Hard floor, hard surfaces, high ceiling and no doors make an echo. Rug, curtains, a
full bookcase and upholstery are the cure, and they are the same four things Part Five asked
for anyway.


[ page 160 ]

THE AWKWARD ROOMS
The low one and the tall one
----------------------------

Two opposite faults with the same cure in reverse: decide where you want the eye to go, and
put a strong horizontal line there.


THE LOW CEILING

Anything under about 2.4 metres feels low, and the usual response — paint everything white —
does very little. What works:

    - Curtains from the ceiling, not from the frame. The largest single gain available, and
      page 92 explains it. A curtain that starts at the ceiling tells the eye the wall is as
      tall as the fabric.
    - Light upwards. An uplight or a shade open at the top puts light on the ceiling and
      dissolves it. A downlight does the exact opposite and presses the ceiling down.
    - Keep the furniture low, and the top edge of everything at a consistent height, so
      there is one horizontal line rather than five.
    - Do not use a deep colour on the ceiling here, whatever page 61 permits elsewhere —
      that is for rooms with height to give away.


THE VERY TALL ROOM

Less common, and almost nobody admits it is a problem, but a room of four metres with
everything at knee height feels like a cathedral with a sofa in it. I grew up in one.

    - Bring the light down. A pendant hung low over a table, lamps at head height. Light is
      what sets the height of a room at night: light only the lower three metres and the
      room is three metres tall after dark.
    - Draw a line at about 2.1 m. A picture rail, a shelf, the top of tall curtains, a
      change of colour. Above it becomes ceiling; below it becomes room.
    - Use the height for one thing only — a tall cupboard, a long curtain, one very large
      picture — rather than filling the whole wall.


[ page 161 ]

The child’s room, honestly
--------------------------

This is the room where the most money is spent on the things that matter least, and I say
that as somebody who was paid to supply them.


WHAT IS GENUINELY WORTH IT

   1. Darkness. Lined or blackout curtains, hung wide enough to cover the glass entirely.
      This is the one purchase in the room that changes a household’s life, and it is not
      decorative at all.
   2. A low, warm light for the night. Not the ceiling light. A small dim lamp at floor or
      bedside level, 2700 K or warmer, that can be left on or found in the dark.
   3. Storage a child can actually use. Low, open, and one material — page 140. Things get
      put away at the height the child can reach, and nowhere else.
   4. A surface to work at, lit from the side. Page 155, at a smaller height.

  [ THE SAFETY RULES, WHICH ARE NOT DECORATING ]
    - Every tall piece of furniture — chest of drawers, bookcase, wardrobe — must be
      anchored to the wall. Toppling furniture injures and kills small children. This is not
      optional and it is not a matter of taste.
    - Looped blind and curtain cords are a strangulation hazard. Use cordless blinds, or fit
      the tensioner and cleat that keep cords out of reach, and keep the cot away from the
      window.
    - Nothing heavy or glazed hangs over a bed.


WHAT IS NOT WORTH IT

The theme. A room decorated as a jungle, a rocket or a castle is a room that will be
outgrown in three years and repainted twice. Put the theme in the things that are easy to
change — the bedding, the posters, the toys, a cheap rug — and let the room itself be a
plain, warm, well-lit room that will still work when the child is fifteen.


[ page 162 ]

The room you do not use
-----------------------

Almost every home has one: the dining room nobody dines in, the spare room that is a
cupboard with a bed in it, the front room kept for visitors who come twice a year.

It is not a decorating problem. It is a problem of not having given the room a job, and no
amount of paint fixes a room that has not been told what it is for.


THE QUESTION, ASKED OUT LOUD

What happens in here every week? Not on special occasions — every week. If the honest answer
is nothing, the room is currently a storage unit that you are heating, and you have two
choices: give it a weekly job, or admit it is storage and make it good storage.


IF YOU GIVE IT A JOB

Pick one, and let the room be unapologetically that: the room where the ironing and the
sewing happen, the room with the piano, the room where somebody works, the room where the
children make a mess so the sitting room stays clean. Then apply the order of operations on
page 146 to that job. A room with a purpose gets used, and a room that gets used gets
corrected naturally over time.


IF IT IS A GUEST ROOM

A guest needs six things, and I have stayed in a great many houses that supplied none of
them:

    - A light beside the bed with a switch that can be found in the dark.
    - Somewhere to put a suitcase that is not the floor or the bed.
    - One empty drawer or one empty shelf, and four hangers.
    - A hook on the back of the door.
    - A mirror they can see themselves in, and somewhere to set a glass of water.
    - Curtains that actually make the room dark.

Six things, most of which cost nothing, and they are worth more than any amount of
decoration in a room somebody sleeps in twice a year.

How to use this list
--------------------

These are working figures, not laws. I give them because a number you can act on tonight is
worth more than a principle you have to interpret.


THREE THINGS TO KNOW BEFORE YOU USE ANY OF THEM

   1. A range means a range. Where I write 15–25 cm, anywhere in that band is right, and the
      choice between them is usually decided by what else is on the wall. Where I give a
      single number — 145 cm for the centre of a picture — it is single because the eye
      genuinely notices when it is wrong.
   2. The room wins the argument. If a figure here fights something obvious in front of you
      — a beam, a radiator, a window that starts at hip height — the room is right and the
      number is wrong. Page 88 is about exactly that conflict.
   3. Measure twice, in the room, at the time of day you use it. Almost every mistake I ever
      made in fourteen years was made by measuring once, in the morning, from the doorway.


METRIC AND IMPERIAL

Both are given throughout. Where the imperial figure is a round number and the metric is not
— 57 inches, 145 centimetres — that is because the convention came from a country that
measures in inches, and I have not tidied it into a rounder metric number that would be
slightly wrong.

  [ FROM THE NOTEBOOK ]
“My grandmother wrote the drop of every window she ever measured, and never once wrote the
reason. Forty-one years of numbers and no arguments. I have tried to give you both.”


TWO HABITS WORTH MORE THAN ANY FIGURE HERE

    - Write it down in the room. Not on your telephone as you walk away — on paper, standing

    - Mark it before you commit to it. Masking tape on the wall for the pole, newspaper on
      the floor for the rug, a pencil cross for the picture hook. Live with it for a day. It
      costs nothing and it has saved me more money than any other habit in this book.

TWO, CONTINUED
Measuring a window, in order
----------------------------

This is my grandmother’s sequence, unchanged. Write every figure down before you calculate
anything, because the arithmetic is where the mistakes live.

   1. Decide where the pole goes first, and mark it on the wall in pencil. Every other
      figure depends on it, and almost everybody measures the window instead.
   2. Measure the pole width, bracket to bracket, once the pole position is marked — not the
      window.
   3. Measure the drop from the ring to the floor, not from the pole. The curtain hangs from
      the bottom of the ring, and the difference is four or five centimetres, which is
      exactly the amount that looks wrong.
   4. Measure the drop in three places — left, middle, right. Floors are not level. Work to
      the shortest.
   5. Multiply the pole width by your fullness to get the fabric width you need, then divide
      by the number of curtain panels.
   6. Add the hem allowances the maker asks for, and add them again if you are unsure.
      Fabric can be shortened. It cannot be lengthened.

  [ THE ONE THAT CATCHES EVERYBODY ]
Ready-made curtains are sold by the width of the fabric, not the width of the window. A pair
labelled 168 cm wide means each panel is 168 cm of cloth — which on a 200 cm pole gives you
336 over 200, about 1.7 ×, and that is why so many bought curtains look thin. Check the
label, then check your arithmetic.

Full working, with a diagram, is on page 96.


THE ARITHMETIC, WORKED ONCE

A window 120 cm wide. The pole goes 20 cm beyond each side, so the pole is 160 cm. At 2.5 ×
fullness that is 400 cm of fabric, over two panels: 200 cm of cloth in each. The ring sits 7
cm below the pole and the floor is 242 cm down from it, so the drop is 241 cm to float one
centimetre clear. Write those four numbers — 160, 2.5, 200, 241 — and you can order curtains
from anybody in the world.

NINE
What to take to the shop
------------------------

Every disappointing purchase I have ever made was made without one of these six things in my
pocket. Photograph this page.

   1. The measurements of the space it is going into, written down — not the room, the
      space. Width, depth, height, and the distance to the nearest thing it must not touch.
   2. The narrowest point of the route home. Door, stair, turn, lift. A sofa that fits the
      room and not the stairwell is the most expensive mistake in this book.
   3. A photograph of the room, taken in the evening with the lamps on. Not in daylight. You
      will use the thing at night.
   4. A sample of whatever it has to live beside — a cushion cover, a paint card, a
      photograph of the floor. Colour is a relationship, not a property, and you cannot hold
      it in your memory for the length of a bus ride.
   5. A tape measure. Shop labels round generously.
   6. The question, written down: can this be repaired? Ask it out loud, of a person, in the
      shop. The answer, and the face they make while giving it, tells you most of what you
      need to know.

  | AND ONE RULE FOR THE SHOP ITSELF
  | Never judge a colour or a fabric under shop lighting. Take the sample outside, or to the
  | door, or home. Page 35 explains why every shop you buy from is lit to flatter what it
  | sells.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


AND TWO THINGS TO DO ON THE DAY IT ARRIVES

   7. Open it and look at it before the delivery leaves. Damage found in the first five
      minutes is the shop’s problem. Damage found next week is an argument.
   8. Put it where it is going before you fill it. A bookcase is very heavy once it holds
      books, and a sofa cannot be turned round in a doorway once the room is rearranged
      round it.

How the audit works
-------------------

This is the thing I actually did, in a client’s home, in the first ten minutes, before I
said a word about what anything cost.


THE RULES

   1. Do it in the evening, with the lights you normally use. Not in daylight, not with
      everything switched on for the occasion. The room as you actually live in it.
   2. Sit down where you normally sit. Most faults in a room are only visible from the
      chair, and every photograph of a room is taken standing up, which is why rooms
      photograph better than they feel.
   3. Answer yes or no. Do not answer “sort of.” If you are hesitating, the answer is no.
   4. Do not fix anything while you audit. Write it down and move on. The order matters more
      than the speed, and the order comes at the end.


WHAT YOU WILL HAVE AT THE END

Four scores out of six — light, height, texture, colour — and a page number. The lowest
score is where you start, and it is nearly always light, which is fortunate, because light
is the cheapest of the four to correct.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | BEFORE YOU BEGIN
  | Nothing on these pages asks what you own, what you spent, or what your taste is. Every
  | question is about what is physically true of the room. That is deliberate. Your taste
  | was never the problem.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

The three audit pages that follow are meant to be written on. If this is a file rather than
a printed book, print these four pages on their own — they are the ones worth having on
paper.


IF YOU SHARE THE HOME

Do the audit separately and compare the sheets. It is the only way I know to have this
conversation without it becoming a conversation about taste — two people who disagree about
a colour will nearly always agree that a room has one light source and a rug that is too
small. Fix what you agree on first. It is usually most of it.

The first evening
-----------------

You have the scores. Here is what to do tonight, in the two hours after dinner, before you

Why this list exists
--------------------

I was, for fourteen years, a person who sold things to people for their homes. I want to be
honest about what that does to advice.

A decorator is paid either by the hour or by the thing. If by the thing, then every problem
in your home has a solution that can be delivered in a van, and that is not corruption — it
is just what the work rewards. I noticed it in myself. A client would describe a room that
felt wrong and I would find myself reaching for a catalogue before I had sat down in it.

So this list is my correction to my own trade. It is every category of purchase where I saw
money spent that did not improve a room, or that improved it for six months and then became
a thing to get rid of.


THE FOUR TESTS BEHIND EVERY ENTRY

   1. Does it solve the fault, or does it decorate over it? A darker cushion does not fix a
      room lit from one place.
   2. Will it still be here in ten years, and will I still want it?
   3. Can it be repaired, recovered, or refinished by an ordinary person?
   4. Does it move house? If you rent, this is the first test, not the last.

  [ A FAIR WARNING ABOUT THIS SECTION ]
These are my opinions, formed by what I saw in one city over fourteen years, and a person
with a different climate, a different housing stock or a different budget may reasonably
disagree with half of it. Where something is a matter of fact I say so; where it is a matter
of taste I have tried to say that too. Page 201 is a whole page of things I might be wrong
about.


HOW TO READ THE ENTRIES

Where an entry ends with a page number, the argument for it is made properly somewhere
earlier in the book and you should go and read it before you accept my verdict. Where it
does not, it is a judgement I formed on the job and have not made a case for at length —
treat those with more suspicion, not less.


[ page 191 ]

ONE
Lighting
--------

    - Cool white bulbs, anywhere in a home. Above 3000 K a room turns grey-blue and every
      warm colour in it dies. This is the single most damaging cheap purchase in decorating.
    - Any bulb that does not print its colour rendering. If the box will not tell you the
      CRI, it is because the number is not good. Page 34.
    - A brighter ceiling light, bought to fix a dark room. The room is not short of light.
      It is short of light in the right places. Page 32.
    - Downlights, added to a room you live in. They light floors. You do not live on the
      floor, and they will put every face in the room into shadow.
    - Bare-filament bulbs at eye level over a table. Fashionable, and genuinely unpleasant
      to sit under for two hours.
    - A lamp chosen for the base alone. The shade does the work — it is what decides where
      the light goes and whether you can see the bulb. Page 38.
    - Colour-changing smart bulbs, for the colour. The dimming is worth having. The sixteen
      million colours will be used twice, and the ones you will actually leave on are the
      two that a plain warm bulb already gives you.
    - Battery candles with a flicker. I am aware this is a matter of taste. I have never
      once seen them improve a room.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | SPEND THE MONEY HERE INSTEAD
  | One more lamp, at a lower height, with a proper shade and a warm, high-rendering bulb in
  | it. That purchase has never disappointed anybody I advised.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


THE TWO EXCEPTIONS I WOULD MAKE

Cooler light is right in exactly two places in a home: a task light where fine work happens,
and a mirror where somebody applies make-up they will wear in daylight. Both are task
lighting, both are switched on for minutes rather than hours, and neither is the light the
room sits in.


[ page 192 ]

TWO
Paint and colour
----------------

    - Paint chosen from a chip, a screen, or a photograph. A colour is a relationship with
      the light in your room, and the chip has none of that information. Page 48.
    - The colour of the year. It is a marketing calendar, it is announced by people who sell
      paint, and by the time a room is finished in it the following one has been announced.
      Page 79.
    - Five tester pots of nearly the same white. Two sheets of lining paper with two
      candidates on them will tell you more than five pots dabbed straight onto the wall.
    - Cheap white emulsion, bought by the drum, for a room you will live in. It is porous,
      it marks, it cannot be cleaned, and it is the exact surface that makes adhesive
      fixings take the wall off. Page 135.
    - A feature wall, chosen because the room needs something. A wall that is different for
      no structural reason reads as an unfinished decision. Page 64.
    - Chalk paint for furniture that was already a good finish. It is excellent on the right
      piece and it is a one-way journey on the wrong one.
    - Any colour tested only in daylight, for a room you use in the evening.
    - Enough paint for two coats. Buy enough for three. The third coat is the one nobody
      plans for and everybody needs, and a second tin mixed later may not match.

And the one thing in this category worth more than it costs: a small pot of the wall colour,
kept, labelled with the date and the room. Every mark on that wall for the next ten years is
then a two-minute job instead of a repaint.


THREE THINGS WORTH PAYING MORE FOR

    - Coverage. A good paint covers in two coats what a cheap one does not cover in four,
      and the labour is the expensive part.
    - A washable finish in a hall or a kitchen. Matt in a corridor is a decision you will
      regret with a cloth in your hand.
    - Brushes. My father owned four and cleaned them for thirty years. A cheap brush sheds
      into the wall and no paint recovers from that.


[ page 193 ]

THREE
Textiles and soft furnishing
----------------------------

    - Curtains that stop at the sill when the floor was available. The single most common
      curtain error, and it costs nothing to avoid at the point of buying. Page 93.
    - Curtains at 1.5 × fullness. They will look thin for as long as you own them, and no
      amount of steaming fixes it. Page 94.
    - A cushion set. Four cushions in one pattern, sold together, is the matched-set problem
      bought deliberately. Page 117.
    - Polyester cushion inserts. They flatten in a season and never recover, and they read
      as flat because they are uniform. Feather, every time, two to five centimetres larger
      than the cover.
    - A rug bought to the size of the gap between the furniture. Measure the size that puts
      the front legs on it, then buy that. Page 97.
    - A very thin printed rug. It reads as a picture of a rug, and it curls at every corner
      within a month.
    - A sofa whose covers cannot be removed or replaced. You are renting it slowly. Page
      118.
    - Blackout blinds for a bedroom, instead of lined curtains. The blind handles the light
      and nothing else; the curtain handles light, cold, sound and the proportion of the
      whole wall.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | SPEND THE MONEY HERE INSTEAD
  | One properly full, properly long, lined curtain on the room’s best window. It is the
  | largest and best-lit texture in the room and it changes the proportion of the wall at
  | the same time.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


ON BUYING TEXTILES SECONDHAND

Linen and wool are worth buying used and improve with age; foam and polyester are not and do
not. Check a secondhand curtain for sun-rot by holding it to the light and pulling gently at
the leading edge — the side that faced the window will go first, and if it parts under your
thumb the whole pair is finished whatever the price.


[ page 194 ]

FOUR
Furniture
---------

    - Anything whose joints are one-way. If the fixings cannot be undone and done up again,
      the piece is finished the first time it is moved badly. Ask the question in the shop
      and watch the face. Page 123.
    - A matched suite — sofa, chair, footstool, all one fabric. It will never look chosen.
      Page 114.
    - A three-seat sofa for a flat you might leave. It is the piece I saw abandoned on more
      landings than any other object. Page 139.
    - Anything built to fit one alcove, if you rent.
    - A coffee table taller than the sofa seat. Level or just below, always — a tall table
      in front of a low sofa makes the whole arrangement look like a waiting room.
    - Storage bought in mixed materials and colours. Six identical boxes read as a system;
      six different ones read as clutter with lids on. Page 140.
    - A bed frame with no under-bed clearance, in a small home. That is the cheapest storage
      you will ever own and you are giving it away.
    - Furniture bought on the same afternoon as the rest of the room. A room furnished in
      one transaction looks furnished in one transaction, whatever it cost.

The honest counter-argument, which I owe you: cheap furniture is how a person on very little
money furnishes a home at all, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. The request is not
that you spend more in total. It is that when you do buy, you buy fewer things and buy the
ones that will still be here in ten years. Page 141.


THE SIX SECONDS THAT TELL YOU MOST, IN THE SHOP

   1. Lift one corner. Weight is not everything, but a chair that is suspiciously light is
      usually stapled softwood and compressed board.
   2. Push the top corner sideways. Any sway in a cabinet or a bookcase now is a wobble
      forever.
   3. Look underneath. This is where the truth is kept: the frame, the joints, the staples,
      and whether anybody expected you to look.


[ page 195 ]

FIVE
The things sold to renters
--------------------------

A whole category of product exists because tenants have been told they may not change
anything. Some of it is excellent. Much of it is a deposit waiting to be lost.

    - Removable wallpaper, on a whole wall, untested. On flat landlord emulsion it can take
      the paint off in sheets. One postcard-sized test patch, two weeks, in a hidden place,
      before you commit a room. Page 135.
    - Stick-down vinyl floor planks. They bond harder than the packet suggests, the seams
      trap damp, and lifting them can take the landlord’s surface with them. A rug is
      reversible. Page 136.
    - Adhesive tile film and worktop wrap. They fail at the edges, near heat and water,
      which in a kitchen is everywhere.
    - Adhesive hooks trusted to their printed rating. Halve it, and never hang anything
      glazed above a bed on one. Page 133.
    - A tension rod carrying lined curtains. It is held up by friction. It will come down,
      and usually at night. Page 132.
    - Peel-and-stick anything in a bathroom. Steam defeats adhesive. Every time.
    - “Temporary” furniture, bought four flats in a row. The arithmetic of that decade is on
      page 141 and it is not kind.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | THE RENTER’S ONE-LINE RULE
  | Buy the thing that moves house with you, not the thing that sticks to somebody else’s
  | wall.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+


AND THE THREE THAT GENUINELY WORK

Adhesive strips, used honestly and well under their rating, on sound modern paint. Removable
wallpaper on the back of a bookcase or inside a cupboard door, where failure costs an
afternoon. And a clamp lamp — it needs no surface, no socket in the right place and no
permission, and it is the single most useful object a tenant can own.


[ page 196 ]

The ten things to buy instead
-----------------------------

Every entry in the last five pages was a refusal. This page is the whole of what I would
actually spend money on, in the order I would spend it.

   1. Bulbs for the whole house. 2700 K, CRI 90 or better. Costs less than a dinner and
      recolours every wall you own.
   2. A third lamp, low. Floor level, in a corner, behind something. The light almost nobody
      has.
   3. A dimmer, or bulbs that dim. Brightness you can take away is worth more than
      brightness you can add.
   4. A curtain pole, long enough. Two brackets and four screws, and it changes the
      proportion of the whole wall.
   5. One properly full, properly long curtain on the best window in the house.
   6. The right-sized rug, and a pad under it. The most expensive item here and the one most
      worth waiting and saving for.
   7. One large piece of art, hung at 145 cm, instead of fifteen small ones.
   8. One unglazed ceramic or stone object. Often the missing fourth texture, and often the
      price of a coffee.
   9. One good chair that can be reupholstered. Secondhand, if that is what the money allows
      — it usually buys better.
   10. A tin of your own wall colour, labelled and kept. The cheapest insurance in the
      house.

Nine of those ten will move house with you. That is not a coincidence either.


WHAT THE WHOLE LIST COSTS

Items one to three — bulbs, a lamp, a dimmer — come to less than a single cushion from a
good shop, and between them they do more than anything else on the page. Items four to ten
can be bought over a year, in that order, and every one of them moves house with you. There

WHERE TO START
Thirty days
-----------

One home, one month, and almost no money. This is the order I would work in if I walked into
your flat tomorrow.


WEEK ONE — LIGHT, AND NOTHING ELSE

    - Tonight. The audit on page 183, for the room you use most.
    - Day two. Read every bulb in the house and write a list.
    - Day three. Buy them. 2700 K, CRI 90+. Change them all in one evening.
    - Day four to seven. Move lamps you already own until the main room has three sources at
      three heights, and leave the ceiling light off all week.


WEEK TWO — HEIGHT, AND AN AFTERNOON

    - Every picture and mirror re-hung to 145 cm at its centre.
    - Every piece of furniture pulled off its wall by a hand’s width.
    - One curtain pole raised and widened, if you are allowed to. If not, page 132.


WEEK THREE — TEXTURE, ONE OBJECT AT A TIME

    - Count the four families in each room and name what is missing.
    - Buy the one cheapest thing that supplies it — usually ceramic or stone, usually small.
    - Break one matched set.


WEEK FOUR — AND ONLY NOW, COLOUR

    - Re-do the audit. Compare the two scores.
    - If colour is still the lowest, test two candidates properly, on lining paper, over a
      weekend — page 47.
    - If it is not, spend the paint money on the rug instead.

Three of those four weeks cost almost nothing. That is the book.

THE HONEST PART
Where the numbers come from
---------------------------

You have been given about a hundred figures. You are owed an account of where each kind of
them came from, because they are not all the same kind of thing.


FOUR SORTS OF NUMBER, AND HOW MUCH TO TRUST THEM

   1. Measured standards. Kelvin, lumens, CRI and Martindale are defined by international
      test standards and printed on the packaging by law or convention. These are facts. If
      a box gives you one, believe it.
   2. Trade conventions. The centre of a picture at 145 cm — 57 inches — is the hanging
      height used by galleries and museums, and it is a convention rather than a law of
      nature. It is a convention that has been tested on millions of visitors, which is why
      I give it as a single number.
   3. Ergonomic ranges. Worktop heights, desk heights, seat-to-table distances, walking
      clearances. These come from furniture and building practice, they vary a little
      between countries, and a person much taller or shorter than average should adjust them
      without guilt.
   4. My own working figures. Fullness at 2.5 ×. Three sources at three heights. Halving an
      adhesive rating. Pulling a sofa 5–15 cm off the wall. These are fourteen years of one
      person’s practice in one city, and they are the ones a reasonable professional might
      argue with.


TWO CLAIMS I HAVE DELIBERATELY SOFTENED

    - Houseplants and air quality. The famous experiments were done in sealed chambers. At
      the densities of an ordinary room the effect is negligible, and I say so on page 112
      rather than repeat the claim.
    - Colour and mood. There is real research, and there is a great deal of confident
      decorating folklore built on top of it. Where I say a dark room feels enclosing I am
      describing what people reported to me, not a measured psychological effect.


WHAT I DID NOT VERIFY

Prices, because they differ everywhere and date within a year — where I mention cost it is
an order of magnitude, never a quotation. Product names, because I would rather teach you to
read a label than send you to a shelf that has changed. And local building and electrical
regulations, which differ by country and are the one place in this book where you must ask
somebody qualified where you live.

THE HONEST PART
What I might be wrong about
---------------------------

A book that never says this is a book that is selling something. Here are the six positions
in these two hundred pages that I hold least firmly.

   1. That a small room should not be painted pale. It is my most unpopular position — page
      57 — and it is drawn from small rooms in old European buildings with poor light. In a
      small, bright, modern room, pale may well be right and I may be arguing from my own
      climate.
   2. That the overhead light should be off. True of every home I worked in. Not necessarily
      true of a room with a very high ceiling and a beautiful fitting, or of anyone whose
      eyes need more light than mine do. Light is also an accessibility question, and
      atmosphere must lose that argument every time.
   3. That matched sets are always wrong. Held firmly, but it is a position about how rooms
      read, not a fact about furniture, and there are households where the calm of a matched
      set is worth more than the interest of a broken one.
   4. That you should buy fewer, better things. Good advice with savings behind it and a
      lecture without them. Page 141 is my attempt to be fair about this and I am not sure I
      succeeded.
   5. That every room needs something living. Not every household can keep a plant alive,
      and a dead plant is worse than no plant. Take stone or dried branches instead and lose
      nothing.
   6. The rug sizes. They come from rooms of a certain size. In a very small flat, a rug
      that puts every leg on it may not exist, and front legs on is not a compromise there —
      it is the answer.

Everything else in this book I would defend at a table with anybody in the trade.


HOW TO DISAGREE WITH THIS BOOK USEFULLY

Do the thing first, live with it for a fortnight, and then disagree. Almost every correction
here looks wrong on the first evening — a picture at 145 cm looks too high, a sofa off the
wall looks like it has been pushed, a dark room looks alarming at lunchtime. Two weeks is
how long it takes an eye to stop arguing. If you still dislike it then, you are right and I
am wrong, and it is your home.

LAST WORDS
The cahier
----------

I have the notebook in front of me as I write this. It is cloth-bound, the corners are gone,
and it is full of numbers in a hand I would know anywhere.

There is nothing beautiful in it. Page after page of windows: the drop, the width, the
fullness, the colour mixed to go with it, and the name of the family. Madame Rivière, salon,
deux fenêtres, 2m40. No opinions. No photographs. Forty-one years of one woman measuring
other people’s rooms and writing down what was true of them.

When I was young I thought it was a record of work. I understand now that it is a record of
attention. She could not afford any of those rooms. She was never going to live in one of
them. And she looked at each one properly anyway, and wrote down what it needed, and went
home.

That is the whole of what I have tried to give you. Not a style — styles belong to other
people and go out of fashion while you are still paying for them. Attention. The ability to
walk into a room, including your own, and see what is actually happening in it instead of
what you feel about it.

You will not need this book for very long. That is the point of it. In a year you will hang
a picture without measuring, and know from the doorway that a room is lit from one place,
and stop yourself in a shop because you have remembered to ask whether the thing can be
repaired. Then the book goes on a shelf and you get on with your life, which is what a home
is actually for.

She made me promise to write it down before it was lost. It is written down. Thank you for
reading it.

  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | AND THE LAST LAW, WHICH IS THE FIRST ONE
  | We do not decorate a room, ma chère. We correct it.
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

The notebook is going to my daughter, who does not want it yet and will. There is no rule in
it that has not been in this book somewhere, and there is nothing in it about style at all.
Only windows, and the light that came through them, and who lived there.

THE FILMS

Everything in this book began as something I said out loud on Chez Colette. If you have
arrived here from there, you already know the voice. If you have not, the channel is the
same method in twenty-minute pieces — one room, one fault, one correction at a time — and it
costs nothing.


TELL ME WHAT HAPPENED

I would genuinely rather have one photograph of a corrected room than a hundred compliments.
If you do the audit on page 183 and fix the thing it points at, tell me. The corrections
readers send me are where the next edition of this book will come from — particularly from
homes unlike mine: different climates, different light, different housing.


TWO REQUESTS

   1. Print pages 183 to 187, even if you read the rest of this on a screen. The audit only
      works with a pencil.
   2. Do one thing tonight. Not the whole plan. One thing. Turn the ceiling light off and

Your taste was never the problem. Nobody ever showed you what to look at. Now somebody has.

This is a file, and files get lost. Save a copy somewhere you will still have it in five
