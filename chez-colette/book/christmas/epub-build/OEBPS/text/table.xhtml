The twenty-fourth

The réveillon table

The French have a phrase for everything that goes on a laid table, les arts de la table, and the whole of it comes down to one question: can every guest see every other guest, and their plate, by candlelight?

The numbers

Width of table per guest 60 cm · 24 in, at least
Tablecloth drop, for dinner 20–30 cm · 8–12 in
Centrepiece, low under 30 cm · 12 in
Or tall and thin, with nothing between above seated eye level
Candles from the nearest greenery 30 cm · 12 in, or more

The table, in order

  1. The cloth first, white or cream, or linen in its own colour, for the reason on page 109 of the book. A cloth with a printed Christmas pattern fights the food.
  2. Light, before anything else is laid. The ceiling light off, the pendant over the table, if you have one, at its proper low height and dimmed (page 151 of the book), and the candles placed and lit, so that you set everything else by the light it will be seen in.
  3. Greenery along the middle, low, as a runner of cut branches straight on the cloth. It costs almost nothing, and it carries the green of the tree to the table.
  4. Plates and glasses last, and fewer of them than the occasion seems to demand. A table so full that the candles have to be moved to serve is a table that has stopped working.

Mémé’s rehearsal

My grandmother laid the réveillon table on the evening of the twenty-third, completely, with the candles lit, and sat in every chair in turn. She moved whatever hid a face. Then she cleared it all away, and laid it again the next evening in a quarter of the time.

The same table, with the same greenery moved to the side, serves the New Year’s Eve dinner a week later. Hang the mistletoe in the doorway, not over the table.

The window from the street

In December your window is the one part of your home that strangers see, and it is seen almost only at night. Decorate it for the pavement, not for the room.

The eighth of December in Lyon is the proof that the simplest window is the most beautiful one. A row of small flames along a sill, seen from below, does more than any arrangement of lit reindeer, because it is light doing what light does best: marking the shape of the window and nothing else.

Lighting a window, simply

  • Along the sill, where Lyon puts its candles: a row of small lights, or battery candles in glasses, at even spacing. A hand’s width apart is enough.
  • Across the top, a single strand along the head of the window, held by a tension rod inside the recess. Two lines of light, top and bottom, frame the glass.
  • In the middle, one thing, or paper stars on thread at different heights. One star in a window is a sign. Twelve plastic figures is a shop.
  • The same warm light as the tree and the lamps, for the reason on page 6. A cool-white window in a street of warm ones is the one everybody notices, for the wrong reason.

The curtains

Open, in the evening, if the window is the decoration. A lit window with the curtains drawn is a strip of light between two dark panels; with them pulled back to the edges of the frame, it is a picture. Close them at bedtime, with the timer, and the street goes dark with you.

Flames behind glass

A real flame on a sill sits between the glass and a curtain. If you light real candles in a window, as Lyon does, take the curtains right back, keep the flames in glasses, and never leave the room while they burn. Otherwise, battery candles. From the street, nobody can tell the difference.

Outside the window — on a balcony, a porch, a front door — use only lights and leads made for outdoors, plugged into a socket made for outdoors. The words “indoor use only” on a box mean exactly that, and rain finds out.
