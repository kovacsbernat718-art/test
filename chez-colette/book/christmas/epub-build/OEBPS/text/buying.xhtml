The shortest list in this book, and the hardest to keep

What not to buy for Christmas

The four tests on page 190 of the book apply in December like any other month. They are simply harder to hear over the music in the shop.

  • Cool-white or “bright white” lights. They turn a tree blue-grey and a sitting room into a shop window. Page 6.
  • Strands that flash, chase or change colour, unless they also have a steady setting you will use.
  • A pre-lit artificial tree. When one strand dies, the tree is a dead strand for ever. Buy the tree and the lights separately. Page 11.
  • The box of fifty matching baubles, bought new to replace last year’s box of fifty. Page 15.
  • Tinsel. Plastic film that sheds into the carpet for the rest of the winter and is rarely recyclable. Light does its job better.
  • Spray snow on the windows. It looks like spray snow from the street, and on a bad day it looks like it until March.
  • “This year’s colours”. A palette designed to be replaced next December. Page 14 has one that lasts.
  • Several scented candles for one room. One scent per room, none on the table.
  • Shiny polyester ribbon on the roll. Velvet, linen or cotton by the metre costs a little more, lasts for years and looks like cloth.
  • Large inflatables and plastic light-up figures for a small flat or a balcony. They are built to be seen from a road, and a room is not a road.
  • Christmas cushion covers, new every year. A plain cover in your one Christmas colour does the same job in December and stays out in January.
  • Anything that needs its own extension lead across a walkway.

A fair warning, again

As with the main list, these are my opinions, formed on the job, and a family with a particular tradition — the flashing lights a grandmother loved, the box of tinsel a child insists on — should keep it. Tradition beats taste every time. These are the things to stop buying, not the things to throw away.

The ten Christmas things worth buying

Like page 196 of the book, in the order I would spend the money, and nine of the ten will still be useful in March.

  1. A timer plug, or two. Lights on at dusk and off at bedtime, every night, without a crawl behind the tree. The cheapest thing on this page and the most used.
  2. Warm-white strands, one batch, enough for the tree by the count on page 7. Unlit tree, separate lights, steady setting.
  3. A stand that holds water, plenty of it, for a real tree. A tree dies in a stand that holds too little.
  4. Unscented candles, a box of tall ones for the table and some short ones in glasses. Battery candles with a timer wherever a flame would be unsafe.
  5. Ribbon by the metre, velvet or linen, in your one Christmas colour. It ties the wreath, the garland, the stockings and the presents, and it lasts.
  6. A garland long enough, by the measure on page 12, or two thin ones to twist together.
  7. Cut greenery, from a florist, a market or a garden: branches for a jug, a runner for the table, filling for the garland. It costs very little and it is the only decoration that smells right.
  8. A wreath of the right size, and the ribbon or the over-door hook to hang it on. Page 13.
  9. One ornament, chosen slowly, with the year written on its tag. Page 15.
  10. Boxes for putting it away, with dividers, so that next year begins untangled. Page 23.

Most of the money goes on light and on greenery. That is not a coincidence: they are the two things a December room is actually made of.

What the whole list costs

Items one to four — timers, lights, a stand, candles — are the ones that change the room, and together they cost less than a box of designer baubles. Items five to nine are the ones that make it look generous, and the greenery is the only one bought again every year. Item ten is the one that saves next December an evening.

The quiet part

Who profits from a new Christmas every year

Nobody plotted it. The trade simply arranged itself around the one season in which almost everybody buys decorations, and the arrangement has a shape worth seeing.

Christmas decorations are sold in a few weeks, mostly to people in a hurry, and much of what is sold is stored in a box for eleven months and brought out again. A decoration that lasts is good for the household and bad for next year’s sales. That is not anybody’s fault. It is just what the season rewards, and it explains three things you will see in every shop this December.

  1. The palette changes every year. A colour story for the season, shown on every display, so that last year’s perfectly good decorations look like last year. Your tree does not know what year it is.
  2. The set is cheaper than the piece. Fifty baubles cost little more than five good ones, so the set always looks like the sensible choice, and a tree dressed from it always looks like the shop.
  3. Everything lights up, and nothing can be repaired. Moulded figures with the lights sealed inside, trees with the strands built in. When one part fails, the whole object is finished, and there is another on the shelf.

None of this needs a villain to work, and none of it needs you to do anything except notice. The household that buys well at Christmas is not the one that buys least. It is the one that buys things which come out of the box next year still wanted: lights that can be replaced on their own, glass that does not date, greenery that is meant to be thrown away.

The box in the cellar

Our decorations live in two wine crates in the cellar, and the oldest thing in them is a glass bird with a tail of real feathers, older than my mother. It has never matched anything on any tree. It goes on first, every year, and it is the one my daughter looks for.

Colette’s rule of the box

Buy for the box in the cellar, not for the photograph. A decoration is good if you are glad to unwrap it in ten years.

Safe, without spoiling it

December brings more flames, more leads and more dry wood into a home than the rest of the year together. None of the rules below costs anything, and none of them shows.

This is the one page to follow exactly

Everything else in these pages is a matter of how a room looks. This page is a matter of whether it is still there in January. If in doubt about any appliance, any socket or any lead, ask an electrician, not a shop.

Lights and leads

  • Every strand checked on the floor before it goes up: no cracked wire, no loose bulb, no scorch mark on the plug
  • Strands joined only up to the number printed on the label
  • Indoor lights indoors, outdoor lights outdoors, on an outdoor socket
  • No lead under a rug, through a closed door or across a walkway
  • Lights off when the house is empty and when everyone is asleep — the timer does it

Flames

  • No candle on or near a tree, real or artificial
  • Thirty centimetres, a foot, between any flame and anything that can burn
  • Candles on a stable, non-flammable holder, out of reach of children and animals
  • Never a candle left burning in an empty room

The tree

  • A metre from radiators, stoves and fires
  • A real tree watered every day, and taken out once it drops needles at a touch
  • Stable in its stand, and tied discreetly to a hook where there are small children or a climbing cat
  • Glass baubles high, where small hands and tails do not reach

The rest

  • Holly and mistletoe berries out of reach of children and pets
  • Batteries for remote candles and toys stored away from small children, who can swallow button cells
  • The smoke alarm tested on the day the tree goes up

Early January

Putting it away well

Next Christmas is decided on the day this one comes down, in about an hour, by whether the lights go into the box in a ball or on a card.

In many French homes the season ends with the galette des rois in early January, and the tree goes out soon after. Many towns collect real trees in the weeks after New Year and turn them into mulch or compost; look for the collection point your town announces, rather than leaving the tree on the pavement.

The hour that saves next December an evening

  1. Photograph the tree and the mantel before anything comes down. Next year you will want to know where the bird went, and how long the garland was.
  2. Lights off the tree in reverse, tip to trunk, and wound around a piece of stiff card, plug end first, so the plug end comes out first next year. One strand per card, and a note of its length.
  3. Batteries out of every battery candle, every timer and every lit figure. A battery left in for eleven months can leak and ruin what it powers.
  4. Glass in egg boxes or wrapped in tissue, never newspaper, whose ink can mark painted glass. Heaviest at the bottom of the box.
  5. Ribbon rolled, not folded, around a cardboard tube, so it does not crease.
  6. Label the boxes by job, not by contents: tree, first; mantel; table; window. The order you take them out next year is the order of this book.
  7. The swap box back. The everyday objects return to their places, and the room is the room again.

Where to keep it

Somewhere dry, with a steady temperature. Not a loft that bakes in summer, where candles bend and adhesive perishes, and not a damp cellar, where cardboard softens and ribbon spots. A cupboard in the flat is better than either, if there is room, and the room there is is the reason the swap box exists.

Colette’s rule of January

Pack it away the way you would want to find it. Next December begins today.
