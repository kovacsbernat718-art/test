How to use this list

These are working figures, not laws. I give them because a number you can act on tonight is worth more than a principle you have to interpret.

Three things to know before you use any of them

  1. A range means a range. Where I write 15–25 cm, anywhere in that band is right, and the choice between them is usually decided by what else is on the wall. Where I give a single number — 145 cm for the centre of a picture — it is single because the eye genuinely notices when it is wrong.
  2. The room wins the argument. If a figure here fights something obvious in front of you — a beam, a radiator, a window that starts at hip height — the room is right and the number is wrong. Page 88 is about exactly that conflict.
  3. Measure twice, in the room, at the time of day you use it. Almost every mistake I ever made in fourteen years was made by measuring once, in the morning, from the doorway.

Metric and imperial

Both are given throughout. Where the imperial figure is a round number and the metric is not — 57 inches, 145 centimetres — that is because the convention came from a country that measures in inches, and I have not tidied it into a rounder metric number that would be slightly wrong.

From the notebook

“My grandmother wrote the drop of every window she ever measured, and never once wrote the reason. Forty-one years of numbers and no arguments. I have tried to give you both.”

Two habits worth more than any figure here

  • Write it down in the room. Not on your telephone as you walk away — on paper, standing where you measured. Every wrong curtain I ever ordered was ordered from a number I was sure I remembered.
  • Mark it before you commit to it. Masking tape on the wall for the pole, newspaper on the floor for the rug, a pencil cross for the picture hook. Live with it for a day. It costs nothing and it has saved me more money than any other habit in this book.

Watts tell you what a bulb costs to run, not how bright it is. Lumens are the brightness. The two were the same thing for a century and have not been for fifteen years, and the habit is the single commonest reason people buy a bulb that disappoints them.

Two, continued

Measuring a window, in order

This is my grandmother’s sequence, unchanged. Write every figure down before you calculate anything, because the arithmetic is where the mistakes live.

  1. Decide where the pole goes first, and mark it on the wall in pencil. Every other figure depends on it, and almost everybody measures the window instead.
  2. Measure the pole width, bracket to bracket, once the pole position is marked — not the window.
  3. Measure the drop from the ring to the floor, not from the pole. The curtain hangs from the bottom of the ring, and the difference is four or five centimetres, which is exactly the amount that looks wrong.
  4. Measure the drop in three places — left, middle, right. Floors are not level. Work to the shortest.
  5. Multiply the pole width by your fullness to get the fabric width you need, then divide by the number of curtain panels.
  6. Add the hem allowances the maker asks for, and add them again if you are unsure. Fabric can be shortened. It cannot be lengthened.

The one that catches everybody

Ready-made curtains are sold by the width of the fabric, not the width of the window. A pair labelled 168 cm wide means each panel is 168 cm of cloth — which on a 200 cm pole gives you 336 over 200, about 1.7 ×, and that is why so many bought curtains look thin. Check the label, then check your arithmetic.

Full working, with a diagram, is on page 96.

The arithmetic, worked once

A window 120 cm wide. The pole goes 20 cm beyond each side, so the pole is 160 cm. At 2.5 × fullness that is 400 cm of fabric, over two panels: 200 cm of cloth in each. The ring sits 7 cm below the pole and the floor is 242 cm down from it, so the drop is 241 cm to float one centimetre clear. Write those four numbers — 160, 2.5, 200, 241 — and you can order curtains from anybody in the world.

Know what your wall is before you drill

Solid masonry, plasterboard on studs, and old lath and plaster all take completely different fixings, and a fixing meant for one of them will fail in the others. Tap the wall: a dull thud is solid, a hollow drum is plasterboard. And check for cables and pipes before any hole — they run vertically and horizontally from sockets and switches, which is exactly where people hang pictures.

Rough guidance on what a wall will carry

Solid masonry, with the right plug almost anything domestic
Plasterboard, into a stud shelves, mirrors, poles
Plasterboard, between studs, with a proper anchor pictures, light shelves
Old lath and plaster find the timber, or ask

Rugs

The most expensive common mistake in a home is a rug one size too small, so these are the figures I would defend hardest.

Sitting room

Minimum that works — front legs of every seat standing on it
Better — all legs of everything on it
Margin left between the rug and the wall 20–45 cm
Clear walking width around it 60–70 cm

Dining and bedroom

Dining — beyond the table edge, all round 60–75 cm · 24–30 in
Which is the distance a chair travels when pulled out and the whole reason for it
Bedroom — beyond the sides of the bed 45–60 cm
Or two runners, one each side 60–80 cm wide

Under it

A pad, on any floor always
Over carpet, the pad must be made for carpet

And the rule that saves the most money: measure the rug you want on the floor in masking tape before you buy it. Ten minutes, no cost, and it has stopped me buying the wrong size more times than I would like to admit.

Nine

What to take to the shop

Every disappointing purchase I have ever made was made without one of these six things in my pocket. Photograph this page.

  1. The measurements of the space it is going into, written down — not the room, the space. Width, depth, height, and the distance to the nearest thing it must not touch.
  2. The narrowest point of the route home. Door, stair, turn, lift. A sofa that fits the room and not the stairwell is the most expensive mistake in this book.
  3. A photograph of the room, taken in the evening with the lamps on. Not in daylight. You will use the thing at night.
  4. A sample of whatever it has to live beside — a cushion cover, a paint card, a photograph of the floor. Colour is a relationship, not a property, and you cannot hold it in your memory for the length of a bus ride.
  5. A tape measure. Shop labels round generously.
  6. The question, written down: can this be repaired? Ask it out loud, of a person, in the shop. The answer, and the face they make while giving it, tells you most of what you need to know.

And one rule for the shop itself

Never judge a colour or a fabric under shop lighting. Take the sample outside, or to the door, or home. Page 35 explains why every shop you buy from is lit to flatter what it sells.

And two things to do on the day it arrives

  1. Open it and look at it before the delivery leaves. Damage found in the first five minutes is the shop’s problem. Damage found next week is an argument.
  2. Put it where it is going before you fill it. A bookcase is very heavy once it holds books, and a sofa cannot be turned round in a doorway once the room is rearranged round it.

The ten that matter most

If you remember nothing else from this part, remember these. They account for most of what is wrong in most homes.

One. Centre of a picture 145 cm
Two. Light sources per room, minimum 3, at 3 heights
Three. Bulb colour, everywhere you sit 2700 K, CRI 90+
Four. Curtain pole, above the frame 15–20 cm
Five. Curtain fullness 2.5 × the pole
Six. Curtain drop to the floor, always
Seven. Rug — front legs of every seat on it
Eight. Pendant above a table 75–90 cm
Nine. Walkway 75 cm, 90 for a main route
Ten. Float furniture off the wall 5–15 cm

Eight of those ten cost nothing at all. That is not an accident; it is the argument of this whole book put into numbers.

And the five that do cost something

In the order I would spend, if the money arrived slowly: the right-sized rug, one lined curtain on the best window, one good chair that can be reupholstered, one large piece of art, and a proper shade for the lamp you already own. Everything else on the previous list is a bulb, a tape measure and an evening.
