Where to start

Thirty days

One home, one month, and almost no money. This is the order I would work in if I walked into your flat tomorrow.

Week one — light, and nothing else

  • Tonight. The audit on page 183, for the room you use most.
  • Day two. Read every bulb in the house and write a list.
  • Day three. Buy them. 2700 K, CRI 90+. Change them all in one evening.
  • Day four to seven. Move lamps you already own until the main room has three sources at three heights, and leave the ceiling light off all week.

Week two — height, and an afternoon

  • Every picture and mirror re-hung to 145 cm at its centre.
  • Every piece of furniture pulled off its wall by a hand’s width.
  • One curtain pole raised and widened, if you are allowed to. If not, page 132.

Week three — texture, one object at a time

  • Count the four families in each room and name what is missing.
  • Buy the one cheapest thing that supplies it — usually ceramic or stone, usually small.
  • Break one matched set.

Week four — and only now, colour

  • Re-do the audit. Compare the two scores.
  • If colour is still the lowest, test two candidates properly, on lining paper, over a weekend — page 47.
  • If it is not, spend the paint money on the rug instead.

Three of those four weeks cost almost nothing. That is the book.

The words of the trade

TermWhat it means
Kelvin (K)The colour of a light, not its brightness. Lower is warmer. 2700 K is the warm light of an old bulb; 4000 K is an office.
Lumens (lm)How bright a bulb actually is. Watts tell you what it costs to run, which stopped meaning brightness fifteen years ago.

The honest part

Where the numbers come from

You have been given about a hundred figures. You are owed an account of where each kind of them came from, because they are not all the same kind of thing.

Four sorts of number, and how much to trust them

  1. Measured standards. Kelvin, lumens, CRI and Martindale are defined by international test standards and printed on the packaging by law or convention. These are facts. If a box gives you one, believe it.
  2. Trade conventions. The centre of a picture at 145 cm — 57 inches — is the hanging height used by galleries and museums, and it is a convention rather than a law of nature. It is a convention that has been tested on millions of visitors, which is why I give it as a single number.
  3. Ergonomic ranges. Worktop heights, desk heights, seat-to-table distances, walking clearances. These come from furniture and building practice, they vary a little between countries, and a person much taller or shorter than average should adjust them without guilt.
  4. My own working figures. Fullness at 2.5 ×. Three sources at three heights. Halving an adhesive rating. Pulling a sofa 5–15 cm off the wall. These are fourteen years of one person’s practice in one city, and they are the ones a reasonable professional might argue with.

Two claims I have deliberately softened

  • Houseplants and air quality. The famous experiments were done in sealed chambers. At the densities of an ordinary room the effect is negligible, and I say so on page 112 rather than repeat the claim.
  • Colour and mood. There is real research, and there is a great deal of confident decorating folklore built on top of it. Where I say a dark room feels enclosing I am describing what people reported to me, not a measured psychological effect.

What I did not verify

Prices, because they differ everywhere and date within a year — where I mention cost it is an order of magnitude, never a quotation. Product names, because I would rather teach you to read a label than send you to a shelf that has changed. And local building and electrical regulations, which differ by country and are the one place in this book where you must ask somebody qualified where you live.

The honest part

What I might be wrong about

A book that never says this is a book that is selling something. Here are the six positions in these two hundred pages that I hold least firmly.

  1. That a small room should not be painted pale. It is my most unpopular position — page 57 — and it is drawn from small rooms in old European buildings with poor light. In a small, bright, modern room, pale may well be right and I may be arguing from my own climate.
  2. That the overhead light should be off. True of every home I worked in. Not necessarily true of a room with a very high ceiling and a beautiful fitting, or of anyone whose eyes need more light than mine do. Light is also an accessibility question, and atmosphere must lose that argument every time.
  3. That matched sets are always wrong. Held firmly, but it is a position about how rooms read, not a fact about furniture, and there are households where the calm of a matched set is worth more than the interest of a broken one.
  4. That you should buy fewer, better things. Good advice with savings behind it and a lecture without them. Page 141 is my attempt to be fair about this and I am not sure I succeeded.
  5. That every room needs something living. Not every household can keep a plant alive, and a dead plant is worse than no plant. Take stone or dried branches instead and lose nothing.
  6. The rug sizes. They come from rooms of a certain size. In a very small flat, a rug that puts every leg on it may not exist, and front legs on is not a compromise there — it is the answer.

Everything else in this book I would defend at a table with anybody in the trade.

How to disagree with this book usefully

Do the thing first, live with it for a fortnight, and then disagree. Almost every correction here looks wrong on the first evening — a picture at 145 cm looks too high, a sofa off the wall looks like it has been pushed, a dark room looks alarming at lunchtime. Two weeks is how long it takes an eye to stop arguing. If you still dislike it then, you are right and I am wrong, and it is your home.

Last words

The cahier

I have the notebook in front of me as I write this. It is cloth-bound, the corners are gone, and it is full of numbers in a hand I would know anywhere.

There is nothing beautiful in it. Page after page of windows: the drop, the width, the fullness, the colour mixed to go with it, and the name of the family. Madame Rivière, salon, deux fenêtres, 2m40. No opinions. No photographs. Forty-one years of one woman measuring other people’s rooms and writing down what was true of them.

When I was young I thought it was a record of work. I understand now that it is a record of attention. She could not afford any of those rooms. She was never going to live in one of them. And she looked at each one properly anyway, and wrote down what it needed, and went home.

That is the whole of what I have tried to give you. Not a style — styles belong to other people and go out of fashion while you are still paying for them. Attention. The ability to walk into a room, including your own, and see what is actually happening in it instead of what you feel about it.

You will not need this book for very long. That is the point of it. In a year you will hang a picture without measuring, and know from the doorway that a room is lit from one place, and stop yourself in a shop because you have remembered to ask whether the thing can be repaired. Then the book goes on a shelf and you get on with your life, which is what a home is actually for.

She made me promise to write it down before it was lost. It is written down. Thank you for reading it.

And the last law, which is the first one

We do not decorate a room, ma chère. We correct it.

The notebook is going to my daughter, who does not want it yet and will. There is no rule in it that has not been in this book somewhere, and there is nothing in it about style at all. Only windows, and the light that came through them, and who lived there.

If you would like more

This book is the whole method. What follows is only where to find the rest of it, and how to tell me when I am wrong.

The films

Everything in this book began as something I said out loud on Chez Colette. If you have arrived here from there, you already know the voice. If you have not, the channel is the same method in twenty-minute pieces — one room, one fault, one correction at a time — and it costs nothing.

Tell me what happened

I would genuinely rather have one photograph of a corrected room than a hundred compliments. If you do the audit on page 183 and fix the thing it points at, tell me. The corrections readers send me are where the next edition of this book will come from — particularly from homes unlike mine: different climates, different light, different housing.

Two requests

  1. Print pages 183 to 187, even if you read the rest of this on a screen. The audit only works with a pencil.
  2. Do one thing tonight. Not the whole plan. One thing. Turn the ceiling light off and put a lamp on the floor in the corner. Then sit down where you always sit and look at the room you have lived in for years.

Your taste was never the problem. Nobody ever showed you what to look at. Now somebody has.

— Colette Dubois, Lyon

One last practical thing

This is a file, and files get lost. Save a copy somewhere you will still have it in five years, and print the audit pages now while you are thinking of it. I would rather you had four sheets of paper and a pencil than two hundred pages you never opened again.
