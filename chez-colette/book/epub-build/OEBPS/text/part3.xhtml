Colour

Colour is the correction people reach for first and should make fourth. This part covers how to test a colour so it cannot surprise you, what an undertone is and how to see one, the six whites worth knowing, why a small pale room looks smaller, and what to do with the ceiling.

Chapter three

Colour is not a property of paint

This is the sentence that makes the rest of this part make sense, so I am going to spend a page on it.

When you look at a painted wall, what reaches your eye is not the paint. It is light that has hit the paint and bounced off it, having lost the wavelengths the pigment absorbed. So the colour you see is the product of two things: what the paint absorbs, and what the light was made of before it arrived.

Change either one and the colour changes. Not your impression of it — the actual light entering your eye.

This is why the same tin produces two different colours on a north wall and a south wall in the same flat. It is why the colour you chose under the shop's lights is not the colour you have at home. And it is why so many people conclude they are bad at choosing colours, when what actually happened is that they chose correctly for a light they do not own.

The first law again, because now you can see why

Light before colour. You cannot choose a colour until you know which way the window faces.

The name for this, since knowing it helps

When two colours match under one light and stop matching under another, the effect is called metamerism. It is the reason a cushion and a wall that agreed in the shop can disagree in your sitting room, and it is not anybody's fault. Two different pigment mixes can reflect the same overall colour under one light source and separate completely under another.

You cannot prevent it. You can only ever judge a colour in the place and the light it will live in — which is the entire method of the next four pages.

What this part will and will not do

It will give you a way of testing colour that removes almost all of the risk, a working understanding of undertone, the handful of whites that are worth knowing by behaviour, and a clear position on ceilings, woodwork and sheen.

It will not give you a palette to copy. I do not know your windows, and anybody who offers you a colour scheme without knowing which way your room faces is selling you something.

How to actually test a colour

One method. It takes two days of waiting and about twenty minutes of work, and it is the difference between choosing a colour and gambling on one.

  1. Buy sample pots, not chips. Two or three shortlisted colours. A chip is printed ink on card; a sample pot is the actual paint.
  2. Paint two large sheets of paper per colour, not the wall. A2 or larger — the biggest cheap paper you can find. Two coats, edge to edge, and let it dry fully. Paper rather than wall for three reasons: you can move it, the colour underneath is not showing through, and you are not left with test patches to cover later.
  3. Leave a white border, or cut the sheet to size. Colour is judged against whatever surrounds it. A patch of colour on an existing wall is being read against that wall and will look wrong in a way that tells you nothing.
  4. Move the sheets around the room and leave them for two days. On the wall facing the window. On the wall opposite it. In the darkest corner. Beside the curtain. Next to the sofa. Tape them up and go about your life.
  5. Look at them at four moments — morning, the middle of the day, dusk, and at night with only your own lamps on. A colour that survives all four is a colour you can commit to. Most shortlists lose a candidate at dusk.

Why paper and not the wall

A patch painted on a wall is being judged against the colour beside it. A sheet you can move is being judged against the room.

The cost of skipping this

A sample pot and two sheets of paper cost a few dollars. Repainting a room because the colour was wrong costs a weekend, the paint, and the furniture moving — twice. I have never met anybody who regretted the two days.

Why a two-centimetre chip tells you nothing

Every paint brand hands you a fan of small printed rectangles and invites you to choose a wall from one. It cannot be done, and here are the four reasons.

One · It is ink, not paint

A printed chip is a reproduction. It has none of the paint's actual pigment, none of its finish, and none of the way real paint sits and scatters light on a surface. It is a photograph of a colour.

Two · Scale changes what you see

A colour covering a whole wall reads as considerably more saturated and usually darker than the same colour on a small card. This is partly the eye adapting and partly light bouncing between walls, each bounce deepening the colour. A colour that looks gentle on a chip can be startling at four square metres. **Choose one or two steps paler than the chip you liked.**

Three · It is surrounded by other colours

On a fan deck your chip sits between two neighbours, and your eye reads it against them. Pull it out and lay it on white paper and it is a different colour. This is simultaneous contrast and you cannot switch it off; you can only control what surrounds the sample.

Four · You are looking at it under shop light

Which is roughly four thousand kelvin from overhead and has nothing to do with your home. Page 35.

The one thing on a paint card worth reading

Many brands print an LRV — Light Reflectance Value — a number from 0 to 100 describing how much light the colour bounces back, where 0 is a theoretical black and 100 a theoretical white. It is the single most useful figure on the card, because it predicts how a colour will behave in a dark room better than the colour itself does. Anything above about 70 is behaving as a pale colour. Below about 25 it is behaving as a dark one.

Use the chips to narrow fifty colours to three. Then stop using them and paint the paper.

Reading the sheets once they are up

You have three painted sheets taped around the room and two days have passed. This is how to read them, because most people look and cannot say what they are seeing.

Ask four questions, in this order

  1. At the darkest moment of the day, is it still a colour? Look at dusk, before the lamps go on. Many pale colours collapse to grey at this hour. If it goes dead, it will be dead every evening of the year.
  2. In the brightest light it gets, does it go chalky or garish? Direct sun on a pale colour can bleach it to nothing; direct sun on a saturated one can make it shout.
  3. Under your own lamps at night, has it changed hue? Not just darkened — changed. Greens go grey, greys go lilac, whites go yellow. This is the test that catches the most mistakes, and it is the one nobody runs.
  4. Against the things that are staying in the room — the floor, the sofa, the curtain, the door — does it sit comfortably or does one of them start to look wrong? Move the sheet next to each in turn.

What a good answer looks like

The colour you want is the one that is slightly less interesting than its rivals at every hour, and never actively wrong at any of them. Colours that are thrilling at one particular moment are usually exhausting to live with, because the other twenty-three hours are the compromise.

From the notebook

My father used to say that the colour you notice is the colour you will get tired of. He chose for the light, not for the card, and he always looked at a colour last thing at night before he would agree to mix a full tin of it.

If two survive

Take the paler one. Every colour goes a little deeper once it covers a whole room and the light starts bouncing between the walls, and almost nobody in the history of decorating has wished their walls were darker after the second coat.

Undertone, and why nobody explains it

This is the concept that separates people who can choose colours from people who cannot, and it is usually described so vaguely that it sounds like intuition. It is not. It is a simple thing.

Almost no paint colour is pure. A grey is never only grey; there is a little blue in it, or a little green, or a little violet, or a little brown. That trace is the undertone, and it is what the colour reveals when it is put next to something else or lit by something else.

You can ignore a colour's undertone right up until the moment it meets another colour. Then it is the only thing that matters. A grey with a blue undertone beside a grey with a green undertone will look dirty, and neither is a bad colour. They simply disagree.

Why this ruins so many rooms

Because most rooms are not one colour. They are a wall colour, a ceiling white, a woodwork white, a floor, a sofa and a curtain — six things, each with an undertone, chosen at different times by different logics. If four of them lean warm and two lean cool, the room will feel subtly wrong and nobody will be able to say why. Everything in it is perfectly nice.

The practical rule

You do not need to know which undertone a room has. You need all of them to be the same one.

The shortcut, if you take nothing else from this page

Decide once, for the whole flat, whether you are warm or cool. Then choose everything on that side — the whites, the greys, the timber, the metals. A consistently warm room and a consistently cool room are both beautiful. A room that is half of each is the one that reads as cheap.

Which one should you pick? Look at what you cannot change. The floor, usually. A honey oak floor has decided that your flat is warm, whatever you were planning. Fighting the largest surface in the room is a losing argument.

How to see an undertone

You cannot see an undertone by staring at a colour on its own. You can only see it by comparison, and once you know the trick you will never lose it.

The white paper test

Take a sheet of ordinary printer paper — which is a very cool, very bright white — and hold your sample against it.

  • If the sample suddenly looks yellow, pink or brown, it is warm.
  • If it looks blue, grey or slightly violet, it is cool.
  • If it looks green, it is green, and greens behave as their own family — see below.

That is the whole test. It takes four seconds and it works on paint, fabric, tile and timber equally.

The three-sample test, which is better

Put your candidate between two colours you already know. If you have a warm white and a cool white to hand, lay the sample between them. The one it resembles is the side it is on. This is more reliable than the paper test because it gives your eye two references instead of one.

The beige trap, since it catches everyone

Beiges and taupes are the hardest colours in the shop, because a great many of them have a pink or mauve undertone that is invisible on a chip and unmistakable on a wall in afternoon light. If you are considering a beige, run the paper test twice — once in daylight and once under a lamp — before you buy anything.

There is no such thing as white

Every paint brand sells between forty and a hundred whites. That is not cynicism. It is because white is not a colour but a whole family of them, and the differences are the entire point.

A white paint is a white pigment with a small amount of something else added. What that something else is decides everything about how the colour behaves on your wall.

The three things that differ between whites

  • The undertone. Yellow, red, blue, green or grey, in a very small quantity. This is what decides whether your room feels warm or cold.
  • How much of it there is. A white with a trace of yellow is a warm white; a white with a good deal of yellow is cream, which behaves as a colour and not as a white.
  • Whether any black or grey has been added. A white knocked back with grey is softer, less reflective, and much easier to live with than a brilliant white — and most of the whites that decorators use are of this kind.

The one to be careful of

Most brands sell a brilliant white or pure white which has optical brighteners in it, making it the coldest and most reflective thing you can put on a wall. It is designed to read as clean and it does. It is also unforgiving: it shows every flaw in the plaster, it goes blue in a north room, and it makes everything you put against it look slightly grubby, including other whites.

It has real uses — a gallery, a workshop, a ceiling in a room that needs every photon it can get. It is almost never the right white for a room where people sit.

If you remember one thing about white

A white with a little grey in it looks expensive. A brilliant white looks like a rental.

That is a judgement rather than a fact, and it is one I will defend. Brilliant white is what a landlord reaches for because it is cheap, fast and neutral to every future tenant. Your eye has learned that association, and it applies it to your own walls.

The six whites

Described by behaviour, not by brand

I am not going to give you product names, because they change, they differ by country, and a name tells you nothing about what a colour will do in your room. I am going to describe six whites by how they behave. Every brand sells all six.

One · Brilliant white

No undertone, often optically brightened. LRV near 90 or above.

The coldest and most reflective. Shows every imperfection. Goes blue in north light. Makes adjacent whites look dirty.

Use it for: a ceiling in a genuinely dark room where reflectance matters more than warmth. A workshop. Almost nothing else.

Two · Soft white — white with a little grey

Grey undertone, LRV roughly 80 to 88.

The most useful white there is. Reads as white but has enough body to stop it glaring, and it is forgiving of imperfect plaster. Works in more rooms than anything else in this list.

Use it for: walls in almost any room, ceilings in most rooms, and woodwork if you want it quiet. If you cannot decide, this is the answer.

Three · Warm white — white with a little yellow

Yellow undertone, LRV roughly 80 to 88.

Gentle, slightly sunny, flatters skin and timber. In strong south light it can tip towards cream, which some people love and some find dated.

Use it for: north-facing rooms, which need the warmth; rooms with honey or oak floors; bedrooms.

Reading the numbers

LRV figures here are the ordinary ranges these whites fall into, not a rule. Use them to compare candidates on the same card: a white at 84 will behave noticeably differently in a dark room from one at 74, and the number will tell you that before your eye does.

The six whites, continued

Four · Cool white — white with a little blue or violet

Blue undertone, LRV roughly 80 to 88.

Crisp and slightly austere. In a south-facing room it is genuinely beautiful, because the warm sunlight cancels the blue and leaves something very clean. In a north-facing room it is bleak.

Use it for: south and west rooms, bathrooms, kitchens with a lot of daylight. Never a north room.

Five · Greige — white knocked well back with warm grey

Brown-grey undertone, LRV roughly 60 to 75.

No longer really a white; it is a very pale colour. Reads as soft, quiet and slightly expensive, and it is extremely forgiving of poor light and poor plaster.

Use it for: the room you cannot make work. Hallways, north rooms, rooms with a lot of mismatched furniture that needs calming down.

Six · Cream — white with a good deal of yellow

Strong yellow undertone, LRV roughly 70 to 84.

Behaves as a colour, not a white. Warm, soft, and strongly associated with older interiors; whether that reads as handsome or dated depends entirely on what you put with it. It is unbeatable against dark timber, old brass and worn stone.

Use it for: period rooms, rooms with substantial woodwork, anywhere with an open fire.

The most common white mistake

Using two different whites in the same room without meaning to — a cool white on the ceiling and a warm white on the walls, or trade white on the woodwork and something chosen on the walls. The eye reads the mismatch as dirt rather than as a decision. Either use one white throughout, or make the difference large enough to be obviously deliberate.

Where white fails

White is the default, which means it is chosen far more often than it is decided. Here are the five rooms where it is the wrong answer, and what to do instead.

One · The room with no daylight

Covered on page 39 and worth repeating here: white needs light to be white. In a sunless room it goes grey and dead, and a grey dead room announces what it is missing. Go warm and deep instead.

Two · The small room you want to feel finished

See the next page, which argues this one out properly.

Three · The room with bad plaster

White, and especially brilliant white, is the least forgiving finish there is. Every ripple, patch and filled hole reads. A colour with some depth in it, in a matt finish, hides a surprising amount. If your walls are old and uneven, paint is doing repair work as well as decoration.

Four · The transitional space

Hallways, landings, corridors, cloakrooms. You are in them for seconds, so they can carry a much stronger colour than a room you sit in — and because they are small and dark they tend to make white look grubby. This is the cheapest place in the house to be brave.

Five · The room that is already full of white things

A white kitchen with white units, white tiles and white appliances does not need white walls as well. At some point the room stops reading as clean and starts reading as unresolved, because there is nothing for the eye to measure the whites against.

The test

Ask whether you chose white or defaulted to it. If you cannot say what the alternative was, you defaulted.

None of which is an argument against white. Half the rooms I have worked in ended up white, and page 55 exists because choosing the right one matters. It is an argument against white as a way of avoiding the decision.

My most unpopular position

A small room painted pale looks smaller

I want to mark this clearly: this is my professional opinion and not a law of physics. It is also the piece of advice I have most often been argued with about, and most often been thanked for later.

The standard advice is that pale colours make a small room feel larger. The reasoning is that pale walls reflect more light, and a brighter room reads as bigger. The first half of that is true — a high-LRV colour genuinely does bounce more light. The conclusion does not follow.

What actually happens in a small pale room

  • You can see exactly where the walls are. A pale wall is legible. Every corner, every junction, every edge is clearly drawn, and your eye measures the room accurately — which in a small room is not what you want.
  • Every flaw is on display. The radiator, the cables, the patched plaster, the mismatched skirting. In a small room these are close to you and there is nowhere for them to hide.
  • It reads as unfinished. Because the association is with rooms that have not been decided about: rentals, new builds before anyone moves in, rooms between tenants.

What happens in a small dark room

The corners soften and the eye loses the exact edges of the space, so the room stops announcing its dimensions. The flaws recede. And most importantly it reads as deliberate — nobody paints a room deep green by accident, so the room is immediately understood as a choice rather than a shortage.

Colette’s rule of the small pale room

A small room painted pale does not look bigger. It looks small and cheap. A small room painted deep looks deliberate.

Where I concede

If the small room is also the only room, and you are in it all day, and it has one small window, a deep colour can genuinely be oppressive. And if you are selling the flat, pale is the commercially safe answer, because buyers are told the same advice I am arguing with. Both of those are real. Neither is about how the room looks.

What a deep colour actually does

If you are going to take the argument on the last page seriously, you need to know what you are buying. Four things happen, and the third surprises people.

One · The edges of the room stop being legible

In a dark room, the corner where two walls meet carries very little contrast, so your eye cannot place it precisely. The room's boundary becomes approximate. This is the whole mechanism behind “it feels bigger”, and it is the opposite of the reason usually given.

Two · Everything in front of the wall gains contrast

A pale lamp against a dark wall has enormous presence. A picture frame reads. A plant reads. A face reads. In a pale room all of these are competing with a bright background and lose; in a dark room they are lit objects against a quiet ground, which is how a painting is hung in a gallery and why it works.

Three · The lighting starts doing much more work

This is the one people are not warned about. A dark room absorbs light rather than bouncing it, so a single ceiling fitting will not carry it — the room will simply be gloomy. Deep colour and the three heights from Part Two are a package. If you paint a room dark and light it from one point, you will hate it, and you will blame the colour.

Four · The room acquires a time of day

A dark room is at its best in the evening, under lamps, and is frankly a little sombre at ten in the morning. That is fine for a sitting room, a dining room or a study. It is a real consideration for a room you work in all day.

Before you commit

Paint the sheets, as always, but for a deep colour paint four and put them on all four walls at once, because a dark colour on one wall of a pale room tells you nothing about a dark room. And check page 61 first: in most cases a deep colour wants the ceiling and woodwork to go with it, and that changes the size of the job.

How dark is dark enough

The commonest failure with a deep colour is not going far enough. A mid-tone is the one place you genuinely should not stop.

There are broadly three places a wall colour can sit, and the middle one is the trap.

RoughlyReads asVerdict
LRV 70–90 Pale. The wall is a background and stays quiet. Works. Commits to being light.
LRV 35–70 Mid-tone. Too dark to disappear, too pale to enclose. The trap. Often reads as a colour that was meant to be braver.
LRV under 25 Deep. The wall recedes and becomes a ground. Works. Commits to being dark.

These bands are a working guide rather than a standard, and the boundaries are soft — a saturated mid-tone in a well-lit room can be excellent. But if you find yourself choosing a colour because it seems like a sensible compromise between pale and dark, that is usually the signal to go one way or the other.

Why the middle fails

Because both mechanisms need commitment. A pale colour works by reflecting light and staying out of the way; a deep colour works by absorbing light and softening the edges. A mid-tone does neither. It is bright enough that you still read every corner, and dark enough that the room is noticeably dimmer. You have paid the cost of the dark room and bought none of the benefit.

From the notebook

A client in Lyon asked my grandmother what colour to paint a small dining room. She said: either a colour you could read a book against, or a colour you could not. Anything in between and you will repaint it within two years. She was right about that woman, who repainted it within two years.

The practical version

Choose your candidate. Then find the version of it two or three steps darker on the same strip and test that one too. Nine times out of ten the darker one is the answer.

The fifth wall

What to do with a ceiling

You met le cinquième mur on page 11. Here is the decision, and there are only three answers.

One · A white, but chosen rather than assumed

The default, and correct more often than not — but not brilliant white, and not a different white from the walls. The usual best answer is the wall colour mixed with white at about a quarter strength, which most merchants will do for you. It reads as white, it belongs to the room, and the junction where wall meets ceiling stops being a hard line.

Two · The same colour as the walls

Wall colour taken straight across the ceiling. This is the one that surprises people, and in the right room it is the best thing in this part of the book. What it does is remove the boundary between wall and ceiling, so the room reads as a single volume rather than a box with a lid.

Best in: small rooms, rooms with awkward angles or sloping ceilings, box rooms, and any room you want to feel enclosing rather than tall. It is also the answer to a ceiling covered in pipework or patches, because it stops advertising them.

Three · Darker than the walls

A deliberate move for a room with a high ceiling that feels cold or cavernous. A darker ceiling appears to come closer, which brings a tall room down to a human scale.

Best in: high-ceilinged period rooms — a Haussmannian apartment runs between two point eight and three point two metres, and a room like that can carry a dark ceiling easily. A dining room. Anywhere you want the evening to feel intimate.

The one thing not to do

Leave the old brilliant white ceiling in place while painting the walls a considered colour. It is the most common half-finished job there is: a careful colour on four surfaces and a cold builder's white on the fifth, meeting it in a hard line all the way round the room. Whatever you choose, choose it.

One colour, everywhere

Walls, ceiling, woodwork, skirting, door, window frame — all in the same colour. It sounds extreme. It is the single most reliable way to make an ordinary room look considered.

Why it works

Every junction in a room is a place where the eye stops: wall to ceiling, wall to skirting, wall to architrave, architrave to door. An ordinary room has dozens, and each one is drawn in high contrast because the woodwork is white and the wall is not. Your eye spends the whole time tracing an outline of the room's plumbing.

Take the contrast out of those junctions and they stop being events. What is left is a single quiet volume with your furniture in it — and the furniture, the art and the people become the things you look at, because they are now the only things with contrast in the room.

Where it earns its keep most

  • Rooms with ugly or excessive woodwork. Boxed-in pipes, a radiator, four doors, a fuse box. Painting all of it out is the only cheap fix that genuinely works.
  • Small rooms and awkward shapes. Attic rooms with sloping ceilings, box rooms, under-stair spaces.
  • Hallways, which are almost entirely made of junctions.

How to do it properly

Use one colour in different finishes, not one finish everywhere: matt on the walls and ceiling, eggshell or satin on the woodwork. The colour reads as continuous, but the woodwork is durable and washable, and the slight difference in sheen gives the room a subtle life that a single flat finish does not have. Page 65 covers sheens.

Why this is not just a trend

Before white paint was cheap, rooms were routinely finished in one colour throughout, because mixing several was expensive. What looks like a bold modern choice is mostly a return to the ordinary.

Skirtings, architraves and doors

Woodwork is white because woodwork is white. It is the same unexamined default as the ceiling, and it is doing more harm in more rooms.

Add up the woodwork in an ordinary room: skirting all the way round, architrave round the door and the window, the door itself, perhaps a picture rail. It is a considerable area, and in most homes it is painted the brightest white in the house, in the shiniest finish in the house, outlining every edge of the room in high contrast.

The four options, from quietest to loudest

  1. The same colour as the walls. Everything disappears. See the previous page. The best answer in most small and awkward rooms.
  2. A shade or two deeper than the walls, same colour family. The woodwork reads as a soft frame rather than a bright outline. Elegant, easy, and almost impossible to get wrong.
  3. A soft white or greige rather than brilliant white. If you want the contrast of white woodwork without the glare. This is the standard decorator's answer and it suits period rooms with good mouldings that deserve to be seen.
  4. A deliberately contrasting colour. A deep green skirting under pale walls, a black door. A real move with real presence, and the one most likely to go wrong. Commit to it across the whole room or it looks like an unfinished job.

The door is worth its own thought

A door is a large flat rectangle at eye level, and painting it the wall colour makes it vanish — which is usually what you want in a small room with several doors. Painting it a deep colour when the walls are pale makes it an object, which is worth doing when the door is handsome and there is only one of them.

Old paint, and please read this one

Woodwork in older buildings can carry many layers. In the United States, lead-based paint was banned for residential use in 1978; other countries have their own dates, generally in the same era. Sanding old woodwork is what makes lead dangerous, because it puts it into the air. If your building predates that and you are planning to sand woodwork back, get the paint tested first. This is not a job to guess at, and it is the one place in this book where I would rather you did nothing than proceed uncertain.

The accent wall, properly examined

I dismissed this in one paragraph on page 20. It is common enough to deserve the full argument, and there is a version that works.

What goes wrong

  • It shortens the room. A darker surface is read by the eye as nearer. Paint one wall dark and three pale, and the room appears shorter in that direction. People almost always paint the far wall, and the far wall is the one whose distance they were hoping to increase.
  • It divides rather than transforms. The room now has two characters and a hard line between them, and the line is the thing you see.
  • It is often the window wall, which is the worst choice available: you have put your darkest surface immediately around your only light source, so the contrast between the bright window and the dark wall is at its most severe and the room reads as gloomy.
  • The junctions get worse, not better. Now there are two colours meeting at two corners, plus white woodwork, plus a white ceiling. More edges, not fewer.

The version that works

Not an accent wall — an accent volume. Take the colour round an entire part of the room that is architecturally separate: an alcove and the shelves in it, a chimney breast including its returns, the inside of a bookcase, a bay, a small connected room seen through a doorway.

The difference is that you are colouring a thing rather than a surface. An alcove painted deep green is an alcove. The far wall painted deep green is a wall that has been painted a different colour from the other three.

The test before you paint one wall

Ask whether the surface you are about to colour is a thing or a side. Paint things. Leave sides alone.

And if what you actually want is for the room to feel different — the thing an accent wall promises — the answer is on page 62. One colour on all four walls and the ceiling changes a room far more than one wall in five ever will, and takes about the same amount of paint.

Sheen, which is half the decision

Two tins of the identical colour in different finishes are two different colours on the wall. Sheen is not a detail at the end of the process; it changes what you see.

Sheen is how much light a finish reflects directly back at you rather than scattering it. A flat finish scatters almost everything, so the colour looks deep, soft and even. A gloss finish bounces a good deal straight back, so the colour looks lighter, harder and more variable depending on where you stand.

The trade you are making, in one line

The sheen trade

More sheen is more washable and more durable. Less sheen is more beautiful and hides more. Choose by how much the surface gets touched.

A colour in satin or gloss will read lighter than the same colour in matt, because more light is coming straight back to your eye. If you test in matt and buy in satin you will not get the colour you chose. Test in the finish you intend to use.

And a practical warning about deep colours in matt: they are the most beautiful finish in this table and the least repairable. A scuff cannot simply be wiped, and a touch-up patch will often show unless you re-coat the whole wall. Lovely in a sitting room. Not in a hallway with a bicycle in it.

Work out how often a hand touches the surface. Never touched: matt. Touched weekly: eggshell. Touched daily or wet: satin. That single question answers nine of these ten rows without the table.

You do not need five tins. For most flats, two is genuinely enough: matt for walls and ceilings, eggshell for all woodwork. Add a satin only if you have a wet room or a hallway that takes real punishment. The five-level table exists so you know what you are choosing between, not so you buy all of it.

A useful story

The most copied French colour is a mistake

There is a grey that the design world has been copying for two hundred years, and restoration has now established that it was never the original colour at all.

It is called gris Trianon — Trianon grey. It appears as a named court colour in Versailles inventory documents of the 1770s and 1780s, and it is associated with the Temple of Love at the Petit Trianon, the small palace in the grounds of Versailles. It is a soft dove grey that reads almost as cement in poor light and as a pale barley-taupe in good light, and if you have ever seen a photograph of a beautiful French room with grey panelling, you have seen its descendants.

What restoration work at the Trianon has revealed is that the famous grey is a nineteenth-century repaint. Underneath it, the original finish was a pale green.

So the colour that the whole world understands as the authentic colour of eighteenth-century French elegance is a Victorian decorator's decision, applied over the real thing, and copied ever since by people who believed they were being faithful.

Why I put this in a book about your flat

There is no authentic colour to be faithful to. There is only what works in the light you have.

And it is still a very good grey

That is the other half of the point. Knowing it is a repaint does not make it worse. It is a warm grey with enough green in it to stay soft under electric light, which is exactly why it has survived two centuries of copying — it behaves well. The nineteenth-century decorator who chose it knew what they were doing.

If you want a grey that does not go cold at night, look for one with a trace of green rather than blue in it. That is the whole technical content of this famous colour, and it costs nothing to apply.

Old walls

Badigeon à la chaux — limewash

If your building is old and has solid walls, this page may save you a great deal of trouble, and it is the one my father would have wanted in the book.

Limewash is one of the oldest wall finishes there is — lime finishes go back to Greek and Roman building. It is made of three things: slaked lime, water, and natural pigment. That is the whole recipe. It dries to a chalky, matte, faintly variegated surface with a quality of depth that modern paint cannot reproduce, because you are looking slightly into it rather than at a film on top.

The property that matters more than the look

Limewash is breathable. It is porous, so moisture in a wall can pass through it and evaporate away.

Old buildings with solid walls and no damp-proof course are designed to work this way. They take up moisture and release it again, constantly, and they stay sound as long as nothing stops them. A modern plastic-based paint forms an impermeable film, which traps that moisture in the wall. The result is damp that appears after redecorating, blown plaster, and paint that lifts off in sheets — and the owner concludes the wall has a damp problem, when what it has is a paint problem.

Two real cautions

Wet lime is caustic. It has a very high pH and it will burn skin and eyes. Gloves and eye protection, every time, and this is not a place to be relaxed about it.

And limewash will not adhere to a wall that is already covered in modern emulsion. It needs a porous surface — bare plaster, lime plaster, brick or stone. Painting it over vinyl emulsion will simply produce dust.

Who this is actually for

If you live in a modern building with plasterboard walls, none of this applies to you and ordinary paint is correct. If you live in something old with thick solid walls, it is worth finding out what is already on them before you add another layer, because the wrong finish on an old wall is a slow and expensive mistake.

How many colours a home should have

Fewer than you think, and repeated more than you think. This is the page that makes a flat feel like one home rather than a series of decisions.

The working scheme

  1. One white. The same white on every ceiling and every piece of woodwork in the flat, unless a room is being drenched in its own colour. One tin, one decision, permanent consistency.
  2. One quiet colour that carries the circulation — hall, landing, stairs, and any room you have not made a specific decision about. This is the flat's ground note.
  3. Two or three room colours, each belonging to a room with a distinct character. The bedroom, the sitting room, perhaps a study or dining room.
  4. One deeper accent that appears in small quantities across several rooms — a door, an alcove, a bookcase interior. This is the thread that stitches the flat together.

Five or six colours for a whole home. Most flats I have worked in had eleven or twelve, all chosen separately, none of them wrong, and the whole never adding up.

Why repetition matters more than variety

A colour used once is a decoration. The same colour used three times in three rooms is a scheme.

The doorway test

Stand in any doorway where you can see into two rooms at once, which in most flats is the hall. Everything visible from that spot has to agree, because you will see it together several times a day.

This is where the undertone rule from page 50 does its real work: not within a room, but across a threshold. Two rooms that are lovely on their own and disagree at the doorway is the commonest whole-flat error there is, and it is invisible until you go and stand there.

And the practical benefit: one white and one circulation colour means you own two large tins that cover nine-tenths of the surfaces in the flat. Touching up becomes trivial, and it costs less.

The floor has already voted

The largest continuous surface in your home is the one you almost certainly cannot change. Every colour decision above it is a response to it, whether you meant it to be or not.

A floor is typically a quarter of the visible surface of a room, it runs under everything, and it is lit from above all day, which means it is bouncing its own colour onto the bottom of everything in the room. A warm honey oak floor is putting a warm cast on your walls continuously.

If you are fighting the floor

You will lose. A cool grey wall above an orange-toned floor is a fight that happens in every room of the flat, all day, and no amount of styling settles it. The honest options are to work with the floor's temperature, to cover a good deal of it with a rug in your chosen temperature, or to change the floor — which is expensive and is the only place in this book where I would say the expensive answer is often correct.

The floor runs through

One continuous floor through connected rooms makes a small flat read as one home. A change of flooring at every threshold chops it into cupboards.

This is mine and it is about layout as much as colour. If you are ever in a position to choose flooring for more than one room, choose the same one and run it through. It is the single most effective thing you can do to make a small flat feel generous.

Matching is a trap

The instinct, once you have chosen a wall colour, is to find a cushion in the same colour. Resist it. Matching is what makes a room look like it came in a box.

Two things prevent an exact match from ever working. The first is that the same colour in two different materials is not the same colour — paint on plaster and dye in wool reflect light completely differently, so a perfect match on paper reads as a near-miss in the room, and a near-miss is worse than a difference. The second is that a room where everything agrees exactly has nothing for the eye to do.

What to do instead

  • Stay in the family, change the depth. If the walls are a soft green, the cushions can be a much deeper green or a much paler one. Same undertone, different value. This always works.
  • Repeat the colour in a different material. The wall colour appearing again in a ceramic, a book spine, a painting — small, and in something with a different surface. The eye enjoys finding it.
  • Let one thing disagree on purpose. A room that is entirely harmonious is slightly dead. One object in a colour that has no relationship to the scheme is what stops it feeling assembled.

The one thing that genuinely should match

Whites. If you have white woodwork, white ceilings and white curtains, those should be the same white or obviously different whites, never nearly-the-same whites. Near-misses in white read as dirt. This is the exception to everything on this page.

From the notebook

My grandmother would not sell a client a fabric that matched their wall. She said a room should look as though it had been arrived at over years, not delivered on a Tuesday. She had been making curtains for thirty years by then and could afford to say so.

Colour when you cannot paint

Part Six is written entirely for rented rooms. This page is the colour half of it, because everything above assumes a paintbrush you may not be allowed to pick up.

First, check whether you actually cannot

A great many tenancies permit redecoration with written permission, and a landlord who has a white magnolia flat is often perfectly happy for you to improve it at your own cost if you agree to return it to neutral. It is worth one email. The worst answer is no, which is where you are already.

If the answer is no, the order changes

Without paint, the largest colour surfaces available to you are, in order of area:

  1. Curtains. Floor to near-ceiling, across a whole wall, in a colour you chose. This is by a wide margin the biggest colour decision available to a tenant, and Part Four covers how to hang them so they read as architecture rather than as fabric.
  2. A large rug. The second largest surface, and the one that can override an unpleasant floor's temperature. Bigger than feels sensible — page 97.
  3. The bed, in a bedroom. A bed dressed in one considered colour is most of the visual field in a small bedroom.
  4. Bookcases and their contents, which are a wall of colour you already own.
  5. Large art or a hanging textile. One big thing beats six small ones.

The white-wall strategy

If you are stuck with landlord white, stop treating it as a background to improve and start treating it as a gallery wall. White walls are genuinely excellent behind strong colour and strong texture — it is only as a whole scheme that they fail. Go deeper and warmer in everything you can control, and let the walls be the quiet part.

One thing worth knowing

Magnolia and builder's white are usually a cheap vinyl matt, which marks easily and cleans badly. If you are allowed to repaint before you leave, budget for it — a flat returned in fresh white is often the difference between getting a deposit back and arguing about it.

Mistakes to avoid

The six colour errors I saw most often

One · Choosing the colour before fixing the light

The error this whole book is ordered around. You choose for a light you are about to change, and then you have chosen wrong. Bulbs first, always — it is an afternoon and it decides everything.

Two · Testing on the wall instead of on paper

A patch painted on an existing wall is judged against that wall, and the old colour around it biases everything you see. It also means you cannot move it to the dark corner, which is the position that matters most.

Three · Two whites that are nearly the same

Ceiling in one white, woodwork in another, walls in a third, none of them chosen together. The eye cannot read the difference as a decision so it reads it as dirt. One white through the whole flat solves it permanently.

Four · The mid-tone compromise

Page 59. Choosing something halfway between pale and deep because it feels like the sensible option gets you the disadvantages of both. Commit in one direction.

Five · Ignoring what is outside the window

A large tree throws green light into a room from spring to autumn. A red brick wall a few metres away throws warm light in all year. Both will shift a wall colour more than the compass direction does, and neither shows up on any paint card.

Six · Buying the paint before the sheets have been up two days

Because the shop is open now and the weekend is free. This is how almost every repainted room happens. The two days cost nothing and they are the entire method.

What all six have in common

Not one of them is a mistake of taste. Every one is a mistake of sequence — deciding something before the thing it depends on was settled.

Troubleshooting

“I painted it and I hate it”

Before you repaint, work out which of these it is. Three of the five are not solved by a different colour, and repainting would waste a weekend.

It looks much darker than the sample

Expected, and usually not a colour problem. A whole room reads deeper than a sample because light bounces between the walls, each bounce deepening it. Give it a week before you judge — your eye adapts considerably. If it is still too much, the fix is often lighting, not paint: the room may simply have gone dark, which is page 58.

It looked fine by day and is horrible at night

A bulb problem, not a paint problem. You have almost certainly got mixed colour temperatures or bulbs above 3000 K. Change every bulb in the room to 2700 K, with a CRI of 90 or above, and look again the following evening. This fixes it far more often than repainting does.

It has gone a completely different colour from the sample

Check three things in order: whether the finish differs from what you tested (satin reads lighter than matt), whether it has had two full coats, and whether the wall underneath was a strong colour showing through. A deep colour over a dark wall can need three coats before it is itself.

It is fine but it fights the floor or the sofa

An undertone clash, page 50. Nothing is wrong with the colour and repainting in the same family will not help. Either change the smaller thing, or put a rug between them in a colour that belongs to both.

It is simply flat and lifeless

Usually not colour at all. Check the four textures on page 10 and the three lighting heights on page 28. A correct colour in a one-texture room lit from the ceiling will always look dead, and painting it a second colour will not change that.

Before you repaint anything

Live with it for two weeks. Colour adaptation is real and substantial — a room that feels overwhelming on day one often feels correct by day ten. I have talked more people out of repainting than into it, and almost none of them came back.

The order of painting a room

Not a decorating manual — there are good ones and this is not trying to be one. But the sequence saves a day and almost nobody is told it.

  1. Empty the room, or as near as you can get. Everything into the middle, covered. The time you spend here you get back twice in not working around things.
  2. Fill, sand, and wash the walls. Fill every hole including the ones you are about to make again. Sugar soap or a cloth and warm water on anything greasy, especially above a radiator and around light switches, because paint does not stick to grease.
  3. Ceiling first. Always. It spatters, and everything below it will be painted afterwards.
  4. Walls second.
  5. Woodwork last. Skirting, architrave, doors, windows. Woodwork paint is slower, more precise, and the thing you most want to avoid rushing — so it goes when nothing else is waiting on it.
  6. Two coats of everything. Including the thing you think looks fine after one. A single coat on a wall reads patchy in raking light for as long as you live there.

The two things worth spending money on

  • A decent brush for the woodwork and the cutting-in. A cheap brush sheds bristles into the wet paint and leaves a visibly worse edge, and you will be looking at that edge every day.
  • Enough paint. Running out two-thirds of the way through a wall and finishing from a second tin mixed at a different time is how you get a visible join. Buy the whole quantity at once, and if it is a mixed colour, check the tins are from the same batch.

Ventilation, and the one real hazard

Keep windows open and the room aired while you paint and for a day after. If the building predates the late 1970s and you intend to sand old paintwork rather than just paint over it, stop and read page 63 first — sanding is what makes old lead paint dangerous.

A worked example

One room, from start to finish

This is a constructed example, written to show the method applied end to end. It is not a real client.

The room

A sitting room of about sixteen square metres in a 1930s flat. One window facing north-east, used mostly in the evenings. Honey oak floorboards. A mid-grey sofa. Landlord white walls, a brilliant white ceiling, white woodwork, and one ceiling pendant with a 4000 K bulb in it. The owner described it as “fine but depressing” and wanted to paint it a pale grey.

The audit

  • Light: one source, one height, cool bulb. Error one, plus the wrong colour temperature.
  • Height: pictures centred at about 122 cm, curtain pole 4 cm above the frame, curtains 6 cm off the floor. Error two, comprehensively.
  • Texture: fabric sofa, fabric curtain, fabric cushions, painted surfaces. Two families. Error three.
  • Ceiling: brilliant white, never considered. Error four.
  • Orientation: north-east, used in the evening — so, per page 26, decorate it as a north room.

What was done, in order

  1. Every bulb changed to 2700 K, CRI 90. Two lamps added — one at 105 cm on a side table, one on the floor behind a chair. The pendant now stays off in the evening.
  2. Curtain pole moved to 12 cm below the ceiling and widened so the curtains stack on the wall. Curtains re-hung to the floor. Pictures re-hung to 145 cm.
  3. Only then, colour. The proposed pale grey was tested and abandoned: it went violet under the new warm bulbs and fought the honey floor. A soft warm white was chosen for the walls, the same white at quarter strength for the ceiling, and eggshell in the same white for the woodwork.
  4. A wool rug in a deep olive, an oak stool, and a large unglazed pot. Four textures.

The point of the example

The owner asked for a colour. Colour was the fourth thing done, it was not the colour requested, and the largest single change in the room was two lamps and a packet of bulbs.

What not to buy

Paint and colour

A colour fan deck

Two thousand chips makes the decision harder, not easier, and every one of them is printed ink viewed under shop light. You need three candidates and two sheets of paper. The fan is a merchandising object.

“One coat” paint

It is thicker, it costs more, and it still wants two coats to look even in raking light. You are paying a premium for a claim you will not act on.

Paint-and-primer-in-one, for a normal repaint

Genuinely useful on bare plaster, new plasterboard or a drastic colour change. Painting a similar colour over sound existing paint, which is most repaints, does not need it.

Peel-and-stick wallpaper as a cure for rented white

Sold directly at tenants and it is a recurring purchase dressed as a solution. It is expensive per square metre, it is difficult to hang without visible seams, it frequently does not survive removal without taking paint with it, and it solves a problem that curtains and a rug solve better and cheaper. Page 72.

A colour consultation that arrives without seeing your room

Anybody choosing your colours from photographs is guessing, because a photograph has an automatic white balance and has already destroyed the information about your light. If you pay for advice, pay for someone who stands in the room at the hour you use it.

Tester pots in more than three colours

Buying eight is a symptom of not having narrowed the decision. Do the orientation table on page 55, get to three, and test those properly.

What to buy instead

Three sample pots. Four large sheets of paper. One good brush. Enough paint for two coats, in one batch. That is the entire shopping list for a room, and it is less than most people spend on the things above.

The quiet part

Who profits from the colour of the year

Every autumn, several large paint companies announce a colour of the year. It is reported as news. It is worth looking at what it is for.

A colour of the year does two things at once. It gives magazines and shops something to write about and arrange displays around, which is ordinary commerce and nobody's business but theirs. And it establishes, by implication, that there is a colour which is current — which necessarily means the colour on your walls is last year's.

That is the whole mechanism. Paint does not wear out. A well-painted wall in a room nobody kicks will look perfectly good in fifteen years. So an industry selling a product that does not expire has to supply a reason to replace it, and taste is the only reason available.

Nobody decided this in a room somewhere. It is quieter and sadder than that.

There is no money in telling you that the soft warm white you chose three years ago, for the north-facing room you still live in, is still exactly the right colour for it. There is a great deal of money in a calendar that quietly makes every room in the country a year out of date each October. Not one person has to intend it for that to be how the whole thing works.

The test that survives any trend

A colour chosen for the direction your window faces cannot go out of fashion, because your window will not move.

What this is not

It is not an argument that the paint companies make bad paint. They make very good paint, the colours in their ranges are carefully developed, and the LRV figure on the card is one of the most useful pieces of information in this whole subject. Use the product. Ignore the calendar.

And if you like this year's colour — genuinely like it, in your own room, at dusk, under your own lamps — then paint your room with it. Liking something is a perfectly good reason. Being told it is current is not.

Questions I am asked

Eight short answers

Can I paint over wallpaper?

Sometimes, if it is sound, well stuck and not textured or vinyl. Test a small area first — paint wets the paste and can lift a seam. If any edge is loose, it will get worse. Stripping is more work and the right answer more often.

Do I need to prime?

Bare plaster, new plasterboard, bare wood or a drastic colour change: yes. Sound existing paint in a similar colour: no.

How much paint will I need?

Measure the walls and check the tin, because coverage varies by brand and colour. Then buy somewhat more than the tin claims. Deep colours and porous surfaces both drink more, and running out mid-wall is the one thing you cannot recover from neatly.

Is expensive paint worth it?

Often, for a deep colour, where the pigment load genuinely shows in depth and coverage and may save you a coat. Much less so for a white ceiling. Spend on the colour that people will look at and economise on the ones they will not.

Can I use exterior paint inside?

No. It is formulated for weather and flexibility rather than for scrubbing and low odour, and some exterior products are not intended for enclosed spaces. Buy the right one.

What about a radiator?

Use a radiator-grade paint; ordinary white paint yellows with heat. Paint it cold, and leave it a good while before turning the heating on.

Should the inside of my front door match the outside?

No, and it almost never should. The inside belongs to the hall's scheme, the outside belongs to the street. They are two different rooms.

Does a dark ceiling really make a room feel lower?

It makes it feel closer, which in a high room reads as intimate and in a low room reads as oppressive. Page 61. Above roughly two point seven metres it is an option worth having; below that, be careful.

The honest part

Where I might be wrong

I will not sell you a fantasy, and that is the whole reason you can trust the rest of this. Four places where what I have told you is opinion, or is limited, or where I know good decorators who disagree with me.

The small pale room

Page 57 is my most contested position and I have marked it as mine throughout. Plenty of excellent designers hold the opposite view and produce beautiful pale small rooms. What I will defend without hesitation is the narrower claim: brilliant white in a small dark room is a mistake. The argument about pale versus deep is a real argument.

The whole book assumes the northern hemisphere

Every orientation rule in Parts Two and Three assumes you are north of the equator. South of it, swap north and south and leave east and west as they are. Near the equator the whole framework weakens, because the sun passes much closer to overhead and the differences between orientations narrow considerably.

LRV bands are guidance, not a standard

The numbers on page 59 are working ranges I use, not an industry classification. A saturated mid-tone in a well-lit south room can be excellent and would fall squarely in the band I called a trap. Use them to compare candidates, not to rule one out.

Sometimes the expensive answer is the right one

I have spent this part explaining how to avoid wasting money on paint. But if your floor is fighting every colour in the flat, page 70, the honest answer is that changing the floor is the real fix and no amount of clever colour work substitutes for it. I would rather say so than sell you a workaround that leaves you dissatisfied for ten years.

The colour weekend

Weekend one — about two hours of actual work

  2. Find your orientation and write it down. Page 23.
  3. Decide warm or cool for the whole flat, by looking at the floor. Page 70.

The week in between

  1. Fill, sand, wash. Every hole, including the ones you will re-drill.

  • Commit in one direction. Pale or deep. The mid-tone buys the costs of both and the benefits of neither.
  • One white for the whole flat. Two nearly-identical whites read as dirt, not as a decision.
  • Choose the sheen by how often a hand touches the surface. Never: matt. Weekly: eggshell. Daily or wet: satin.
  • Paint things, not sides. An alcove, a chimney breast, a bookcase. Never one wall out of four.
