Part Two

Light

Light is the first correction and the largest one, and it is almost always the cheapest. This part covers what your windows are giving you, how to read a bulb box, the three heights a room has to be lit from, and what to do with a room that gets no sun at all.

Chapter two

Why light comes first

You cannot see anything in a room except light. Everything else you think you are looking at is light coming off a surface.

That sounds like a philosophical remark and it is in fact a completely practical one. The colour of your wall is light. The texture of your sofa is light — specifically, it is the pattern of tiny highlights and shadows across a weave, which is why velvet and linen look different even in the same colour. The apparent size of the room is light, because your eye judges depth largely from how brightness falls off across a surface.

Change the light and you have changed all three. Nothing else in decorating has that reach.

Which is why it is first, and why the money goes furthest here. Three good bulbs and a lamp you already own will do more for a room than a new rug at ten times the price. I have seen it happen in an afternoon more times than I can count, and it still surprises the person who lives there.

The two halves of the problem

A room has daylight, which you did not choose and cannot change, and it has electric light, which you chose without thinking about it and can change tonight. They have to be handled differently.

  • Daylight is a fixed fact of the building. What you can do is find out exactly what you have been given, stop wasting it, and choose colours that work with it rather than against it. That is the first six pages of this part.
  • Electric light is entirely yours. Number of sources, their heights, the colour of the bulbs, whether they dim, what the shades do. Almost every error in the fourth error list lives here, and almost every one is correctable for under the price of a takeaway meal.

Why I know this

The building I grew up in was put up for silk looms, and the looms needed north light — light with no direct sun in it, that does not move across the day and does not glare, so a weaver could match a thread at nine in the morning and at four in the afternoon and get the same answer. Two hundred years later I am telling you the same thing about your sitting room. The weavers were right.

So we start with the window, because the window sets the terms for everything else.

Which way does your window face?

If you take one thing from this book, take this. Almost nobody knows the answer for their own home, and every colour decision depends on it.

How to find out, three ways

  1. The compass on your phone. Stand at the window looking out. The bearing it shows is the direction the window faces. Thirty seconds, and it is the only thing your phone is better at than a tape measure.
  2. Midday. In the northern hemisphere the sun is due south in the middle of the day. Stand at the window at noon. If the sun is in front of you, the window faces roughly south. Behind you, roughly north.
  3. Watch it for a day. Direct sun in the early morning means east. Direct sun in the evening means west. Direct sun in the middle of the day means south. No direct sun at all, ever, at any hour, in any season — north.

Everything in this part and in Part Three assumes the northern hemisphere. If you are south of the equator, swap north and south throughout and leave east and west as they are.

Write the answer down for every room in the house. Put it on the audit sheet. You will use it on the paint page, on the bulb page, on the curtain page, and every time you are standing in a shop wondering whether a colour will work.

Colette’s first law

Light before colour. You cannot choose a colour until you know which way the window faces.

North-facing

Cool, steady, and unfairly maligned

A north-facing room receives no direct sunlight at any point in the day or the year. Estate agents treat this as a defect. Painters and weavers have always treated it as the best light there is.

Both are right about different things. What north light is, precisely, is light from the whole dome of the sky rather than from the sun. That gives it two properties, and understanding them is the whole of managing a north room.

It does not move. There is no patch of sun crossing the floor, no hour when the room transforms, no glare. The light at nine is the light at four. This is why every proper painter's studio and every weaver's atelier in Europe faces north, and why my grandmother would only match a thread at a north window.

It is cool. Skylight is blue-biased, so everything in a north room is being lit by very slightly blue light, all day, forever.

What this does to your colours

It pushes them cold. A warm grey turns blue. A greige goes grey. A white with any blue in it goes clinical — and a great many fashionable whites have blue in them, which is exactly why they look so unpleasant in north rooms and so crisp in the showroom photograph that was taken in a south room.

What to do about it

  • Choose colours with warmth in them, and more warmth than you think. A colour that looks slightly too yellow or too pink on the chip will look correct on the wall. Part Three gives you the specific whites.
  • Do not fight it with brightness. Adding more cool light to a cool room makes it colder. Add warmth instead: 2700 K bulbs, and lamps rather than the ceiling.
  • Lean into it. A north room will never be a sunny room, and a north room pretending to be sunny is the saddest thing in decorating. Deeper, warmer, quieter colours make it feel intentional. This is the room to be brave in.
  • Use the steadiness. It is the one room in the house where a colour will look the same in the morning and the evening. If you have art you care about, or you sew, or you paint, this is where it goes.

The test

Hold a sheet of plain white paper up in your north room at midday, then carry it to a south-facing window. The same sheet of paper. The difference startles people — and it is applied to every surface in both rooms, permanently.

South-facing

Warm, strong, and harder to handle than people expect

A south-facing room gets direct sun for most of the day. Everyone wants one. Nobody warns you that it is the most difficult light to decorate for.

The difficulty is that it changes. Direct sunlight is enormously stronger than skylight, and it arrives, crosses the room, and leaves. So a south room is really three rooms: a bright one in the morning, a blazing one at midday, and a normal one after the sun has moved past. A colour that is handsome at ten can be bleached to nothing at two.

It is also warm. Direct sun is yellow-biased, which means a south room flatters warm colours and can turn a cool colour muddy. And in summer it is a heat problem as much as a light problem.

What to do about it

  • You can afford cool colours here, and you generally should. This is the room where a grey-green, a slate blue, or a proper cool white works — the sun supplies the warmth so the paint does not have to. In a south room, warm colours on top of warm light can tip into orange.
  • Test at the worst hour, not the best. Judge a colour in a south room at full midday sun. If it survives that, it will be fine the rest of the day. Judge it at six in the evening and you will choose wrong.
  • Give yourself a way to soften the sun. A sheer panel behind the main curtain, or a blind you can drop at midday, turns direct sun into diffused light without turning the room dark. This is what shutters do in Mediterranean buildings, and it is the single most useful thing you can add to a south room.
  • Expect fading, and plan for it. Strong sun over years will lighten textiles, paper and some woods. Rotate cushions. Do not put a watercolour you love on the wall the sun crosses. This is not pessimism, it is just what ultraviolet does.

The one that catches people

Gloss and satin finishes in a south room. Direct sun across a shiny wall shows every roller mark, every filled hole, every unevenness in the plaster, in a way that no other light does. In a strong south room, keep walls matte and save the sheen for woodwork.

East and west-facing

The rooms with two personalities

An east or west room has direct sun for part of the day and skylight for the rest. So it has two characters, and the decision you have to make is which one you are decorating for.

That decision is not a matter of taste. It is a matter of when you are in the room.

East

Direct sun from sunrise until late morning, then cool skylight for the rest of the day. The sun is low and the light is clear and slightly golden, and then at some point mid-morning it simply goes, and from then on the room behaves like a north room.

Decorate it for the afternoon, because the afternoon is most of the room's life. Which means treating it much like a north room: warmth in the colours, 2700 K bulbs, lamps for the evening. The morning sun will be a gift on top of that rather than something you have to accommodate.

Best use: kitchens and breakfast rooms, and bedrooms for people who like to wake with the light.

West

Cool skylight all morning, then direct sun from mid-afternoon until sunset — and evening sun is the warmest, most orange light of the day, arriving at a very low angle, straight into the room and straight into your eyes.

Decorate it for the evening if that is when you use it, which for a sitting room it usually is. West rooms can carry cooler colours than you would expect, because that evening light is so warm it will pull everything back. But you will need something to cut the glare — a sheer, a blind, a shutter.

Best use: sitting rooms and dining rooms, where the golden hour lands exactly when you want it.

If your room has windows on two sides

Then you are fortunate and you have a slightly more interesting problem. Cross-lit rooms are the easiest rooms in the world to make look good, because everything in them has a lit side and a shaded side without you doing anything at all — the two errors of flatness and of single direction are solved by the architecture.

Work out which window is dominant, by sitting in the room at the hour you use it and seeing which one is actually doing the lighting. Decorate for that one. Treat the second window as modelling light and do not block it, ever, with a piece of furniture or a plant.

Electric light

The three heights

Here is the rule stated plainly, and then the reason, and then the numbers.

Colette’s rule of the three heights

Light must arrive at three heights — overhead, table, and floor. A room lit from one height is a room lit like a corridor.

This is mine and I will not dress it up as physics. But the reason underneath it is physics, and the reason is modelling. An object looks solid when one side of it is lit and another is not. That is the only cue your eye has for three-dimensionality in a still scene, and it requires light to arrive from more than one direction.

Put light at three different heights and every object in the room is caught from three angles. The jug has a bright side, a mid side and a shadow. The sofa arm has a top, a face and an underside. The room acquires depth, which is the quality people are describing when they say a room feels “warm” or “inviting” and cannot say why.

The three, and what each one is for

Three sources, three heights, three switches. If you do nothing else in this book, do this.

Height one

Overhead, and what it is good for

I have spent a page telling you that the ceiling light is the problem. Now let me be fair to it, because there are three jobs it does that nothing else can.

  • Finding things. You want a lot of even light when you are looking for a dropped earring or cleaning the room. That is a task, it lasts four minutes, and it is exactly what a ceiling fitting is for.
  • Safety. Stairs, thresholds, anywhere someone might trip. Overhead light shows the edge of a step better than a lamp does.
  • Work. A kitchen counter and a bathroom mirror need light from above and in front. In a kitchen this is not a compromise, it is correct.

What the ceiling light is not for is being the light you sit in. That is the whole error. It is a tool, not a setting, and the mistake is leaving the tool switched on all evening.

If you are stuck with a single central fitting

Most rented flats have exactly one, in the middle of the ceiling, and you cannot move it without an electrician. Three things you can do without touching the wiring:

  1. Change the shade so it throws light sideways and up, not straight down. An open-topped drum or a fabric shade will bounce a good deal of its output off the ceiling, and light that has bounced off a surface is soft, directionless and much kinder than light straight from a bulb. This turns your worst fitting into something closer to indirect light, for the price of a shade.
  2. Reduce it and warm it. Put in a lower-output 2700 K bulb than you think you need. You are no longer asking it to light the room; the lamps are doing that.
  3. Put it on a dimmer if the fitting and bulb allow. See page 37 — and check that the bulb says dimmable, because most do not.

Never

A bare bulb in a ceiling fitting, in a room anybody sits in. It is the single harshest light source available in a home: a small, very bright point directly overhead, producing hard shadows in every eye socket in the room. If a shade is missing, a paper lantern costs almost nothing and is a real improvement.

One honest exception: a beautiful pendant, hung low over a dining table, is one of the great pleasures of a room. But look at what it is doing — hung that low it is no longer overhead light, it is table-height light with a cable. The height is the point.

Height two

Table height — the one that does the work

If you may only have one lamp, it goes here. This is the height that makes a room feel like somewhere people live.

The reason is that it is at the height of a seated person's eye. Light arriving from roughly your own eye level does two things at once: it lights faces from the front rather than the top, which is enormously more flattering, and it puts a bright object in your field of view without glaring, which reads as warmth.

The number that matters

Bottom of the shade, above the floor, for a lamp beside a chair or sofa 100–115 cm · 40–45 in
Which for a table at typical side-table height means a lamp of about 45–60 cm · 18–24 in
Bottom of the shade, for a lamp on a desk or console you stand at 125–135 cm · 49–53 in

The rule behind all three: the bottom of the shade should sit at or just below the eye level of whoever is using the lamp. Sit down in the chair and hold your hand at your eye. That is where the bottom edge of the shade wants to be. Higher and you are looking at the bulb. Much lower and the light pools on the table and never reaches your book.

This is why so many lamps are the wrong size — not the wrong style, the wrong size. A lamp is chosen in a shop, at standing height, on a display plinth. Then it goes onto a low side table beside a low sofa, and the shade ends up at chest height, and the room is lit from the chest up only.

How to check a lamp before you buy it

Take two numbers with you: the height of the table it will stand on, and your own seated eye height (sit in a dining chair and have somebody measure from the floor to your eye — for most adults it lands between 110 and 125 cm). Table height plus lamp height to the bottom of the shade should land in that range. This is the only measurement anybody needs to buy a lamp, and no shop displays it.

Where they go

Not side by side. Two lamps at table height on the same wall light one half of a room twice. Put them diagonally opposite, or at least on two different walls, so their light crosses. Two lamps on two walls is a lit room; two lamps on one wall is a lit wall.

Height three

Floor level — the one nobody does

This is the height that almost no ordinary home has, and it is the one that makes a room look like a photograph.

Think about where the light is in your room. It is all in the top half. Ceiling fitting at two and a half metres, perhaps a lamp at a metre. Everything below knee height is lit only by whatever falls down there, which is very little, because light falls off quickly with distance — double the distance from a source and you have a quarter of the light.

So the bottom third of the room sits in gloom, and because the bottom third of the room is where the floor is, the room reads as sitting in a dark pool. Adding a single low source transforms this, and it costs almost nothing because the fitting can be very modest.

Five ways to do it, cheapest first

  1. A small lamp on the floor behind a chair or a plant. Aimed up a wall or into the leaves. You cannot see the source, only the glow. This is the cheapest genuine transformation in the whole of this book — any small lamp you already own, put on the floor.
  2. A lamp on the lowest surface in the room. A hearth, a stack of books, a low stool, the bottom shelf of a bookcase.
  3. An uplighter in a corner. A canister that sits on the floor and throws light up the two walls of a corner. The corner glows, the ceiling picks it up, and the room gains a soft general light with no visible source.
  4. Candles. Not a joke and not a decoration. A candle is a light source at table or floor height with the warmest colour temperature in the house, and four of them grouped is a real light. Never left unattended, never near a curtain.
  5. Lit shelving. A strip tucked under a shelf, invisible, washing down the books below. The most expensive option here and the most permanent.

Why it works

Light from below reverses every shadow in the room. Nothing else you can do in twenty minutes changes a room this much.

One caution, since the effect is strong: low light aimed at a face is theatrical and unflattering — it is how a horror film is lit. Aim it at a wall, a corner, a plant or a bookshelf, never across the seating.

How many lamps, and where they go

More than you have. Almost certainly more than you think is reasonable.

The count

Minimum separate light sources in any room anybody sits in 3
A sitting room, comfortably 4–6
Of which on the ceiling 1, off in the evening
Of which below 60 cm at least 1

Five lamps in a sitting room sounds like a great many until you realise that each is small, dim and doing one job. Five modest sources use less electricity than one bright pendant trying to light the whole room, and the room will feel brighter. Brightness in a room is not the total output; it is how many surfaces have light on them.

Where they go — the four rules

  1. Spread them around the perimeter, not the middle. Light on the walls makes a room feel larger, because the walls are what define its size and a lit wall reads as further away. Light in the middle does the opposite.
  2. Get one into every corner you can. Dark corners make a room feel smaller and slightly unresolved. Not every corner — one should stay dark, see page 102 — but the others want something.
  3. Put one where you sit and one where you look. A lamp beside your chair for reading, and a lamp at the far end of the room so there is something lit to look towards. A room where all the light is behind you feels like a cinema.
  4. Never point one at the television. Or at a mirror, or at any glass, from opposite. You will spend the evening looking at the reflection of a bulb.

The switch problem, which nobody mentions

Five lamps means five switches in five awkward places, and a system you have to walk around the room to operate will be abandoned within a fortnight. Solve it before you build it: put lamps on a single switched extension where you can, or use plug adaptors you can control together. A lighting scheme that takes one action to turn on is a lighting scheme you will actually use, and that matters more than any individual fitting.

Two rules that explain most of the table

  • You should not be able to see the bulb from where you sit. Every height above is really this rule expressed in centimetres for a particular piece of furniture. If you can see the bulb, the lamp is too high or you are too low.
  • Light a face from the side, not from above. This is why a bathroom mirror wants lights beside it rather than over it — a fitting above a mirror puts your own eye sockets in shadow, which is why you look worse in a hotel bathroom than you do at home.

The pendant over the table, because it gets argued about

Seventy-five to ninety centimetres above the tabletop looks far too low when the room is empty. It is correct when people are sitting down — it lights the food and the faces and not the ceiling, and you can see across the table underneath it. Hang it, then sit down before you judge it. Everyone who raises it wishes they had not.

The number on the box

Colour temperature

There is a number on every bulb box that decides more about how your home feels than the price of your furniture, and most people have never read it.

It is printed in kelvin, with a capital K, and it describes the colour of the light rather than how much of it there is. The scale is confusing because it runs backwards from how you would expect: a low number is warm and orange, a high number is cool and blue.

The whole recommendation

Colette’s rule of the warm bulb

Twenty-seven hundred kelvin, or nothing. Everything above three thousand is shop lighting, and a shop wants you to look at objects, not live among them.

Two thousand seven hundred kelvin is the colour of the old incandescent bulbs everybody grew up with, and it is the colour every human being alive associates with evening and with being at home. It is warm without being orange. It flatters skin. It makes wood look like wood.

Three thousand is acceptable and very slightly cooler; some people prefer it in a kitchen or bathroom. Four thousand belongs in a garage or a shop. Five thousand and up is sold as “daylight” and has no business in a room where anyone relaxes, because it tells the human body it is the middle of the day.

The other number worth finding

CRI, the colour rendering index, on a scale to one hundred. It measures how faithfully a light source shows colours compared with a reference light. A cheap bulb may sit around eighty, and under it your red cushion is a slightly brown cushion and your skin looks grey. Ninety or above is what you want, and it is often printed very small or as “CRI 90+”. It costs a little more and it is the difference between your colours working and not working after dark.

The quiet part

Why every shop you buy from is lit wrong

Walk into any large homeware shop and look up. The light is cool, it is bright, and there is a great deal of it, coming from directly overhead across the whole ceiling.

Every single thing about that is the opposite of what this chapter has told you to do. And it is not a mistake. It is correct — for a shop.

Cool bright overhead light makes goods read clearly. It shows edges and colours at a glance, it reaches into every aisle without shadows anyone could trip in, it keeps people alert and moving, and it is cheap to run and easy to maintain at scale. A shop is a place where objects are examined. It is lit for examining objects.

Your sitting room is not a place where objects are examined. But here is the consequence, and it is worth sitting with for a moment:

Every purchase you have ever made for your home, you made under light that your home will never have.

You chose the grey of that sofa at four thousand kelvin from above. You will live with it at two thousand seven hundred from the side. Those are genuinely different colours. This is the real reason the cushion looked one way in the shop and another way at home, and why people assume they have been somehow deceived. Nobody deceived you. Two different lights.

And the economics underneath it

Nobody decided this in a room somewhere. It is quieter and sadder than that.

There is no money in telling you the answer is a two-dollar bulb. There is a great deal of money in a shop floor arranged so that the object looks its absolute best under lighting conditions that you will never reproduce, and in the next purchase you make when the first one disappoints you at home. Nobody has to intend that for it to be how the whole thing works.

So the fix is not a boycott, it is a habit:

  • Never make a final colour decision in a shop. Bring the sample home.
  • Judge fabric and paint in the room they are going into, at the hour you use it, with your own lamps on.
  • Change your bulbs before you change anything else, so that the light you are judging in is the light you will keep.

What to actually buy

This page is the cheapest correction in the book. An afternoon, a handful of bulbs, and the light in your home stops being wrong.

The specification, every time

  • 2700 K. Sometimes sold as “warm white”, but the words on the front vary between brands and the number does not. Find the number.
  • CRI 90 or above if it is stated. If nothing is stated, assume it is not.
  • Dimmable, if there is any chance the fitting is ever on a dimmer. It must say so explicitly.
  • The output you want, in lumens — not watts. Watts describe how much electricity it uses, which used to be a rough proxy for brightness and is not any more.

And the shape

Under a fabric shade, buy the cheapest thing that meets the specification above — nobody sees it. In an open fitting where the bulb shows, buy frosted or opal rather than clear: a visible filament is charming in a photograph and glaring in a room, because you are looking straight at a naked point source.

Do it in one go

Walk round the house with a bag and count every socket, including the ones in fittings you never use. Buy them all at once, in the same specification, and change them in one afternoon. Mixed colour temperatures in one room is worse than either temperature alone — it is the only genuinely unfixable lighting error, because the eye cannot settle on a white point. If you do nothing else: make every bulb in a single room match.

Dimming, which matters more than brightness

A room needs different amounts of light at four in the afternoon and eleven at night. A room with one setting is wrong twice a day.

This is the part of lighting that people skip because it sounds like a luxury, and it is closer to a necessity. Your eye adapts: at eleven at night, with your pupils wide, the light you wanted at four in the afternoon is uncomfortably bright. And the amount of light in a room is one of the strongest signals your body uses to decide whether it is time to wind down.

Four ways, in order of cost

  1. Separate switches. The cheapest dimmer in the world is having five lamps on five plugs and turning two of them off. No wiring, no cost, and it is genuinely most of the benefit. Two lamps at eleven at night is a different room from five.
  2. An in-line dimmer on a lamp flex. A small wheel that sits on the cable. Inexpensive, plugs in, nothing to install. The bulb must say dimmable.
  3. A plug-in dimmer or a smart plug. Sits between the plug and the socket. Works with a remote or a phone. More expensive, no tools, and it lets you dim several lamps together.
  4. A wall dimmer. Replaces the light switch. This is electrical work behind a faceplate and it needs an electrician — and if you rent, it needs permission. Do not do this one yourself.

The dimmable LED problem, which catches nearly everyone

Most LED bulbs are not dimmable, and putting a non-dimmable LED on a dimmer gives you flicker, buzzing, a bulb that cuts out before the bottom of the range, or a shortened life. It has to say dimmable on the box. And even dimmable LEDs vary in how well they behave with any given dimmer, so if you are fitting several, buy one and try it before buying ten.

One thing better than a dimmer

A dimmed bulb gets dimmer but it does not get much warmer. Real evening light — a fire, a candle, a setting sun — gets warmer as it gets lower, and that is the quality a dimmer cannot reproduce on its own.

Which is why a candle or two, at the point in the evening when you have dimmed everything else, does something a dimmer cannot. It is not decoration. It is the only source in the house at around two thousand kelvin, and it is the reason a room with candles in it feels late in a way that a dimmed room does not.

What a shade actually does

A shade is not a decoration on a lamp. It is the part that decides where the light goes, and choosing it by appearance alone is how people end up with a lamp that does not light anything.

The three behaviours

ShadeWhat it does with the lightUse it for
Opaque — card, metal, lined fabric Sends light up and down in two cones. The shade itself stays dark. Reading. Lighting a specific surface. Anywhere you want a pool rather than a glow.
Translucent — unlined linen, paper, parchment Glows in every direction and lights the room generally. The shade becomes a soft object. Atmosphere. The workhorse of a sitting room. Almost always the right answer at table height.
Directional — a hood, a cone, a spot Sends light one way only. A desk. A picture. Washing a wall. Never as the only light in a room.

Colour inside the shade

Look inside the shades you own. The inner surface is the last thing the light touches before it reaches you, and it tints everything.

  • White or off-white inside: reflects efficiently, keeps the light neutral. The safe default.
  • Gold, brass or warm cream inside: warms the light and increases the glow. Genuinely lovely in a north room and one of the oldest tricks there is.
  • Black or dark inside: absorbs most of the light. Very dramatic, very inefficient. One in a room, never two.

The proportion rule for a table lamp

Shade height, as a share of the whole lamp about one third
Shade width at the bottom, compared with the base width roughly twice
The shade must hide the bulb and the fitting, from seated

That last line is the one that matters, and the first two are only rules of thumb that usually produce it. Sit down. If you can see the bulb or the metal collar it screws into, the shade is too small or too shallow, whatever the arithmetic says.

The room that gets no sun

A basement flat. A ground-floor room with a wall two metres away. A north room on a dark street. I have worked in a great many of these and I am not going to pretend they are the same as a room with a view.

Here is the honest position, and then what genuinely helps.

The honest position: a room with no useful daylight cannot be made to feel like a room with daylight. Every technique for “brightening” such a room is really a technique for making it feel intentional instead of deficient. That is achievable, and it is a different goal from the one the magazines imply.

What does not work, which is most of the usual advice

  • Brilliant white. The most common advice and the worst. White needs light to be white; without it, white goes grey and dead. You have painted the room the colour of the problem.
  • More lumens. A dark room flooded with cool light becomes a lit dark room — gloomy and glaring at once.
  • A mirror on a blank wall. It reflects the blank wall. Page 20.

What works

  1. Stop trying to be bright and commit to being warm. This is the whole strategy. A deep, warm, enveloping colour in a sunless room reads as a decision — a study, a snug, a dining room for the evening. The same room in pale grey reads as a mistake. Deep green, warm brown-red, ink blue, a proper dark plaster colour. Be braver than feels sensible.
  2. Four or five small warm sources, none overhead, with one dark corner left alone. You are building an evening room, so light it like an evening.
  3. Every texture you can find. Texture is what gives a dark room something to do — light catching a coarse weave, a worn timber, a matte glaze. This is the room for the roughest, most tactile things you own.
  4. Something living, realistically. Choose the tolerant plants and accept slow growth, or use branches in water.
  5. Take back whatever daylight exists. Page 41. Here every centimetre of glass matters, and it is usually half blocked.

From the notebook

My grandmother made curtains for a dressmaker whose courtyard workroom never saw the sun. She lined its walls in a dark red and put six lamps in it. Everyone who came in said it was the loveliest room in the building. It was the darkest.

Corridors, landings and internal rooms

Nobody decorates a corridor and everybody walks through one several times an hour. It is the most neglected surface area in a home.

A corridor has a particular problem: it is long, narrow, usually windowless, and lit by one or two ceiling fittings that produce bright patches with gloom between them. So you walk down a tunnel of alternating light and dark, and the corridor feels longer and meaner than it is.

The correction

  • Light the walls, not the floor. Two or three wall lights along one side, centred around 150 to 165 cm, will do more than any number of ceiling fittings. Lit walls make a narrow space feel wider; a lit floor makes it feel like a route.
  • If you cannot add wiring, use the surfaces. A small lamp on a narrow console, or a shelf at the far end with a lamp on it, gives you a lit destination. A corridor with something lit at the end of it stops being a tunnel.
  • Hang things at the right height, all the way along. A corridor is the easiest place in the house to hang pictures, because you walk past rather than sitting, so the standard 145 cm centre is exactly right. A run of small pictures at a consistent height turns dead space into the best wall in the flat.
  • Do not paint it the same white as everything else. A corridor is a transition and can take a stronger colour than a room you sit in, because you are only in it for four seconds. It is the cheapest place to be brave, because it is small and nobody lives in it.

Internal rooms and windowless bathrooms

Treat these as the sunless room on page 39, with two additions. First, a windowless bathroom has a humidity problem as well as a light problem, and the extract fan matters more than anything decorative — if it does not clear the mirror within a few minutes of a shower, that is a ventilation job and worth sorting before you paint.

Second, these are the rooms where mirror placement is genuinely powerful, because a mirror opposite a lit wall or a lamp will double what little light there is and give the eye some depth. Put the light source and the mirror opposite each other, and stand where you will stand to check what you will be looking at.

Bathrooms and electricity

Lighting and sockets in a bathroom are governed by specific rules about how close a fitting may be to water, and those rules exist for an extremely good reason. Anything beyond changing a bulb in an existing fitting is an electrician's job. There is no version of this where doing it yourself is sensible.

The daylight you are throwing away

Before you buy a single lamp, go and get back the light you already have. It is free, it takes an hour, and almost every home I have been in was wasting between a fifth and a third of it.

Go and look at your window. All of it.

  1. What is standing in front of it? A tall plant, a lamp, a drying rack, a chair with coats on it, a stack of boxes that has been there since you moved in. Anything in the lower third of a window is taking light from the whole room.
  2. Where do the curtains sit when they are open? This is the big one. In most homes the open curtains cover fifteen to twenty centimetres of glass down each side, because the pole is barely wider than the window. That is a tenth of your window, permanently, in every room, for years. The fix is a wider pole — it should extend 15 to 25 cm beyond the frame on each side so the curtains stack on the wall rather than on the glass.
  3. Is the blind fully up? A roller blind left a third down is invisible to you after a week. Go and look. Roll it right up.
  4. Are the nets or sheers doing anything? Permanent net curtains on a window that nobody can see into are costing you light for no reason. If privacy is only needed in the evening, a blind you drop is better than a sheer you never move.
  5. Is the glass clean, inside and out? Unglamorous and genuinely significant on a window that has not been washed in a year or two, especially on a street.
  6. What colour is the reveal? The little returns of wall at the sides of the window. If they are a dark colour, or grubby, they are absorbing light at the exact point it enters the room. Painting the reveal the palest thing in the room is a trick worth knowing.

Worth saying on its own

Most people's curtains, when open, cover a tenth of their window. Nobody notices, because the curtains have always been there.

Do all six of those before you spend anything. Then reassess the room. A surprising number of “dark” rooms are ordinary rooms with a blocked window, and you will have spent nothing.

What not to buy

Lighting

Six things this industry sells constantly that will not solve the problem you bought them for.

Colour-changing smart bulbs, for a sitting room

The colour-changing is not the problem; the problem is that they are usually bought instead of a second lamp. A single ceiling fitting that can turn pink is still a single ceiling fitting. If you want smart bulbs, put them in three lamps at three heights and you have something genuinely good. One is a novelty.

“Daylight” bulbs for a dark room

Sold specifically at the problem and it makes it worse. Five or six thousand kelvin in a room with no daylight gives you a cold blue room. The word on the box is doing the selling.

A brighter ceiling fitting

The instinct when a room is gloomy, and it is treating a direction problem as a quantity problem. You will get a bright ceiling and the same gloomy room.

Spotlight tracks and downlight grids, retrofitted

Often sold as a modern fix for a dull room. What they produce is a ceiling of small bright sources all pointing straight down — error one, multiplied. They have real uses over a worktop or aimed at art. As general lighting for a room where people sit, they are the most expensive way to get a corridor.

Very cheap multipacks of bulbs

Where the colour temperature and the CRI are not printed, both are usually bad. Your colours will not work after dark and you will not know why. A bulb is the highest-leverage cheap thing in a house; buy the ones with the numbers on.

A dimmer switch, before you have checked your bulbs

Fitting a dimmer to non-dimmable LEDs produces flicker and buzz, and people conclude the dimmer is faulty. Check the bulbs first. And a wall dimmer is electrical work behind a faceplate, which is an electrician's job.

What to buy instead, in order

2700 K bulbs for the whole house. Then one lamp for the floor, if you do not already own something that will do. Then an in-line dimmer for the lamp you sit next to. That is a complete lighting correction for a room, and in most homes it comes to less than the cost of a single ceiling fitting.

Part Two, in nine lines

One

Light before colour. You cannot choose a colour until you know which way the window faces.

Two

Light must arrive at three heights — overhead, table, floor. A room lit from one height is a room lit like a corridor.

Three

Twenty-seven hundred kelvin, or nothing.

And the six that do not need their own page

  • You should never see a bulb from where you sit. Every height in this part is that one rule, measured for a particular piece of furniture.
  • Every bulb in one room must match. Mixed colour temperatures is the only lighting error the eye cannot get used to.
  • Brightness is not output, it is how many surfaces have light on them. Five small lamps beat one big pendant, and use less electricity.
  • Light the walls, not the middle. Lit walls make a room read larger.
  • Get the free light back first. Widen the pole, unblock the sill, raise the blind, wash the glass. Before you spend anything.
  • Never make a final colour decision in a shop. You are standing under four thousand kelvin from above, and you will never live under it.

If you do exactly one thing

Buy 2700 K bulbs for the whole house and change them all in one afternoon. It is the cheapest, fastest, largest single correction available to you, and everything in Part Three depends on it, because you are about to choose colours and you need to know what light they will be living in.

Turn the ceiling light off tonight and put on two lamps instead. That is Part Two. Everything else is detail.
