What Not to Buy

The three shorter lists from earlier in the book, collected, with two more that had nowhere else to go. Fourteen years of watching money leave people’s hands for things that did not work. It is the least charming part of this book and possibly the most useful.

Why this list exists

I was, for fourteen years, a person who sold things to people for their homes. I want to be honest about what that does to advice.

A decorator is paid either by the hour or by the thing. If by the thing, then every problem in your home has a solution that can be delivered in a van, and that is not corruption — it is just what the work rewards. I noticed it in myself. A client would describe a room that felt wrong and I would find myself reaching for a catalogue before I had sat down in it.

So this list is my correction to my own trade. It is every category of purchase where I saw money spent that did not improve a room, or that improved it for six months and then became a thing to get rid of.

The four tests behind every entry

  1. Does it solve the fault, or does it decorate over it? A darker cushion does not fix a room lit from one place.
  2. Will it still be here in ten years, and will I still want it?
  3. Can it be repaired, recovered, or refinished by an ordinary person?
  4. Does it move house? If you rent, this is the first test, not the last.

A fair warning about this section

These are my opinions, formed by what I saw in one city over fourteen years, and a person with a different climate, a different housing stock or a different budget may reasonably disagree with half of it. Where something is a matter of fact I say so; where it is a matter of taste I have tried to say that too. Page 201 is a whole page of things I might be wrong about.

How to read the entries

Where an entry ends with a page number, the argument for it is made properly somewhere earlier in the book and you should go and read it before you accept my verdict. Where it does not, it is a judgement I formed on the job and have not made a case for at length — treat those with more suspicion, not less.

One

Lighting

  • Cool white bulbs, anywhere in a home. Above 3000 K a room turns grey-blue and every warm colour in it dies. This is the single most damaging cheap purchase in decorating.
  • Any bulb that does not print its colour rendering. If the box will not tell you the CRI, it is because the number is not good. Page 34.
  • A brighter ceiling light, bought to fix a dark room. The room is not short of light. It is short of light in the right places. Page 32.
  • Downlights, added to a room you live in. They light floors. You do not live on the floor, and they will put every face in the room into shadow.
  • Bare-filament bulbs at eye level over a table. Fashionable, and genuinely unpleasant to sit under for two hours.
  • A lamp chosen for the base alone. The shade does the work — it is what decides where the light goes and whether you can see the bulb. Page 38.
  • Colour-changing smart bulbs, for the colour. The dimming is worth having. The sixteen million colours will be used twice, and the ones you will actually leave on are the two that a plain warm bulb already gives you.
  • Battery candles with a flicker. I am aware this is a matter of taste. I have never once seen them improve a room.

Spend the money here instead

One more lamp, at a lower height, with a proper shade and a warm, high-rendering bulb in it. That purchase has never disappointed anybody I advised.

The two exceptions I would make

Cooler light is right in exactly two places in a home: a task light where fine work happens, and a mirror where somebody applies make-up they will wear in daylight. Both are task lighting, both are switched on for minutes rather than hours, and neither is the light the room sits in.

Two

Paint and colour

  • Paint chosen from a chip, a screen, or a photograph. A colour is a relationship with the light in your room, and the chip has none of that information. Page 48.
  • The colour of the year. It is a marketing calendar, it is announced by people who sell paint, and by the time a room is finished in it the following one has been announced. Page 79.
  • Five tester pots of nearly the same white. Two sheets of lining paper with two candidates on them will tell you more than five pots dabbed straight onto the wall.
  • Cheap white emulsion, bought by the drum, for a room you will live in. It is porous, it marks, it cannot be cleaned, and it is the exact surface that makes adhesive fixings take the wall off. Page 135.
  • A feature wall, chosen because the room needs something. A wall that is different for no structural reason reads as an unfinished decision. Page 64.
  • Chalk paint for furniture that was already a good finish. It is excellent on the right piece and it is a one-way journey on the wrong one.
  • Any colour tested only in daylight, for a room you use in the evening.
  • Enough paint for two coats. Buy enough for three. The third coat is the one nobody plans for and everybody needs, and a second tin mixed later may not match.

And the one thing in this category worth more than it costs: a small pot of the wall colour, kept, labelled with the date and the room. Every mark on that wall for the next ten years is then a two-minute job instead of a repaint.

Three things worth paying more for

  • Coverage. A good paint covers in two coats what a cheap one does not cover in four, and the labour is the expensive part.
  • A washable finish in a hall or a kitchen. Matt in a corridor is a decision you will regret with a cloth in your hand.
  • Brushes. My father owned four and cleaned them for thirty years. A cheap brush sheds into the wall and no paint recovers from that.

Three

Textiles and soft furnishing

  • Curtains that stop at the sill when the floor was available. The single most common curtain error, and it costs nothing to avoid at the point of buying. Page 93.
  • Curtains at 1.5 × fullness. They will look thin for as long as you own them, and no amount of steaming fixes it. Page 94.
  • A cushion set. Four cushions in one pattern, sold together, is the matched-set problem bought deliberately. Page 117.
  • Polyester cushion inserts. They flatten in a season and never recover, and they read as flat because they are uniform. Feather, every time, two to five centimetres larger than the cover.
  • A rug bought to the size of the gap between the furniture. Measure the size that puts the front legs on it, then buy that. Page 97.
  • A very thin printed rug. It reads as a picture of a rug, and it curls at every corner within a month.
  • A sofa whose covers cannot be removed or replaced. You are renting it slowly. Page 118.
  • Blackout blinds for a bedroom, instead of lined curtains. The blind handles the light and nothing else; the curtain handles light, cold, sound and the proportion of the whole wall.

Spend the money here instead

One properly full, properly long, lined curtain on the room’s best window. It is the largest and best-lit texture in the room and it changes the proportion of the wall at the same time.

On buying textiles secondhand

Linen and wool are worth buying used and improve with age; foam and polyester are not and do not. Check a secondhand curtain for sun-rot by holding it to the light and pulling gently at the leading edge — the side that faced the window will go first, and if it parts under your thumb the whole pair is finished whatever the price.

Four

Furniture

  • Anything whose joints are one-way. If the fixings cannot be undone and done up again, the piece is finished the first time it is moved badly. Ask the question in the shop and watch the face. Page 123.
  • A matched suite — sofa, chair, footstool, all one fabric. It will never look chosen. Page 114.
  • A three-seat sofa for a flat you might leave. It is the piece I saw abandoned on more landings than any other object. Page 139.
  • Anything built to fit one alcove, if you rent.
  • A coffee table taller than the sofa seat. Level or just below, always — a tall table in front of a low sofa makes the whole arrangement look like a waiting room.
  • Storage bought in mixed materials and colours. Six identical boxes read as a system; six different ones read as clutter with lids on. Page 140.
  • A bed frame with no under-bed clearance, in a small home. That is the cheapest storage you will ever own and you are giving it away.
  • Furniture bought on the same afternoon as the rest of the room. A room furnished in one transaction looks furnished in one transaction, whatever it cost.

The honest counter-argument, which I owe you: cheap furniture is how a person on very little money furnishes a home at all, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. The request is not that you spend more in total. It is that when you do buy, you buy fewer things and buy the ones that will still be here in ten years. Page 141.

The six seconds that tell you most, in the shop

  1. Lift one corner. Weight is not everything, but a chair that is suspiciously light is usually stapled softwood and compressed board.
  2. Push the top corner sideways. Any sway in a cabinet or a bookcase now is a wobble forever.
  3. Look underneath. This is where the truth is kept: the frame, the joints, the staples, and whether anybody expected you to look.

Five

The things sold to renters

A whole category of product exists because tenants have been told they may not change anything. Some of it is excellent. Much of it is a deposit waiting to be lost.

  • Removable wallpaper, on a whole wall, untested. On flat landlord emulsion it can take the paint off in sheets. One postcard-sized test patch, two weeks, in a hidden place, before you commit a room. Page 135.
  • Stick-down vinyl floor planks. They bond harder than the packet suggests, the seams trap damp, and lifting them can take the landlord’s surface with them. A rug is reversible. Page 136.
  • Adhesive tile film and worktop wrap. They fail at the edges, near heat and water, which in a kitchen is everywhere.
  • Adhesive hooks trusted to their printed rating. Halve it, and never hang anything glazed above a bed on one. Page 133.
  • A tension rod carrying lined curtains. It is held up by friction. It will come down, and usually at night. Page 132.
  • Peel-and-stick anything in a bathroom. Steam defeats adhesive. Every time.
  • “Temporary” furniture, bought four flats in a row. The arithmetic of that decade is on page 141 and it is not kind.

The renter’s one-line rule

Buy the thing that moves house with you, not the thing that sticks to somebody else’s wall.

And the three that genuinely work

Adhesive strips, used honestly and well under their rating, on sound modern paint. Removable wallpaper on the back of a bookcase or inside a cupboard door, where failure costs an afternoon. And a clamp lamp — it needs no surface, no socket in the right place and no permission, and it is the single most useful object a tenant can own.

The ten things to buy instead

Every entry in the last five pages was a refusal. This page is the whole of what I would actually spend money on, in the order I would spend it.

  1. Bulbs for the whole house. 2700 K, CRI 90 or better. Costs less than a dinner and recolours every wall you own.
  2. A third lamp, low. Floor level, in a corner, behind something. The light almost nobody has.
  3. A dimmer, or bulbs that dim. Brightness you can take away is worth more than brightness you can add.
  4. A curtain pole, long enough. Two brackets and four screws, and it changes the proportion of the whole wall.
  5. One properly full, properly long curtain on the best window in the house.
  6. The right-sized rug, and a pad under it. The most expensive item here and the one most worth waiting and saving for.
  7. One large piece of art, hung at 145 cm, instead of fifteen small ones.
  8. One unglazed ceramic or stone object. Often the missing fourth texture, and often the price of a coffee.
  9. One good chair that can be reupholstered. Secondhand, if that is what the money allows — it usually buys better.
  10. A tin of your own wall colour, labelled and kept. The cheapest insurance in the house.

Nine of those ten will move house with you. That is not a coincidence either.

What the whole list costs

Items one to three — bulbs, a lamp, a dimmer — come to less than a single cushion from a good shop, and between them they do more than anything else on the page. Items four to ten can be bought over a year, in that order, and every one of them moves house with you. There is no step on this list that has to wait for the step after it.
