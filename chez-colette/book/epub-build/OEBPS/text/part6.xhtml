The Rented Room

Everything so far assumed you could change the room. Most people cannot. This part is the whole book again, rewritten for a home where the paint, the floor, the lights and the windows belong to somebody else — and it is the part I am proudest of, because it is where I did most of my work.

Chapter six

What you actually own

A rented flat is not a smaller version of the problem. It is a different problem, and it deserves its own chapter rather than a paragraph of apology at the end.

Here is the difficulty stated plainly. The four corrections in this book are light, height, texture and colour. In a rented home you generally do not own two of them. The colour on the walls is not yours. The fittings in the ceiling are not yours. In many flats the floor is not yours either, and neither is the thing screwed above the window.

So the advice most renters are given is: wait. Wait until you buy something. In the meantime, live in beige and do not make a fuss.

I think that is a small cruelty, and it is also wrong. I spent fourteen years working almost entirely in rented flats, and the correction that does the most in any room — light at three heights, which is all of Part Two — requires no permission from anybody. You plug it in. Height, which is Part Four, needs one removable fixing and sometimes not even that. Texture, which is Part Five, is entirely portable and goes with you to the next flat.

That is three of the four corrections available to a tenant on the first evening.

The renter’s arithmetic

You do not own the paint. You do own the light, the height of everything hung, and every texture in the room. That is three corrections out of four, and the one you are missing is the one that matters least.

Colour is the missing one, and Part Three already dealt with it honestly on page 72 — a room can be recoloured entirely in fabric, and in a rented flat that is usually the only sensible route anyway, because fabric moves house and paint does not.

What follows is the practical version. What to ask for, what to do without asking, what genuinely works, and — this part matters — what is sold to renters that does not work and will cost you your deposit.

Read the lease before you despair

Almost every tenant I worked with believed their lease forbade more than it actually did. Nobody had read it. They were living by a rumour.

I am not a lawyer and this book is not legal advice, and tenancy law differs in every country and often in every city. What I can tell you is what I found again and again when a client finally took the document out of the drawer and we read it together at the kitchen table.

What the document usually actually says

  • No alterations without consent — which is a rule about consent, not a refusal. Consent that is never asked for is never given.
  • Return the property in the same condition, reasonable wear excepted. That is the real test in most disputes: did you change it, and can it be put back?
  • Nothing whatsoever about lamps, rugs, cushions, plants, mirrors leaning against a wall, or what colour any of it is. This is the important discovery. The largest corrections in this book are not mentioned in your lease at all, because they are not alterations. They are your belongings.

The two questions that decide everything

  1. Does it make a hole? Holes are the whole argument. Nearly every dispute I ever saw between a tenant and a landlord over decoration was about a hole or a mark.
  2. If it does, can it be made invisible in an afternoon by somebody competent? A filled and touched-in screw hole in an ordinary painted wall is the most routine repair there is. A ripped patch of plaster is not.

One thing I do know, because it is French law and I was asked about it constantly: here a room only counts as a habitable main room if it has at least nine square metres of floor with a ceiling of at least 2.20 m, or failing that a volume of at least twenty cubic metres. A “bedroom” that fails those figures is not a bedroom, whatever the advertisement said. Your own country will have its equivalent, and it is worth ten minutes of an evening to find out what it is.

Before you do anything

If your tenancy came with an inventory or a check-in report — in France the état des lieux, elsewhere a schedule of condition — find it and read the section on walls. That single page tells you exactly what you will be measured against when you leave, which is more useful than any opinion of mine.

The five permissions worth asking for

Tenants almost never ask. Landlords are almost never asked. I have watched a polite two-sentence email get a whole flat repainted at the owner’s expense.

Ask for these five, in this order of likelihood. Ask for them in writing, keep the reply, and ask for one at a time rather than presenting a list that reads like a renovation.

  1. To change the light fittings, keeping the originals. The most likely yes of the five, because it costs the owner nothing and the flat is improved. Say that you will store the originals and reinstate them.
  2. To put up a curtain pole. Two brackets, four screws. Frame it as improving the property, because it does — a pole stays behind and the next tenant inherits it.
  3. To hang pictures with proper fixings. Offer the number. “About six screw holes, filled and touched in when I leave.” A number is reassuring; “some pictures” is not.
  4. To repaint, at your own expense, in a colour you name. Attach the colour. Many owners say yes to this if you promise white-back at the end; many more say yes and never ask for white-back, because a freshly painted flat lets faster.
  5. To replace the floor covering. The least likely, and usually not worth spending your credit on. A large rug does most of it. See page 136.

The email that works

Copy this

Dear —, I would like to make one small improvement at my own cost: replacing the ceiling fitting in the sitting room with a plain fabric shade. I will keep the original fitting and put it back at the end of the tenancy, and there will be no change to the wiring. May I go ahead?

Three things make it work: it is one thing, not five; it is at your expense; and it is reversible, and says so. Every refusal I ever saw was a refusal of risk, not of decoration.

Light, which needs nobody’s permission

This is the page that changes a rented flat, and it is the reason Part Two came second in this book and not last.

The single overhead fitting is the defining feature of rented housing everywhere in the world. It is there because it is cheap to install and impossible to break. It produces exactly the light described on page 29: one source, at the worst possible height, with nothing to soften it. Every rented flat I ever walked into was suffering it.

And every one of them could be corrected the same evening, by a tenant, with no tools, no permission and nothing screwed to anything.

The plan, unchanged from Part Two

Table height — a lamp at roughly seated eye level 2 sources
Floor level — one light low, in a corner, behind something 1 source
Overhead — off, most of the time 0 sources

The renter’s complications, and their answers

  • There are no sockets where the lamps should go. Then the flat is telling you to use a floor lamp that can stand behind a chair, and an extension lead run along the skirting and taped down. I have never once regretted an extension lead. I have often regretted a lamp placed where the socket was.
  • No switch by the door, so you walk into darkness. A plug-in remote socket, or bulbs that come on to a timer at dusk. This costs very little and it is the difference between using the plan and abandoning it in week two.
  • Nowhere to put a table lamp at all. A clamp lamp on a shelf edge, a bookcase or a door frame. It is the trade’s own solution and it is not ugly if the fitting is plain.

Three lamps and a fortnight of switching the big light off will do more for a rented room than any amount of paint you are not allowed to use.

The bulb is the only free renovation

If you do one thing in this part, do this one. It takes ten minutes, it costs less than lunch, it is entirely reversible, and it changes the colour of every surface you own.

Landlords buy bulbs in packs. Nobody selects them. They arrive in the flat as a consequence of a hardware shop having a special offer, and they are almost always the same: cool, bright, cheap, and poor at showing colour.

Go round the flat tonight and read what is written on the bulbs. You are looking for two numbers, both explained on pages 34 and 36.

Colour temperature, for every room you sit in 2700 K
Colour rendering, the number after CRI or Ra 90+
Brightness, in lumens, for a table lamp beside a chair 400–600 lm

Why this is the renter’s correction above all others

Because you cannot change the colour of the walls, and the bulb changes the colour of the walls. A landlord white under a 4000 K bulb is the grey-blue that everybody complains about. The same wall under 2700 K at CRI 90 is a warm, quiet, entirely acceptable white. The paint did not move. The light did.

Colette’s rule of the rented bulb

In a flat you may not paint, the bulb is the paint.

The box

Put the landlord’s bulbs in a box, label it, and keep it at the back of a cupboard for the whole tenancy. On the last day they go back in. This is the same box that will hold the original light shades, the original curtain, and anything else you swapped — and it is the single most useful object in a rented home. Page 142 is about that box.

The ceiling light you may not remove

Sometimes you are refused, or there is no fitting to change — a bare bulb on a flex, or a sealed plastic dish, or a row of spotlights you cannot reach.

Take them in order of how much they can be helped.

A bare bulb on a flex

The easiest of all, because the flex will take any shade with a standard ring and the whole operation is reversible in a minute. Buy a plain paper or fabric drum, hang it, keep the packaging. If you want the light to fall down onto a table rather than sideways into everyone’s eyes, choose one closed at the top; if the room needs bounce off the ceiling, choose one open at both ends. Page 38 explains which does what.

A sealed dish or a plastic bowl

You cannot improve its shape, so improve its behaviour. A warm, high-rendering bulb inside it is the whole correction. Then dim it — a bulb you can dim from a remote or a phone costs little and needs no electrician, because the dimming happens inside the bulb rather than in the wall. That single change turns a hard overhead light into a background wash that you can leave on at a quarter and stop resenting.

A row of downlights you cannot change

These are the hardest, and honesty is better than cleverness: you will not make them flattering. Warm, dimmable replacements where the fitting allows it, then use the lamps and leave the row off. Downlights light floors. You do not live on the floor.

The safety line

Never put a fabric or paper shade over a bulb that runs hot, and never exceed the wattage printed inside the fitting. With modern LED bulbs this is rarely an issue because they run cool, but a halogen or an old incandescent in a close-fitting shade is a genuine fire risk, not a decorating question. If anything here means touching wiring rather than unscrewing a bulb, it is an electrician’s job and your landlord’s bill.

Curtains without a drill

Part Four called the curtain pole the largest prize in the room. In a rented flat it is also the hardest, and I am going to be straight with you about what works.

What is usually already there

Look above the window before you buy anything. Most rented flats have either a track screwed to the ceiling, a pole at frame height, or a plain batten. A track can be re-hung with longer curtains. A pole at frame height cannot be moved without holes — but it can be lengthened at the ends, and page 92 explains why width often matters more than height.

Tension rods — the honest assessment

A tension rod is held up by friction alone, inside the window reveal. It works for one job: a light unlined curtain or a café panel across a narrow window. It fails, and falls, with lined curtains, with a wide span, and with any fabric that is pulled hard twice a day. I have seen a rod in a reveal hold for years and I have seen one come down in a week. Weight and width decide it.

Tension rod, sensible span under 120 cm
Tension rod, sensible curtain unlined only

The compromise I used most often

Two screws. That is what a proper pole costs you — two brackets, four small screws, in solid plaster or a timber frame, filled with a fingertip of filler on the day you leave. It is the best value in holes anywhere in this book, and it is the permission most worth asking for on page 128.

From the notebook

“A curtain hung too low says the room is rented. A curtain hung high says nothing at all — and saying nothing is the goal.”

Hanging things without nails

Adhesive fixings are the renter’s great modern convenience and the renter’s great modern disaster, and the difference between the two is entirely in the preparation.

What they will actually carry

Read the packet, believe the packet, and then halve it. A set of the largest picture-hanging strips is typically rated at around seven kilograms — but that rating assumes a smooth, clean, sealed, fully cured painted surface at room temperature, which is a description of very few rented walls. My own working rule after a number of expensive lessons:

Adhesive strips, what I will trust them with half the rating
Anything with glass, over a bed or a cot a screw, or nothing

Where they always fail

  • Textured or embossed wallpaper. The adhesive touches perhaps a third of the surface.
  • Limewash, distemper and chalky old paint. The strip holds perfectly. The paint lets go of the wall, and takes a patch with it.
  • Fresh paint. Emulsion needs time to cure properly — a week is the usual instruction, and longer is better. A strip on week-old paint will pull it off.
  • Bathrooms and kitchens. Steam and heat defeat adhesive. Every time.

The five minutes that decide it

  1. Wipe the spot with rubbing alcohol, not a household spray — sprays leave a film that is precisely what the adhesive cannot grip.
  2. Press for thirty seconds, hard, with your thumb.
  3. Wait an hour before hanging anything on it.
  4. To remove: pull the tab straight down, slowly, flat against the wall, until it stretches free. Pulling outwards is what takes the plaster with it.

And the fixing nobody thinks of: if your building is older, look for a picture rail near the ceiling. If there is one, you can hang almost anything at any height, on cord, for the price of two hooks, and you will not make a single hole.

The wall you may not paint

Landlord white is not a colour. It is the absence of a decision, chosen because it is cheap by the drum and can be patched by anybody.

You cannot change it. You can stop it being the loudest thing in the room, and there are exactly four ways, in order of how well they work.

One · Change the light on it

Page 130. A warm bulb at high rendering recolours every wall in the flat for the price of a sandwich, and it is the only one of the four that is free and invisible. Do this before you consider any of the others.

Two · Cover the right amount of it

Not a gallery of small frames — page 89 explains why a scatter of small things makes a wall look busier and emptier at once. One large piece, hung at 145 cm to its centre, takes more white off the wall than fifteen postcards and costs one fixing instead of fifteen.

Three · Put fabric on it

A quilt, a rug, a length of linen on a slim pole, a curtain along a whole wall rather than just a window. This is the oldest wall treatment there is and it does three things at once: it colours, it textures, and it quiets the room acoustically, which nobody expects and everybody notices.

Four · Lean things against it

A large mirror on the floor, a picture ledge, a leaning frame. No fixings at all, and the diagonal of a leaning object is more interesting than the flat of a hung one.

Do not skip this

Anything large and leaning must be secured to the wall or to the furniture behind it, with a strap or a hook, even though it looks stable. Falling mirrors and toppling furniture injure small children every year. This is the one place in the book where I will not offer you a no-fixings option: one screw into a wall, or do not lean it.

The honest page

Peel-and-stick, and what it does to a deposit

Removable wallpaper is sold to renters as the answer to this entire part of the book. I have used it, I have removed it, and I have twice watched it take a wall with it.

What it genuinely does well

On a smooth, sound, slightly sheened surface it is excellent, and there are three places I recommend it without hesitation: the back of a bookcase, the inside of a cupboard door, and a small panel behind a desk. In all three it is small, it is on a surface nobody inspects, and if it fails you have lost an afternoon rather than a deposit.

Where it goes wrong

  • Flat, matt, cheap emulsion — which is what a landlord’s wall almost always is. Matt paint is porous and mechanically weak at the surface. The adhesive often ends up stronger than the paint’s grip on the plaster, and the paper takes the paint off in sheets.
  • Textured walls or lining paper — it will not seat, and the edges lift within a month.
  • Anywhere warm or damp — above a radiator, in a kitchen, in a bathroom. It curls.
  • Left up for years — the longer it stays, the harder it comes off. The claim of removability quietly assumes you remove it reasonably soon.

The test patch, which is not optional

Put one piece, the size of a postcard, somewhere hidden — behind a door, low in a cupboard, behind the wardrobe. Leave it two weeks, in the season you are actually in. Then take it off and look at the wall. That piece of paper is the cheapest insurance policy in this book.

If the test patch lifts even a fleck of paint, the answer for your flat is no, and you have learned it for the price of one postcard instead of one room. Go back to page 134 and use fabric instead. Fabric has never taken a wall off in the history of decorating.

A floor you did not choose

The two rented floors are grey laminate and beige carpet, and they are the same problem in two costumes: a large, flat, uniform surface with no texture and no interest, laid by somebody buying by the square metre.

Page 70 said the floor has already voted. In a rented flat it voted without you, and the correction is the same as it was there: do not fight it, cover the part of it that matters.

The rug is the floor now

All of the sizing from page 98 applies unchanged, and the mistake is the same — too small. In a rented sitting room, the rug should be large enough that the front legs of every seat stand on it. That single rule turns a collection of furniture stranded on somebody else’s laminate into a room.

Sitting-room rug, the minimum that works front legs on
Clear walking width left around it 60–70 cm

A rug on carpet, which everybody asks about

Yes, and it is one of the most useful things a tenant can do — it is how you get texture onto a floor that has none. Two conditions. Use a pad made for laying rug over carpet, not the ordinary sort for hard floors, or it will creep and ruck within a week. And choose a rug with some weight and a low, dense pile; a thin flat-weave over a soft carpet feels like walking on a dropped tea towel.

On stick-down vinyl planks

I understand the temptation and I will still say no. On a rented floor they can bond far harder than the packet suggests, the seams trap damp, and lifting them off a landlord’s vinyl or a varnished board can take the surface with it. A rug is reversible. That is worth more than a better-looking floor for the eighteen months you will live there.

What to look for, since the rug is doing so much

Wool if you can afford it, because it takes weight and recovers; a flat wool or a wool-and-jute blend if you cannot. Avoid the very thin printed sort entirely — it reads as a picture of a rug, and it will curl at every corner within a month. And buy the pad. A rug without a pad wears out from underneath, slides, and feels cheap underfoot however much it cost.

The kitchen you cannot change

Nobody rents a kitchen they chose. The doors are laminate, the worktop is a pattern pretending to be stone, and there is one fluorescent-looking fitting in the middle of the ceiling.

Five corrections, none of them structural, in the order I would do them.

  1. Light the worktop, not the room. A plug-in strip or a pair of small lamps under the wall cupboards is the single biggest change available here. You currently work in your own shadow, because the only light is behind you. This is not decoration; it is the difference between a kitchen that is pleasant to cook in and one that is not.
  2. Change the handles, keep the originals. Most cupboard handles are two screws. A plain metal handle on a poor door reads as a plain door; a shiny moulded handle reads as a cheap one. Twenty minutes, entirely reversible, and the originals go in the box on page 142.
  3. Clear the worktop to two things. A rented kitchen is usually small and always looks worse cluttered. Everything that is not used daily goes into a cupboard. This costs nothing and it is the largest visual improvement on the list.
  4. Add the missing family. Page 111. A kitchen is laminate, steel and glass — hard, smooth, cold. A wooden board left standing, a ceramic jug, a stone mortar, a linen cloth on the rail: the four families arrive on one worktop for very little money, and the room stops reading as a fitted unit.
  5. Something growing. Herbs if the window faces anywhere but north, and see page 112 for what to do if it does.

From the notebook

“A rented kitchen cannot be made beautiful. It can be made kind to cook in, and that is nearly the same thing by seven in the evening.”

And what I would not bother with

Stick-on tile film over the splashback, adhesive worktop wrap, and anything that promises to make laminate look like marble. All three fail in the same place — at the edges, near heat and water — and all three can leave a residue that costs you far more at the end of the tenancy than the cupboard doors were ever worth.

The bathroom you cannot change

The smallest room, the worst light, and the one place where the corrections cost almost nothing because there is so little surface to correct.

The light, which is the whole problem

A bathroom is nearly always lit from directly overhead, which puts every face into the shadow pattern that makes people believe they look tired. The professional answer is light beside the mirror at roughly face height, not above it, so that both sides of the face are lit. In a rented bathroom you often cannot add a fitting, so the two things you can do are:

  • Change the bulb to 2700–3000 K at CRI 90 or better. A bathroom under a cheap cool bulb is the origin of a great deal of unnecessary unhappiness at eight in the morning.
  • If there is a socket, a small lamp on the windowsill or a shelf, on in the evening. A bathroom lit low at night is a different room.

The safety line

Electricity and water are governed by real rules, and they differ by country — in many places sockets are not permitted in a bathroom at all, and fittings must carry a moisture rating. Whatever you plug in, do not improvise around this. Nothing in this book is worth a shock.

The other four, in one paragraph

A heavier shower curtain, hung high and long, is the largest piece of fabric in the room and behaves exactly like a curtain elsewhere — page 116. Two good towels of the same colour instead of six of different ones. A plant that likes low light and damp air, which is a short and forgiving list. And nothing on any surface except what is used every day, because in a room this small, clutter is the decoration.

The three-minute version

Change the bulb. Take everything off the surfaces except what you use daily. Hang two towels of the same colour. That is a bathroom corrected, and it costs the price of one bulb.

Furniture that moves with you

A tenant buys furniture twice: once for this flat, and once again when it does not fit the next one. The second purchase is avoidable, and avoiding it is how a renter ends up with good things.

The three questions before anything large is bought

  1. Will it go through a standard door and round a standard turn? Measure the narrowest point of the route, not the room — the front door, the stairwell, the turn at the top. A sofa that fits the room and not the stair is a sofa you will sell at a loss.
  2. Does it depend on this wall? Anything built to fit one alcove is bought for one flat. Freestanding beats fitted for anybody who moves.
  3. Can it be repaired, and would I still want it in ten years? Page 118 and page 123, unchanged. This is more important for a renter than for anybody, because your furniture is the only part of your home that is actually yours.

What is worth spending on, in a rented flat

Lamps — they go everywhere, forever spend
One good chair, one good table spend
Rug, linen, ceramics — portable texture spend
Anything fitted, cut down, or sized to this room do not

The size rule for people who move

Buy slightly smaller than this flat allows. Rooms shrink more often than they grow, a two-seat sofa fits nearly everywhere, and a three-seat sofa is the piece I saw abandoned on more landings than any other object in Lyon.

Where to buy it

Secondhand, for anything made of solid wood or with a frame worth having. A renter’s budget goes furthest in the market for good old furniture, because the things that survived forty years are by definition the things built to. Page 120 is about what that does to how a room looks; this page is only about what it does to what you can afford.

Storage, without building anything

Rented flats are short of cupboards, and the shortage does more visible damage than any colour on any wall, because a room with nowhere to put things is a room that is always covered in things.

I put this in Part Six rather than in a chapter of its own because it is almost entirely a renter’s problem. People who own their homes build a cupboard. You cannot, so you must be cleverer.

The four places a flat is hiding

  • The top thirty centimetres of every wall. Nothing is ever up there. A shelf at 200 cm, running the length of a wall, holds everything you own but rarely use, and reads as architecture rather than storage. Page 91.
  • Under the bed. Flat boxes, lidded, on the floor — and if the bed is too low, bed risers are cheap and invisible under a valance.
  • Behind a curtain. A rail across an alcove with a linen curtain in front of it is a wardrobe, costs almost nothing, comes down in ten minutes and is what half of Paris does.
  • The back of every door. Over-door hooks make no holes and take coats out of the room entirely.

The rule that keeps it from looking like a storeroom

Colette’s rule of visible storage

Visible storage must be one material and one colour, or it stops being storage and becomes clutter with a lid on.

Six identical boxes read as a system. Six different boxes read as a mess, even if they hold exactly the same things. This is the cheapest tidying in the book: not buying more storage, but making the storage you already own match.

Two cautions

  • A high shelf must be fixed into something structural — that is a screw, not an adhesive strip, and it is one of the few places in this part where I will tell you to spend a permission on it. A shelf that comes down at 200 cm is not a decorating mistake.
  • Do not store anything in the room you actually live in if there is any alternative. Storage in a sitting room is the reason that sitting room never feels calm, whatever colour the walls are.

The quiet part

Who profits from a temporary home

Ask why a renter is told to wait, and who is standing at the other end of the waiting.

There is an entire category of furniture that exists because people believe their home is temporary. It is light, it is cheap, it is designed to be assembled once, and it is very difficult to repair — the fixings are one-way, the material at the joint is often compressed board that cannot take a screw twice, and the covers are not made to be replaced. That is not a moral failing on anybody’s part. It is a product designed honestly for a customer who has said, in effect, that they only need it for now.

But look at what happens over a decade of renting. You buy the temporary thing. It half survives one move. You buy another. Over ten years and four flats, a person can quite easily spend more on furniture that was never meant to last than they would have spent on a few things that were — and end up at the end of it owning nothing.

Meanwhile the walls stay landlord white, because the tenant has been told that changing anything is not their place. Magnolia and off-white are not chosen for beauty. They are chosen because they can be patched by any decorator from any tin, which is a perfectly rational decision for a person who owns twelve flats and a slightly sad one for the person living in this one.

Say this plainly

This is not a conspiracy. Nobody sat in a room and decided that renters should live badly. It is what happens when one party buys for turnover and the other buys for eighteen months, and nobody in the chain is buying for ten years. I am only pointing out that you can step out of that pattern on your own, this week, without anybody’s permission.

Buy few things, buy them to last, and let them move house with you. The flat is temporary. You are not.

The fair objection, which I will answer

Somebody will say: cheap furniture is how a person on very little money furnishes a flat at all, and a lecture about buying well is a lecture from somebody with savings. That is true, and I mean it. So let me be precise about what I am actually asking: not that you spend more in total, but that when you do buy, you buy fewer things and buy the ones that will move house. Three lamps and one good chair, bought over two years, cost less than a room of new furniture bought in one afternoon.

Day one, and the last day

Everything in this part depends on being able to put the flat back exactly as you found it. That is a practice, and it starts on the day you get the keys.

Day one, before a single box is unpacked

  1. Photograph every wall, every floor, and every existing mark, with the date on the file. Not a general view — go close on the chips, the scuffs, the stained corner of the carpet. This takes fifteen minutes and it is the only evidence that will exist.
  2. Photograph every light fitting before you touch it, so that reinstating them at the end is a matter of copying a picture.
  3. Find out what the walls are painted with. Ask, or look in the cupboard under the sink where half-used tins live. A tin of the landlord’s own paint is worth more to you at the end of the tenancy than anything you will buy.
  4. Start the box. Page 130. One box, labelled, at the back of a cupboard, for every original thing you remove: bulbs, shades, handles, the shower curtain, the plastic dish from the ceiling.

The last day, which is now one afternoon

Take down what you put up. Fill the screw holes with a fingertip of filler, let it dry, sand it flat with a piece of fine paper, and touch in with the landlord’s paint using a small brush and a dabbing motion rather than a stroke. Put the box back where it came from. Photograph the walls again.

Colette’s rule of the filled hole

A hole you can fill is not damage. Take the photographs on day one, keep the originals in a box, and every correction in this part costs you nothing when you leave.

I have handed back a great many flats this way, and the honest truth is that the ones I had corrected came back cleaner and better documented than the ones I had not.

One

You do not own the paint. You own the light, the height of everything hung, and every texture in the room — three corrections out of four.

Two

A hole you can fill is not damage. Photographs on day one, originals in a box, and nothing here costs you a deposit.

And the six that do not need their own page

  • Read the lease, then ask. Most refusals are refusals of risk. One thing at a time, at your expense, reversible, in writing.
  • Halve every adhesive rating, and never trust one over a bed. Wipe with alcohol, press thirty seconds, remove by pulling straight down.
  • Test-patch removable wallpaper on a postcard of hidden wall for two weeks before you commit a room to it.
  • The rug is the floor now — front legs on, and a proper pad if it is going over carpet.
  • Buy slightly smaller than this flat allows. Rooms shrink more often than they grow.
  • Visible storage must be one material and one colour, or it is clutter with a lid on.

And the one that is not practical at all

Stop treating the flat as a waiting room. A home that is corrected properly for eighteen months is eighteen months of living well, and the lamps, the linen and the rug all come with you to the next one.

The flat is temporary. The way you see a room is not, and neither is anything worth buying.
