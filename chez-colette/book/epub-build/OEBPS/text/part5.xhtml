Part Five

Texture and Material

The last of the four corrections, and the one that turns a correctly lit, correctly coloured, correctly proportioned room into somewhere a person lives. It is also where almost all the money in decorating goes, so this part is careful about what is worth buying.

Chapter five

Why texture comes last, and decides everything

Texture is fifth in the order of operations because it depends on everything above it. It is the first thing anybody notices when they walk in.

That is not a contradiction. The order of operations is about what you fix first; it says nothing about what a visitor perceives first. And what a visitor perceives first, before they have registered a single colour or noticed a lamp, is whether the room feels hard or soft, warm or cool, dressed or bare. That reading happens in well under a second and it is almost entirely a reading of surface.

Why it has to be last

Because texture is read by light. The whole visible difference between velvet and linen is how each one handles a beam: velvet has a pile that absorbs light down its length and catches it across, linen has a slubbed weave that throws tiny highlights and shadows in every direction. Change the light and you have changed the texture, exactly as you changed the colour in Part Three.

So a room lit from one height has, in a real sense, no texture at all. Flat overhead light strikes every surface at the same angle and produces almost no shadow, which is precisely the information your eye uses to tell one material from another. This is why a room can be full of beautiful fabrics and still look like a catalogue page: the light is not letting you feel any of them.

Worth knowing before you spend anything

Texture is invisible without raking light. A fifteen-dollar lamp on the floor will do more for the linen you already own than a better linen will.

What this part is careful about

This is where the money goes. Sofas, rugs, curtains, upholstery — the big purchases all live in this part, and most of them are made once a decade. So I am going to be specific about which of them reward spending and which do not, and page 122 lists what I would not buy at all.

The four families

You met the rule on page 10. Here is the working version, with what each family is actually doing in a room.

Why four and not three or five

This is my rule and not a law, so here is the reasoning rather than an assertion. Two families give you no contrast at all. Three is enough for a shop display, where the goods are the point and the room is a backdrop. The fourth is what stops the room reading as arranged — because in a real home, materials accumulate from different sources over different years, and four different families is the minimum that convincingly suggests that history.

Five and six are fine. Nothing goes wrong above four. The rule is a floor, not a ceiling, and the page that matters is the one after next — because the missing family is nearly always the third.

Family one

Woven — weight, weave and what each does

Almost every room has too much of this family and too little variety within it. Four soft things in four different colours is still one texture.

Weight matters more than fibre

A heavy cloth hangs in deep folds and reads as substantial; a light cloth of the same fibre hangs limp and reads as cheap. This is why a thin linen curtain can look worse than a heavy cotton one despite costing three times as much.

When you are choosing fabric, hold a length of it up and let it fall. If it drops into a few deep folds it is heavy enough for curtains. If it clings to itself in many small creases, it is a dress fabric.

Contrast within the family

The cure for a four-soft-things room is not fewer soft things. It is soft things that disagree: a smooth linen against a nubbly wool against a coarse jute. Same family, three completely different surfaces. Page 115.

Why linen reads expensive

It is worth understanding, because the reason is not the price — and once you know it you can get most of the effect from cheaper cloth.

Linen is spun from flax. The fibre is not uniform: it varies in thickness along its length, so a linen yarn has thick and thin places, and a woven linen cloth has a surface of small irregularities. That irregularity is called slub, and it is the whole thing.

A slubbed surface catches light at hundreds of slightly different angles. So a linen curtain in raking light is never one flat tone — it is a field of tiny highlights and shadows that shifts as you move. Your eye reads that variability as depth, and reads depth as quality.

A perfectly uniform synthetic of the same colour reflects light identically across its whole surface. It has one tone. Your eye reads one tone as flat, and flat as cheap — not because it knows the price, but because uniformity is what machines produce and irregularity is what natural fibre produces.

The general principle, which is worth more than the linen

Irregularity reads as expensive. Uniformity reads as manufactured. This is true of cloth, timber, glaze, stone and plaster equally.

How to get the effect without the price

  • A linen blend. Linen-cotton or linen-viscose keeps most of the slub at a fraction of the cost, and creases less.
  • Any irregular weave. Slub cotton, basketweave, herringbone, bouclé. It is the surface variation you are buying, not the flax.
  • Raking light. As on page 106 — the effect is created by the light. Cheap cloth lit from the side beats expensive cloth lit from above.

And the honest cost of linen

It creases the moment anyone sits on it, and the creases do not fall out. If you want linen you have to want the creased version, because that is the only version there is. People who buy linen and then resent it have bought an idea of linen from a photograph taken before anybody sat down.

Family two

Wood — grain, finish and age

My husband is a joiner, so I have been corrected about wood for twenty years. Three things matter and the third is the one nobody mentions.

One · Solid, veneer, or neither

  • Solid timber can be sanded and refinished, sometimes many times. It moves with humidity, which is why solid tabletops are built to allow it. It lasts generations.
  • Veneer is a thin layer of real timber over a stable board. It looks identical when new, it is more stable than solid, and it can usually be refinished once or not at all. Good veneer on good board is a perfectly honest product.
  • Printed foil or laminate is a photograph of wood. It cannot be repaired, it chips at the edges, and once chipped it announces exactly what it is. This is the one to avoid in anything you intend to keep.

How to tell: look at an edge and at the end of a leg. Solid shows continuous grain wrapping round. Veneer shows a fine seam. Print shows a repeat — find the same knot twice and you have found a photograph.

Two · The finish decides the texture

Oiled timber is matte, open-pored and warm to touch; it marks, and it can be repaired with more oil. Lacquered timber is sealed and shiny; it resists marks and cannot be locally repaired. Waxed is between the two. An oiled oak table and a lacquered oak table are, texturally, two different materials.

Three · The thing nobody mentions

Mixing wood tones is not a mistake. Almost every guide tells you to match your woods, and it is the single worst piece of received advice in this family, because it produces exactly the matched-set problem on page 114.

A room with an oak floor, a walnut table and a pine shelf reads as a room that accumulated. A room where every timber matches reads as a showroom set, and it is achievable only by buying everything at once from one place — which is precisely the impression you do not want.

What to actually match

Not the species. The undertone — page 50. Warm timbers together, cool timbers together. An orange-toned pine beside a grey-limed oak is the clash people mistake for a species problem.

Family three

The missing family

In the great majority of rooms I have worked in, this is the one that was not there. It is also the cheapest of the four to correct.

Ceramic and stone do something no other family does: they are cool and dense. They take light and hold it rather than absorbing or scattering it, so their shadows have hard edges. In a room of fabric and timber — both soft-edged, both warm — a single unglazed pot introduces a completely different kind of surface, and the whole room sharpens.

What counts

  • Unglazed or matte-glazed pottery. A pot, a jug, a bowl, a lamp base. Matte and slightly rough is doing far more work than high-gloss, which reads closer to glass.
  • Plaster. A limewashed wall is this family — page 68 — which is one reason those walls feel so different from painted ones.
  • Stone and marble. A worktop, a hearth, a small offcut used as a board or a tray.
  • Terracotta and brick. Exposed brick counts. So does a single terracotta pot, which is the cheapest entry into this family there is.
  • Concrete and cement in small quantities.

Why it is the cheapest fix in the book

Because you need one object, and it can be very small. A single unglazed pot on a shelf changes the count from three families to four. You do not need a stone worktop. Charity shops and garden centres are full of this family for the price of a coffee, and a plain terracotta plant pot — the ordinary kind — is both this family and the container for the next.

The test, for any room that feels soft and slightly shapeless

Count the things in the room that are cold to the touch. If the answer is none, you have found what is missing.

One caution, since it is the family most often bought badly: high-gloss white ceramic reads as bathroom fittings rather than as material. Matte, unglazed, or a glaze with some variation in it — the irregularity rule from page 109 applies here exactly as it does to cloth.

Family four

Something living, honestly

A plant is the only thing in a room that changes shape over months. That is its entire job, and it is a job nothing else can do.

Every other object in your home is fixed. The plant grows, leans towards the window, drops a leaf, needs water. It introduces irregularity that is not designed — which, by the rule on page 109, is exactly what reads as real.

Choose by the light you have, not by the leaf you like

This is the whole of it, and it is why so many people conclude they cannot keep plants. A plant sold as an indoor plant is not therefore suited to a north-facing corner three metres from the window.

  • Bright light, near a south or west window: most of what is for sale will be happy.
  • Moderate light, a few metres back or a north window: choose deliberately — pothos, philodendron, spider plant, cast iron plant, dracaena.
  • Genuinely low light, an internal corner: the tolerant survivors — snake plant, ZZ plant, aspidistra. Expect slow growth and accept it.
  • No natural light at all: nothing will thrive long-term. Use branches in water and change them, or rotate a plant in and out of a brighter room.

The claim I will not repeat

You will read that houseplants purify indoor air. The famous experiment behind that claim was run in small sealed chambers, and later analysis of what it implies at realistic room densities suggests the effect is negligible compared with simply opening a window. Have plants because a room with something alive in it is better than a room without. Do not buy them as air filtration.

The cheapest version, which I use constantly

Branches in a jug of water. Bare in winter, in leaf in spring, replaced whenever you like. They need no light, cost nothing, and satisfy this family completely. My grandmother kept beech or hornbeam in water on the windowsill from October to March and never owned a houseplant in her life.

Metal and glass — the seasoning

These are not a fifth family. They are a seasoning, and the quantity is the whole question.

Metal and glass are both specular — they reflect light directly rather than scattering it. That gives them enormous visual weight for their size: one brass lamp draws more attention than a whole wool rug. A little is lively. A lot is a hotel lobby.

Should metals match?

No — for the same reason woods should not. Two metals in a room is normal and looks considered; one metal everywhere looks specified. What I would do is let one be dominant and the second appear two or three times, so it reads as a decision rather than an oversight.

What genuinely should agree is temperature: warm metals with warm metals, cool with cool. Brass and chrome in the same sightline is the combination that looks accidental, and it is the undertone rule again.

The quantity rule

Metal and glass should be the smallest family in the room by area. If they are not, the room will read as commercial whatever else you do.

Glass has one additional property worth using: it is the only material that disappears. A glass table in a small room takes up its volume without taking up its visual weight, which is occasionally exactly the right answer — though it is also a fingerprint magnet and it gives you nothing texturally.

The most expensive habit in decorating

Nothing that came in a set

Colette’s rule of the matched set

Nothing that arrived as a set will ever look like it was chosen.

A three-piece suite. A bedroom set. A dining table with its six matching chairs and the sideboard that goes with them. All bought together, all delivered on the same day.

The problem is not that any individual piece is bad. It is that the set announces its own origin. Every object in it shares one timber, one finish, one proportion, one design language, because it was designed to be sold together — and a room furnished this way tells anyone walking in that all of its decisions were made in a single afternoon by somebody else.

What a room without a set looks like

A sofa from one place. Two armchairs that do not match each other. A coffee table that was somebody's grandmother's. A floor lamp bought years later. None of them agree on species, period or price, and they all agree on temperature and scale — and the room looks as though it was assembled by a person with opinions over a decade, because it was.

How to break a set you already own

You do not have to replace it. You have to interrupt it.

  • Remove one piece. Take the matching armchair out of the room and put something else in its place. One dissenting object breaks the pattern for the whole set.
  • Change what is on it. Different cushions on the sofa from the ones it came with. A cloth or a run of books over a matching sideboard.
  • Separate the pieces. A set reads as a set when it is together. Move the sideboard to the hall and it becomes a piece of furniture again.
  • Add the two families it lacks. A set is almost always one timber and one fabric. Ceramic and something living will do more than any rearrangement.

Where I would spend, if you are starting from nothing

One good sofa, bought alone. Then everything else acquired slowly and separately, secondhand where possible. A room furnished over three years for the price of one suite will look better than the suite, and every piece in it will be something you chose.

Contrast inside one family

The four-family rule gets you to a room that works. This page is what separates a room that works from a room people remember.

Having all four families is a floor, not a ceiling. The next move is contrast within each one — because the eye can distinguish surfaces far more finely than four categories, and a room that only satisfies the minimum still reads a little schematic.

Within the woven family

The most common failure, because it is where the volume is. A room with four soft things that are all medium-weight, medium-smooth cotton has one texture repeated four times.

Put a smooth cloth against a coarse one: a fine linen cushion on a nubbly wool sofa, over a jute rug. Three surfaces from one family, and every one of them feels different under a hand. That is a richer room than four different families all of the same smoothness.

Within the wooden family

Oiled against lacquered. Sawn against planed. A smooth walnut table beside a rattan chair beside a rough-sawn shelf. Page 110 — do not match your woods.

Within ceramic and stone

Matte against glossy. A rough unglazed terracotta pot next to a smooth glazed bowl is a genuine contrast, and both are cheap.

The refinement

Four families is the minimum. Two surfaces within each family is the difference between a room that works and a room somebody wants to sit down in.

The hand test

Walk round the room and actually touch ten things. Not look — touch. If more than about four of them feel substantially the same under your palm, the room is texturally thinner than it looks, and you now know exactly which surfaces to change.

This is the test I use in a room that is bothering me and I cannot say why. It works because your hand has not been trained by photographs, and your eye has.

The curtain is the largest texture in the room

Part Four treated the curtain as proportion. It is also, in most rooms, the single biggest area of fabric you own — larger than the sofa.

Measure it. A pair of curtains at two and a half times fullness across a normal window is several square metres of cloth, hanging vertically, in folds, usually next to your main light source. Nothing else in the room is that much of one material, that well lit, at that angle.

Which means the curtain is doing more texturally than anything else in the room, and it is almost always chosen on colour alone.

What to choose it for instead

  • Weight, first. Page 108 — hold a length up and let it fall. Deep folds or small creases tells you everything.
  • Surface, second. A slubbed or irregular weave against a window is the single best-lit texture in your home, because it is side-lit for most of the day. This is where linen genuinely earns its price.
  • Colour, third. Genuinely third. A curtain in a plain, quiet colour and a beautiful cloth beats a striking colour in a flat one, every time.

From the notebook

My grandmother chose cloth by holding it against the window of the room it was going in, never in the shop, and never flat — always gathered in her hand, because a curtain is never seen flat. She said a fabric that is only beautiful lying down is a tablecloth.

The sheer, which is the underrated one

A fine sheer behind a heavier curtain does three jobs at once: it diffuses direct sun into soft light — page 25 — it gives daytime privacy without blocking the view, and texturally it puts a second, completely different surface at the window. A heavy wool curtain over a fine linen sheer is two contrasting textures in the best-lit place in the room, and it is why the arrangement has survived for two centuries.

The honest page

Cushions and throws

This is the category people reach for when a room is not working, and it is almost never the answer — but it is not nothing either, so let me be exact.

Why it is the wrong instinct

A cushion is small, soft, and belongs to a family the room already has too much of. If your sitting room is dissatisfying, the cause is on one of the previous four parts' pages — the lighting height, the hanging height, the missing third family — and a cushion addresses none of them. This is the purchase that feels like progress and produces none, which is precisely why it is sold so hard.

What cushions genuinely do

  • Introduce contrast within the woven family. Page 115. A coarse wool cushion on a smooth sofa is real texture work.
  • Break a matched set. Page 114. The cheapest possible interruption.
  • Carry the accent colour into a third place. Page 69.

The rules, such as they are

How many on a three-seat sofa3–5, odd numbers

Feather or down insert vs. polyesterfeather, every time

The insert is the one that matters and it is the one nobody thinks about. A polyester insert the same size as the cover gives you a flat, slightly lumpy square. A feather insert two to five centimetres larger fills the corners and can be knocked into shape. Good covers on bad inserts look cheap; plain covers on good inserts look expensive. If you buy anything in this category, buy inserts.

The one that costs people most

Cushions bought as a matching set of four, in the fabric the sofa came with. That is the matched-set problem in miniature, bought deliberately, at the exact moment somebody was trying to fix it.

Upholstery — where the money should go

A sofa is the most expensive thing in most sitting rooms and the thing people know least about buying. Four things decide whether it is still good in fifteen years.

One · The frame

Kiln-dried hardwood, jointed and glued, is what lasts. Softwood, stapled, or particle board will loosen and there is nothing to be done about it. Ask what the frame is made of; a shop that cannot tell you is telling you.

Two · Whether it can be recovered

This is the question that decides everything else. A sofa with a good frame can be reupholstered two or three times across fifty years, and each time it becomes a new sofa for a fraction of the price. A sofa built so that the cover cannot come off is a disposable object however much it cost.

The one question to ask in the shop

Can this be reupholstered? If the answer is no, you are not buying furniture. You are renting it slowly.

Three · The rub test on the fabric

Upholstery fabric is tested for abrasion resistance. In Europe the figure is the Martindale count; in the United States it is Wyzenbeek double rubs. Higher is more durable. As working bands:

Light domestic — occasional chair10,000–15,000 Martindale
General domestic — a normal sofa15,000–25,000
Heavy domestic — family, daily, pets25,000+

It is a laboratory abrasion figure, not a guarantee, and it says nothing about staining, fading or how a fabric will pill. But it is published, it is comparable between fabrics, and almost no shopper asks for it.

Four · Removable covers

Washable loose covers on a sofa that gets real use is the difference between a piece you keep and a piece you replace. It is the single most practical thing on this page.

Rugs — fibre and pile

Pile, which changes the texture more than the fibre

  • Flatweave — no pile. Crisp, hard-wearing, reversible, cool. Reads as a graphic rather than as softness.
  • Low pile — the practical default. Chairs move over it, dirt comes out of it.
  • High pile and shag — the softest texture available in a room, and the hardest to keep. Chair legs sink into it and it traps everything.

The underlay nobody buys

A rug pad stops the rug creeping, stops it wearing through from the friction underneath, adds noticeably to how it feels underfoot, and prevents the corners curling into a trip hazard. It costs very little relative to the rug and roughly doubles its life. Buy it with the rug, not later.

Patina — the thing you cannot buy

Everything in this part has been about choosing materials. This page is about what happens to them afterwards, which is the part that cannot be bought and is most of what you are actually responding to in rooms you admire.

Patina is what use does to a surface. Brass darkens in the places hands touch it and stays bright elsewhere. Oiled oak deepens in colour and picks up small marks. Linen softens and its creases become permanent and soft rather than sharp. Leather lightens where it is sat on. Stone wears hollow in a doorway over a century.

In every case the material is being changed by its own use, so the marks are a true record of how the object has been lived with. That is why patina reads as authenticity and why no manufactured distressing quite convinces — a factory-applied wear pattern is in the wrong places, because nobody's hand has been there.

Which is the practical point

Choose materials that get better as they are used, not materials that only get worse.

Materials that improve

Unlacquered brass and copper. Oiled and waxed timber. Linen. Full-grain leather. Wool. Unglazed terracotta. Lime plaster. Stone.

Materials that only deteriorate

Lacquered and foil-wrapped anything. Chrome. Printed laminate. Polyester velvet. Viscose. High-gloss paint on woodwork that gets touched. Each of these begins at its best possible condition, and every subsequent day is a small loss.

What follows from this, practically

It is a strong argument for buying fewer, better, repairable things, and it is the reason the secondhand version of a good object is frequently preferable to the new one — somebody else has already done the first ten years of the work for you, and the price reflects the opposite.

It is also the honest argument against the aesthetic this book is working in. Rooms that look like this take time as well as decisions, and no amount of correct choosing on a Saturday produces a surface that has been touched for thirty years. That is not a reason not to start. It is a reason to buy things that will still be here in thirty years.

What not to buy

Texture and soft furnishing

A matching cushion set

Four identical cushions in the fabric the sofa came with. It is the matched-set problem bought deliberately, usually by somebody trying to fix the room. Page 117.

Cushion covers without inserts

Or worse, covers with the polyester insert that comes free. The insert is the part that decides whether it looks expensive, and it is the part being economised on. Buy feather inserts two to five centimetres larger than the cover and keep the covers you already have.

Viscose rugs sold as “art silk” or “banana silk”

Beautiful in the shop, and they mark with a drop of water, show every footprint, and deteriorate exactly where people walk. This is the only rug fibre I would tell you to avoid outright.

A sofa whose cover cannot be removed or replaced

Page 118. Whatever it costs, it is a disposable object, and a decade from now the choice will be to live with it or throw it away.

Faux-distressed anything

Factory-applied wear is in the wrong places, because no hand has been there. It reads as costume next to a single genuinely worn object. If you want patina, buy the secondhand version of a good thing — page 120.

Scented candles as a decorating purchase

Not because there is anything wrong with them, but because they are sold in this aisle as though they were a texture. They are a consumable. They do not change a room and they run out, which is the business model.

What to buy instead, in order

One unglazed pot, from a charity shop or a garden centre. Feather inserts for the cushions you already own. A rug pad for the rug you already own. And then nothing else in this category until the lighting and the hanging heights are corrected, because a room fails those two before it ever fails on texture.

The quiet part

Who profits from furniture that cannot be repaired

Go and look underneath your sofa. Then look at the back of a chest of drawers. You are looking for the same thing in both places: whether anybody expected this object to be taken apart.

A hardwood frame with proper joints can be reupholstered indefinitely. A stapled softwood frame cannot. A drawer on wooden runners can be repaired by anybody; a drawer in a particle-board carcass whose runners have torn out of the board cannot be repaired by anyone at all, because there is nothing left to fix it to.

The second kind is not badly made. It is precisely made, to a specification, and the specification includes the end.

Nobody decided this in a room somewhere. It is quieter and sadder than that.

There is no money in a sofa that a family recovers twice and hands on. There is a great deal of money in a sofa that is pleasant for six years and impossible to repair in the seventh, and in the replacement, and in the one after that. No one has to intend it. A market that competes on the price of the first purchase will arrive here on its own, because the cost of the second purchase is invisible at the moment of the first.

The test that costs nothing

Before you buy anything large, ask what would happen if one part of it broke. If the answer is that you would throw the whole thing away, you have learned the price properly.

What I am not saying

I am not saying buy expensive. Some very expensive furniture is built exactly this way, and a secondhand hardwood-framed armchair with a tired cover is usually cheaper than a new flat-pack one and will outlive it by forty years. The division is not price. It is whether the thing was built to be opened.

And if the flat-pack one is what you can afford this year, buy it without apology. Then know what it is, keep it going as long as you can, and spend differently when you are next able to. That is a completely respectable way to furnish a home.

  • Weight before fibre, surface before colour — in curtains above all, because the curtain is the largest and best-lit texture in the room.
  • Buy the insert, not the cover. Feather, two to five centimetres larger than the cover.
  • Ask whether it can be reupholstered. If not, you are renting it slowly.

That is the four corrections

Light, height, texture, colour — in that order, always. Everything in the rest of this book is those four applied to a particular situation — a rented flat where you may not drill, and then a room at a time.
