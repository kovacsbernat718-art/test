How the audit works

This is the thing I actually did, in a client’s home, in the first ten minutes, before I said a word about what anything cost.

The rules

  1. Do it in the evening, with the lights you normally use. Not in daylight, not with everything switched on for the occasion. The room as you actually live in it.
  2. Sit down where you normally sit. Most faults in a room are only visible from the chair, and every photograph of a room is taken standing up, which is why rooms photograph better than they feel.
  3. Answer yes or no. Do not answer “sort of.” If you are hesitating, the answer is no.
  4. Do not fix anything while you audit. Write it down and move on. The order matters more than the speed, and the order comes at the end.

What you will have at the end

Four scores out of six — light, height, texture, colour — and a page number. The lowest score is where you start, and it is nearly always light, which is fortunate, because light is the cheapest of the four to correct.

Before you begin

Nothing on these pages asks what you own, what you spent, or what your taste is. Every question is about what is physically true of the room. That is deliberate. Your taste was never the problem.

The three audit pages that follow are meant to be written on. If this is a file rather than a printed book, print these four pages on their own — they are the ones worth having on paper.

If you share the home

Do the audit separately and compare the sheets. It is the only way I know to have this conversation without it becoming a conversation about taste — two people who disagree about a colour will nearly always agree that a room has one light source and a rug that is too small. Fix what you agree on first. It is usually most of it.

The audit · part two

Height and proportion

Stand in the doorway and look, then measure two things

  • Every picture and mirror has its centre at about 145 cm
  • The curtain pole is above the window frame, not on it, and wider than the glass
  • The curtains reach the floor
  • No piece of furniture is pushed flat against the wall behind it
  • The rug is large enough that the front legs of every seat stand on it
  • There is one empty corner, or one clear surface, that is doing nothing

The audit · part three

Texture, and then colour

Texture — by touching, not by looking

  • Something woven — curtain, rug, upholstery, cushion
  • Something wooden, with visible grain
  • Something ceramic, stone or plaster
  • Something living
  • At least two genuinely different surfaces within the woven family
  • Nothing in this room arrived as a matched set — or a set has been interrupted
Score, out of six — and the missing family is

Colour — last, and only now

  • I know the name and the finish of the paint on these walls
  • The wall colour and the floor do not fight — their undertones agree
  • The colour was tested on this wall, in this light, before it went on
  • No more than three or four colours run through the whole home
  • The woodwork is either the same as the wall or deliberately different
  • Looking at it now, with the lamps on, I still like it
Score, out of six

Do not act on a low colour score until light, height and texture are all above four. Half the rooms I was called to repaint did not need repainting.

What your answers mean

Write your four scores in a row. The lowest is where you begin, and if two are equal, begin with whichever comes first in the order light, height, texture, colour.

Reading the whole row

  • All four at five or six. The room is correct. If you still do not like it, the problem is not in this book — it is that the room is not being used for anything you enjoy, and page 162 is the honest page about that.
  • Light low, everything else high. The best possible result, because one evening and three lamps will move every other score with it.
  • Colour high, everything else low. The signature of a room that has been decorated rather than corrected: repainted more than once, still not right. Leave the paint entirely alone for a month and work the other three.
  • Everything at two or three. Do not be discouraged — this is the most common result of all, and it is also the most rewarding, because the first evening’s work will be visible from the door.

The order, one last time

Light, height, texture, colour. Never colour first. Anyone who sells you the last one first is selling, not correcting.

Do it again in a month

Keep the first sheet. Re-audit the same room four weeks later and put the two side by side — it is the only honest measure of whether what you did actually worked, and it is far more reliable than how you feel about the room on any particular evening.

The first evening

You have the scores. Here is what to do tonight, in the two hours after dinner, before you have bought anything at all.

  1. Take out what does not belong. Everything in the room by accident rather than by decision, into a box, into another room. Twenty minutes.
  2. Turn the big light off and see what is left. If there is not enough, move a lamp in from another room rather than buying one. Nearly every home already owns more lamps than it uses.
  3. Read the bulbs. Write down what you need. That is tomorrow’s shopping and it will cost less than dinner.
  4. Pull the largest piece of furniture off the wall by a hand’s width. Sit down again and look.
  5. Put your hand on five things and name the missing texture family.
  6. Write one sentence in the space below: what you will do next, and when. Not a plan for the room — one sentence.

From the notebook

“Nobody ever corrected a room by reading about it. Two hours, one room, tonight — and tomorrow you will walk in and feel the difference before you can name it.”

And tomorrow

Buy the bulbs. That is the whole of tomorrow. It is the only purchase in this book that changes every room in the house at once, and it costs less than a takeaway. Everything else can wait for the weekend, and most of it can wait for the month.
