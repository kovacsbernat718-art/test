Height and Proportion

Chapter four

The cheapest correction in this book

Nothing else in decorating gives you this much for this little. A packet of picture hooks, a longer curtain pole, and an afternoon.

You met the error on page 9: almost everything that hangs in an ordinary home hangs lower than it should, because you hang things at the height where it is comfortable to hold them against the wall, and that is a good deal lower than where they needed to go.

What I want to give you in this part is the set of numbers that replaces that instinct. Not because numbers are more tasteful than your eye, but because your eye has been trained by rooms that made the same mistake, and it will keep making it until something external interrupts.

What happens when you correct the heights

  • The room gets taller. Not metaphorically — the apparent height of the wall changes, because your eye reads the topmost object as the top of the usable space and everything above it as ceiling.
  • The furniture stops looking cheap. A sofa under art hung at the right height reads as a considered arrangement. The same sofa under art hung twenty centimetres too low reads as a sofa with a picture over it.
  • The window becomes architecture. This is the largest single effect in the part, and it is what the curtain pages are about.
  • Everything you already own looks better. Nothing has been bought.

The habit that prevents every error in this part

Before you put a hole in anything, mark the height with masking tape and leave it up for a day. Walk past it. Sit down and look at it.

A word on the numbers

Everything in this part is a working figure from the trade, not a law. Where a number is a genuine standard — the gallery hanging height, for instance — I say so. Where it is what I use and what most decorators use, I say that instead. Rooms differ, and a number that fights your room is wrong for your room.

The one number to remember

One hundred and forty-five centimetres

Art hangs with its centre at 145 cm — about 57 inches — from the floor. Not the top, not the bottom, not the hook. The centre.

This is the standard hanging height used in galleries and museums, and it is not a matter of taste. It puts the middle of the work at the eye level of an average standing adult. It is a fact about where human eyes are, and it is why gallery walls feel comfortable and most domestic walls do not.

How to find the hook position

You want the centre of the picture at 145. The hook goes above that, and where exactly depends on how far the wire drops when it takes the weight.

  1. Measure the picture's height. Halve it. Call that H.
  2. Hang the wire on your finger as if it were the hook, pull it taut, and measure from there down to the top of the frame. Call that D.
  3. Hook height = 145 + H − D.

For a picture 60 cm tall whose wire pulls up to 8 cm below the top of the frame: 145 + 30 − 8 = 167 cm for the hook. Do the arithmetic on paper rather than in your head at the top of a ladder.

If your ceilings are very low or very high

Under about 2.3 m, drop to roughly 140. Above about 3 m — a Haussmannian room runs 2.80 to 3.20 — you can lift to 150 or so, and larger work will want it. But 145 is right far more often than people expect, including in tall rooms, because eyes do not get higher when ceilings do.

When the wall rule and the furniture disagree

Art hung above a sofa, a bed, a console or a fireplace stops following the room and starts following the furniture. Here is how to resolve it.

The furniture rule

Gap between the top of the furniture and the bottom of the frame 15–25 cm · 6–10 in
Width of the art, as a share of the furniture below it roughly two thirds

Those two numbers do most of the work. The gap is the important one: closer than about 15 cm and the art looks stuck to the sofa; further than about 25 and the two stop being a group and become two separate things on a wall.

When the two rules conflict

A tall sofa back plus a 15 cm gap can put the picture's centre well above 145. A low bed with a small picture can put it well below. Which wins?

The resolution

Furniture wins. A picture above furniture belongs to that furniture, and the eye reads the pair, not the wall.

Take the 145 rule as governing anything hung on open wall — a corridor, a stairwell, a blank wall, a chimney breast with nothing under it. Take the furniture rule as governing anything hung above something. Almost all the disagreement disappears once you decide which case you are in.

Above a fireplace

A third case, and the one people get wrong most. A mirror or picture over a mantel follows the mantel, not the room: leave roughly 10 to 15 cm above the shelf. And keep it narrower than the mantel — art wider than the fireplace opening below it makes the fireplace look shrunken.

The one to be careful about

A television over a fireplace is almost always too high for comfortable viewing, whatever it does for the wall. If the room's main use is watching it, that is a real cost and worth being honest with yourself about before the bracket goes in.

Pairs, rows and gallery walls

Several pictures stop being several pictures and become one object. That is the whole principle, and every rule below follows from it.

The principle

A group of pictures is a single shape. Hang the shape at 145, not each picture in it.

So for a group, the centre of the whole arrangement sits at 145. Individual frames within it will sit higher and lower, and that is correct.

The spacing

Gap between frames in a group 5–8 cm · 2–3 in
Same gap, used consistently throughout yes — this is what makes it read

Consistent spacing is what separates a gallery wall from a wall with a lot of pictures on it. Wider gaps than about 8 cm and the group dissolves into individuals; narrower than about 5 and it reads as cramped.

Three arrangements that work

  • A grid. Identical frames, identical gaps, equal rows and columns. Formal, calm, very easy to get right, and the best answer for a set of related images.
  • A shared line. Mixed sizes, but all frames aligned along one horizontal — either their tops, their bottoms, or their centres. The shared line is what holds it together.
  • A block. Mixed sizes filling a rectangle, with the outer edge of the whole arrangement forming a clean rectangle even though the interior is irregular. The hardest of the three and the most rewarding.

The paper method, which removes all the risk

Cut a piece of paper to the size of every frame. Tape them to the wall and move them around until the arrangement is right. Mark the hook position on each piece of paper while it is still on the wall, then drill through the paper. You will get it right the first time, and the only holes in your wall will be the ones you meant.

Mirrors, and what they are actually for

A mirror reflects what is in front of it. That is the entire mechanism, and almost every mirror mistake comes from forgetting it.

You met the short version on page 20. Here is the working method.

Before you hang one

Stand where the mirror will go and look at what is directly opposite. That is what you are about to put on your wall.

What is worth reflecting

  • A window. The best reflection there is. A mirror opposite or adjacent to a window puts a second bright rectangle into the room and genuinely increases the light.
  • A lamp or a lit corner. Especially in a dark room or a hallway.
  • A long view — down a corridor, through a doorway into another room. Depth is what a mirror is best at.
  • Something you like looking at. A bookcase, a plant, a good painting.

What is not

A blank wall. The back of a door. A radiator. The kitchen bin. A television, which is a black rectangle for most of the day and a distraction for the rest. And — the one people forget — the ceiling, which is what a mirror hung too high above a fireplace usually shows you.

Heights

Decorative mirror on open wall — centre 145 cm · 57 in
Over a mantel — above the shelf 10–15 cm · 4–6 in
Over a basin or console — above the surface 15–20 cm · 6–8 in
A full-length mirror — lean it, or fix it top at 175–185 cm

Two practical points

A large mirror is heavy and heavier than it looks. Fix it into something solid, not into plasterboard alone, and use the fixings rated for the weight — this is one of the few genuinely dangerous things in this book.

And a leaning full-length mirror must be secured to the wall behind it. They tip.

Shelves, and the rail you already own

If your home has a picture rail, you have a hanging system already installed and you are almost certainly not using it.

A picture rail is the moulded strip running round a room at or near the head height of the doors. It exists because plaster walls were once difficult to fix into, so pictures hung from cords or chains dropped from the rail. In older buildings it is still there, painted over, ignored.

Why it is worth using

  • No holes. Which makes it the single most useful thing in a rented flat with period features — and it is the first thing I look for in one.
  • Everything is adjustable. Move a picture in thirty seconds, with no filler.
  • The drop itself reads well. Two visible cords running down to a frame is a handsome, deliberate detail, not a compromise.

Hooks that slip over the rail cost very little. Use two cords rather than one — a single cord makes a triangle and the picture will swing; two parallel drops hang straight and stay put. And the 145 rule still applies: adjust the cord length so the picture's centre lands there, rather than hanging everything from the rail at the same drop.

Shelves

Lowest shelf of an open unit, above the floor at least 25–30 cm
Highest shelf you will use daily no higher than 180 cm
Between shelves, for ordinary books 28–32 cm
A single floating shelf, above a sofa or desk treat as art — page 88

The thing about bookcases

A bookcase that stops two-thirds of the way up a wall leaves an awkward band of empty wall above it and makes the ceiling look lower. A bookcase that runs to the ceiling — or has something on top that closes the gap — reads as architecture. If you are buying one, measure the wall first and buy the tallest that fits.

The largest prize in this part

The curtain pole

Moving a curtain pole upward and outward changes a room more than any other twenty-minute job in this book. Most people have never considered that it could move at all.

The default installation puts the pole a few centimetres above the window frame and barely wider than it. Both are wrong, and both are wrong in the same direction.

Pole height — above the top of the window frame 15–20 cm · 6–8 in
Or, better, below the ceiling or cornice 8–15 cm · 3–6 in
Pole width — beyond the frame, each side 15–25 cm · 6–10 in

What each one buys you

Height makes the window taller. The eye reads the whole curtained area as the window, so a pole near the ceiling produces an apparently floor-to-ceiling opening rather than a hole in a wall with fabric above it.

Width gives you back your daylight. Open curtains stack onto the wall instead of onto the glass — which, as page 41 said, is typically a tenth of your window returned to you permanently.

The drop, and the four inches that matter

A curtain goes to the floor. Almost the only exception is a radiator, and even that one is arguable.

A curtain that stops short — at the sill, or a hand's width above the floor — draws a horizontal line partway down the wall, and the eye reads that line as the bottom of the room. The ceiling comes down with it. This is the same mechanism as the pole height working in reverse, and it is the most common single error in the whole of window dressing.

Colette’s rule of the dropped curtain

A curtain that stops above the floor makes the ceiling lower. Hung close to the ceiling and dropped to the floor, it makes the window taller than it is.

The three finishes at the floor

FinishClearanceCharacter
Floating1 cm aboveCrisp, practical, moves freely. The safe default, and best where a curtain is drawn daily.
Kissingjust touchingThe most elegant and the hardest to achieve, because floors are rarely level.
Breaking2–5 cm on the floorSoft, slightly languid, very forgiving of an uneven floor. My preference in a sitting room.

A deeper puddle than about 5 cm collects dust, trips people and needs arranging every time it is moved. It photographs beautifully and lives badly.

The radiator question

A full-length curtain in front of a radiator traps the heat behind it and sends it up the window. Three honest options: stop the curtain at the sill and accept the proportional cost; hang full-length curtains that you draw only when the heating is off; or fit a blind or shutter for warmth and hang the curtains as decorative panels that never close. The third is what I do most often, and it keeps the proportions.

Measure in three places

Floors are not level, especially in old buildings. Measure the drop at the left, the centre and the right, and work to the shortest. A curtain cut to the longest measurement will drag at one end.

Fullness — how much fabric

This is the number that decides whether a curtain looks expensive, and it has nothing to do with what the fabric cost.

Fullness is the ratio between the total width of fabric and the width of the pole it hangs on. A curtain with little fullness hangs flat and reads as a sheet. A curtain with generous fullness falls in deep, even folds and reads as a curtain.

Bare minimum, will look thin 1.5 × pole width
Standard, and what most ready-made curtains give you 2 ×
Generous — what makes the difference 2.5 ×
For sheers and very light fabric 2.5–3 ×

Why this is the honest place to spend money

Between a cheap fabric at 2.5 times fullness and an expensive fabric at 1.5, the cheap one looks better every time. Fullness is visible from across the room; fibre content is not. If your budget is fixed, buy more of a plainer cloth rather than less of a finer one.

From the notebook

My grandmother would not make a curtain under two and a half times. If a client could not afford the fabric she would suggest a cheaper cloth rather than less of it, and she would explain why. Forty-one years and she never once lost that argument, because she would hold the two up side by side and let the client see it.

Working out what to buy

  1. Measure the pole, not the window.
  2. Multiply by your fullness — use 2.5 unless there is a reason not to.
  3. Divide by the fabric width, and round up to whole widths. This gives the number of fabric widths.
  4. Multiply the number of widths by the drop, plus an allowance for hems and heading, plus a pattern repeat if the fabric has one.

The worksheet on page 96 does this arithmetic for you, with space for the measurements. If you are having curtains made, take it with you.

Headings, and what each one costs you

The heading is how the curtain attaches to the pole. It decides how the folds fall, how much of the pole you see, and how much fabric you need.

The practical hierarchy

If the curtain is decorative and never drawn, any of them will do and you should choose on looks. If it is drawn every evening, pinch pleat or wave will run smoothly and stay handsome for years; tab top will annoy you within a month.

Lining, which matters more than the heading

A lined curtain hangs heavier, falls in better folds, blocks more light, insulates the window and lasts far longer, because the lining takes the sun damage instead of the face fabric. An unlined curtain is a panel of cloth. If you are choosing between a better fabric unlined and a plainer fabric lined, take the lining every time. This is one of the four things in this book I will tell you to spend on.

Interlining — a soft layer between face and lining — is a further step again, and it is what gives the heavy, quiet, expensive drape you see in old houses. It roughly doubles the making cost and it is genuinely transformative on a tall window.

The most expensive common mistake

The rug is too small

Nearly every rug in nearly every home is one size too small, and a rug is one of the more expensive things in a room. It is worth getting right once.

It happens for an understandable reason. A rug is bought in a shop, where it is hanging on a rail or stacked in a pile, and at that moment it looks enormous. It arrives home, goes down on the floor, and becomes a mat floating in the middle of the room with all the furniture standing around it on bare boards.

What a rug is actually doing

A rug is not floor covering. It is a boundary. It draws the edge of a zone and tells your eye that everything standing on it belongs together. That is why a too-small rug is so damaging: instead of grouping the furniture, it puts an object in the middle of the group and pushes everything outward.

The test

If the furniture is standing around the rug rather than on it, the rug is too small. It is doing the opposite of its job.

The three legitimate arrangements

  1. All legs on. Every piece of seating sits entirely on the rug, with a margin beyond. The most generous and the most expensive, and unbeatable in a large room.
  2. Front legs on. The front legs of every sofa and chair sit on the rug, the back legs on the floor. The standard answer for most sitting rooms, and it works because the rug still reaches under and connects everything.
  3. Deliberately floating — but sized to the furniture. A rug entirely inside the seating arrangement, but wide enough that it visibly relates to the sofa rather than looking dropped there. It should be at least as wide as the sofa.

What is not on that list is the common accident: a small rectangle under a coffee table only, touching nothing else in the room.

The margin

Leave a border of bare floor between the rug and the walls, and keep it roughly even — somewhere between 20 and 45 cm in a normal room. A rug that runs almost to the skirting reads as failed fitted carpet.

The dining rule is the strict one

Sixty to seventy-five centimetres beyond the table on every side is not aesthetic advice. A chair whose back legs drop off the edge of a rug when someone sits down catches, tips, and damages the rug. If you cannot fit the margin, do not put a rug under a dining table.

Before buying, mark the size out on the floor with masking tape and live with it for two days. The same habit as page 86, and it costs nothing.

The heights that have to agree

Furniture is bought one piece at a time, over years, and nobody measures. Then the pieces stand next to each other and quietly disagree.

These are the relationships that matter. Not absolute heights — relationships. A coffee table is not too tall in the abstract; it is too tall for that sofa.

The three that cause the most complaints

  • The coffee table that is too tall. Modern sofas have got lower and coffee tables have not. A table well above the seat height means you reach down and then up for a cup, and it blocks the view across the room.
  • The side table that is too low. If it sits well below the sofa arm you cannot put a glass down without leaning, and a lamp on it will be at the wrong height — page 30.
  • The walkway that is too narrow. Under about 75 cm people turn sideways without noticing they are doing it, and the room feels cramped for a reason nobody can name.

Before you buy anything

Take three measurements with you: your sofa seat height, your sofa arm height, and the narrowest walkway in the room. Almost every furniture-buying mistake is prevented by those three numbers, and no showroom will give them to you.

Pull it off the wall

The instinct in a small room is to push every piece of furniture flat against a wall to create space in the middle. It produces the opposite of space.

You met this as myth three on page 19. Here is what to do about it.

A room with all its furniture against the walls has a void in the centre, and a void does not read as generous — it reads as a waiting area. Nothing is happening in the middle of the room, and the middle of the room is what you see first.

The float

Pull a sofa off the wall by 5–15 cm · 2–6 in
Which costs you, in usable floor nothing you were standing on

Five to fifteen centimetres is enough. It is not about creating a walkway behind the sofa — it is that the shadow gap between the furniture and the wall tells your eye the piece is a free-standing object in a room, rather than built into its edge. The room acquires an inside.

Three more moves in the same family

  • Turn one chair off-axis. A single armchair at an angle to the grid breaks the sense that everything has been pushed to the perimeter. One is enough; two looks accidental.
  • Let something stand in front of a window. Not blocking the lower third — page 41 — but a low chair or a small table in front of glass is normal in French apartments and reads as confident. A window is not an altar.
  • Put the sofa across the room rather than along it, where the proportions allow. A sofa that divides a space defines two zones; a sofa against the long wall defines none.

The principle underneath all four

Furniture pressed flat to the walls describes the edges of a room. Furniture pulled inward describes the room itself.

Doors, thresholds and the vertical line

A room has a horizontal rhythm that most people never notice, and once you see it you cannot stop.

Stand in a doorway and look along a wall. There is a line running round the room at the head height of the doors — the top of the door frame, the top of the window frame, the picture rail if there is one, often the top of a fitted cupboard. In a well-proportioned room these align, or nearly do, and the room feels settled without anybody being able to say why.

In most homes something breaks that line: a shelf at an odd height, a wall cupboard, a boxed-in pipe, a television bracket. The line is what your eye was using to organise the wall, and the break is read as disorder.

How to use it

  • Align the tops of things, not the bottoms. Two bookcases of different heights look like an error; two units whose tops align look like a decision, even when they are different widths.
  • Put nothing new above the door line unless it goes all the way up. A shelf floating just above door height is the single most disruptive thing you can add to a wall. Either keep below the line, or run to the ceiling.
  • Use the line for the curtain pole where you can. If the pole lands close to the door head, everything in the room agrees at once.

The threshold

Where one room meets another, the floor either continues or it changes. A change of flooring at every doorway chops a small flat into a series of cupboards, which is the law on page 70. If you cannot change the floors, you can at least keep the colour continuous across the threshold and let one run of skirting carry through.

The doorway view

Every doorway frames a view. Stand in each one in your home and look at what it frames. In most flats at least one doorway is aimed squarely at the side of a wardrobe or the back of a television. Moving one piece of furniture so that a doorway frames something worth seeing is free and it is the sort of thing decorators are paid for.

The one I am asked about most

Every room needs one empty corner

I have been promising this page since Part One. It is the shortest rule in the book and the one people find hardest to obey.

Colette’s rule of the empty corner

Every room needs one corner where nothing happens.

One corner with no furniture, no lamp, no plant, no stack of anything. Ideally unlit, or lit only by whatever spills into it. Genuinely empty.

Why

Because a room in which every corner is doing something has nowhere for the eye to rest. Every direction you look, there is an object asking to be considered. It is tiring in a way that is difficult to identify, because nothing in the room is wrong — there is simply no pause in it.

The empty corner is the pause. It is also what makes everything else in the room read as chosen: when three corners are occupied and one is not, the occupied ones look deliberate. When all four are occupied, they look like the places things ended up.

This is also why I told you to leave one corner dark

Page 32 said to get light into every corner you can, except one. Same rule, seen from the lighting side. A room lit evenly into all four corners is a lit box; a room with three lit corners and one in shadow has depth, because the eye has something to read the light against.

What counts as empty

Bare floor and bare wall meeting. A single tall plant in an otherwise unused corner is a borderline case and I would allow it — it is one object, it moves, and it does not ask to be considered. A chair with a coat on it, a drying rack, a stack of boxes, a floor lamp and a side table: not empty.

From the notebook

My grandmother called it le coin qui respire — the corner that breathes. She said you could tell how a household was doing by whether it still had one.

And the six that do not need their own page
