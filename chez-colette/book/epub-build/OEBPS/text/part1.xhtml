Part One

How to Read a Room

Before you change anything, you have to be able to see what is wrong. This part teaches you the four errors that ruin almost every ordinary room, the three questions that find them, and the order in which corrections have to be made if they are not going to waste your money.

Chapter one

The room is not the problem

You have already tried. That is the part nobody credits you for.

You have moved the sofa. You have moved it back. You have bought cushions, and then different cushions. At some point you painted a wall, and it looked better for about a week, and then it went back to looking the way it looked before. You have a folder of saved photographs of rooms you like, and not one of them looks like yours, and you cannot say why.

So you have quietly arrived at the conclusion that the problem is you. That other people have an eye and you do not. That your room is simply an ordinary room and this is what an ordinary room looks like.

I have been in several hundred of these rooms. That is not what is happening.

What is happening is that you are being asked to solve a problem you have not been given the vocabulary for. Nobody taught you to look at a room the way a decorator looks at a room, which is not as a collection of objects but as a set of relationships — between the light and the wall, between the height of a thing and the height of your eye, between one material and the next. Without that vocabulary there is only one move available to you, and that move is to buy something. So you buy something, and it changes the objects in the room without changing any of the relationships, and the room looks the same.

And every single part of this industry is content for that to continue.

What we are actually doing

Almost no ordinary room is short of objects. Nearly all of them are suffering three or four specific, nameable errors.

Three or four. Not thirty. That is the good news buried in this, and it is the reason this book is possible at all. The errors repeat. They are the same errors, in flat after flat, in one country after another, and once you know their names you will see them in every room you walk into for the rest of your life — including on television, including in the photographs you saved.

Most of them cost nothing to correct. Some of them take twenty minutes. One or two are worth real money, and when we get to those I will tell you plainly.

The four errors

Here they are. Every one of them gets its own page after this, and every one of them gets a whole part of the book later on. For now, just learn the four names.

One · The room is lit from one height

There is a light in the middle of the ceiling and that is all there is. Every object in the room is lit from the same angle, so nothing has any depth, and everyone in the room looks tired.

Two · Everything is hung too low

The pictures, the curtain pole, the mirror, the pendant. All of it sitting lower than it should, which drags the whole room down towards the floor and makes the ceiling feel closer than it is.

Three · One texture, repeated

Everything in the room is the same kind of surface. Four soft things, or four shiny things, or four flat painted things. The room reads as a showroom, or as a waiting area, and you cannot say why because nothing in it is ugly.

Four · Nobody has ever looked at the ceiling

It is the largest surface in the room. No furniture will ever hide any part of it. It is the last thing you see at night and the first thing you see in the morning. And you painted it white without once asking whether that was the right answer.

Error one

The room is lit from one height

Go and stand in your sitting room tonight with the lights on as you normally have them. Now look at the ceiling, and count the sources.

In most rooms the answer is one. There is a fitting in the middle of the ceiling, it is doing all of the work, and everything you own is being lit from directly above.

Here is why that single fact is doing so much damage.

Light from directly overhead falls on the top of everything and nothing else. The top of the sofa arm is bright, the front of it is not. The top of your head is bright and your eye sockets are in shadow, which is why everyone in a room lit this way looks exhausted. Nothing has a lit side and a shaded side, so nothing has any apparent depth — and a room without depth reads flat whatever you paid for the furniture in it.

And because that one fitting lights the whole room from one point, it has to be bright. So the brightness is the thing you notice, and the room feels like a place where tasks are performed rather than a place where anyone sits.

Colette’s rule of the three heights

Light must arrive at three heights — overhead, table, and floor. A room lit from one height is a room lit like a corridor.

This is my rule rather than a law of physics, and Part Two is entirely about it. But the correction is so cheap that I want you to have it now.

The twenty-minute version

Tonight, turn the ceiling light off. Put two lamps on in the room instead — any two lamps you already own, one at roughly the height of a side table and one lower or taller than that — and put them in two different corners rather than side by side. Then sit down in the chair you actually sit in and look at the room.

Nothing bought, nothing moved more than a metre. And every object in that room now has a lit side and a shaded side, which is the whole difference between a room and a corridor.

If you only have one lamp

Use it, and turn the ceiling light off anyway. One lamp and a dark corner beats one ceiling fitting and none — page 102.

Error two

Everything is hung too low

This is the most common error in this book and the cheapest one to correct, which is a combination you will not meet often.

Almost everything that hangs in an ordinary home hangs lower than it should. Pictures, mirrors, curtain poles, pendant lights, shelves, coat hooks. It happens for a reason that is completely understandable: you hang a thing at the height where it is comfortable to hold it up against the wall. That is roughly the height of your own hands at rest, and your hands at rest are a good deal lower than where the object needed to go.

Repeat that across nine or ten objects and the effect is cumulative. The whole visual weight of the room collects in a band about a metre off the floor, everything above that band is empty, and the empty part reads as unfinished while the crowded part reads as cluttered. Both at once, in the same room.

The two numbers that fix most of it

A picture hangs with its centre at 145 cm · 57 in
A curtain pole goes as close to the ceiling as it can, and the curtain drops to the floor 0–1 cm clearance

One hundred and forty-five centimetres to the centre of the picture — not to the top, not to the bottom, and not to the hook. This is the standard hanging height used in galleries and museums, and the reason it works is that it puts the middle of the work at the eye level of an average standing adult. It is not a matter of taste. It is a matter of where human eyes are.

Go and measure one picture in your home right now. If the centre of it is at 120 or 125 centimetres, which it probably is, you have found one of your three or four errors, and the fix costs one nail hole and four minutes.

One honest exception

Art hung above a sofa or a console follows the furniture, not the room — leave roughly 15 to 25 cm (6 to 10 in) between the top of the furniture and the bottom of the frame, and let that decide the height. Page 88 covers the whole business of hanging over furniture, including what to do when the two rules disagree.

Curtains are the larger prize and they get their own chapter, because moving a curtain pole upward by twenty centimetres does something to a room that is genuinely difficult to believe until you have seen it. The window appears taller. The ceiling appears higher. The wall stops looking like a wall with a hole in it.

Error three

One texture, repeated

This is the error that makes a room full of nice things feel like a hotel lobby.

Stand in the doorway and do not look at colour or at shape. Look only at surface. What is each thing in this room actually made of, and what would it feel like under your hand?

In a great many rooms the honest answer comes back as one material wearing four costumes. A grey fabric sofa, a grey fabric armchair, a grey fabric ottoman, and a grey fabric curtain: four soft things. Or: a glass table, a gloss-painted unit, a mirror, and a chrome lamp — four hard shiny things. Nothing in either room is ugly. Nothing is even badly chosen. And the room is lifeless, because a surface only reads as interesting next to a surface that is not like it.

A hand runs over linen and then over oak and then over unglazed clay and registers three completely different things. A hand that runs over four woven surfaces registers one thing, four times. Your eye does exactly the same arithmetic, faster, and without telling you.

Colette’s rule of the fourth texture

A room reads as finished when four textures meet: something woven, something wooden, something ceramic or stone, and something living. Three is a showroom. Four is a home.

This is mine, and it is a rule of thumb rather than a truth — but I have used it for fourteen years and it has never once let me down in a room that was not working. Count the families. If you can only find two, you have found another of your errors, and it is almost always the cheapest one in the book to fix, because the missing family is usually clay or wood and both of those can be answered from a charity shop for the price of a coffee.

Error four

Nobody has ever looked at the ceiling

In French we have a name for the ceiling that English does not have. We call it le cinquième mur — the fifth wall.

I did not understand what a good name that was until I started working in other people’s homes and noticed that the ceiling was the only surface in the house that nobody had ever made a decision about. Every other surface had been considered. Somebody had chosen the floor. Somebody had chosen, or at least argued about, the colour of the walls. The ceiling was white because ceilings are white.

Consider what that surface actually is. It is the largest single uninterrupted plane in the room — larger than any wall, because no window or door is cut out of it. It is the only surface that no piece of furniture will ever stand in front of, which means you see all of it, all of the time. And it is the last thing you look at before you go to sleep and the first thing you look at when you wake up.

One coat of paint. Nobody sells it to you, because there is nothing in it for anyone.

The law of the fifth wall

The ceiling is the largest surface in the room, the only one no furniture will ever hide, and the only one you painted white without once asking why.

What the ceiling is doing to your room right now

A pale ceiling appears to recede. A darker ceiling appears to come closer. That is a real perceptual effect and you can use it in either direction, which is the part almost nobody is told: a low ceiling is not automatically improved by white paint, and a very high ceiling in a small room is often improved by a darker one, because it brings the room down to a human scale and makes it feel occupied rather than echoing.

The choice depends on what the room is for and what is wrong with it, and Part Three gives you the whole decision. What I want from you here is smaller. I want you to go into the room you like least in your home, lie on the floor, and look up for thirty seconds.

Most people discover two things. The ceiling is not actually white any more — it has gone cream, or grey, or yellow near the light fitting. And there is more of it than they remembered.

How to look

The three questions

You now know the four errors by name. This is how you find which of them are in your room.

Do not do this from the doorway. Sit down in the chair you actually use, at the time of day you actually use the room, with the lights as you actually have them. A room that is being inspected is not the room you live in.

  1. Where is the light coming from, and how many places is it coming from? Count the sources that are switched on. Then look at one solid object — a jug, a lamp base, the arm of a chair — and find its shadow. If it does not have one, the room has a single source and you have error one.
  2. Where is the highest thing on the wall, and where is my eye? Sit still and look straight ahead without tilting your head. Note where your gaze lands on the far wall. Now look at the top edge of the highest picture, mirror or shelf. If everything on that wall sits below where you were already looking, you have error two.
  3. How many different materials can I count, and how many families do they belong to? Not how many objects — how many kinds of surface. Woven, wooden, ceramic or stone, living. Fewer than three families is error three.

Then, last, look up. If you cannot remember making a decision about that surface, you have error four, and you have almost certainly got it, because almost everyone has.

What you are likely to find

Three of the four. That is the ordinary result and it is not a disaster — it is why the room has been resisting you. Four separate problems, none of which is fixable by buying a cushion, and nobody ever told you their names.

Do not fix anything yet

Write down what you found and put the pencil down. The order in which these get corrected matters a great deal, and doing them in the wrong order is how people spend money twice. That is the subject of page 15 and it is the most expensive page in this book to ignore.

Why your room photographs badly when it looks fine to you

Nearly everyone has had this experience, and almost nobody knows what it means.

You stand in the room and it is perfectly pleasant. You take a photograph of it to send to somebody and the photograph is dreadful. The proportions look wrong, the colours look muddy, the whole thing looks cheaper than it does in life. So you conclude that you are bad at photography.

You are not. The camera is telling you the truth and your eye has been protecting you from it.

Your eye is not a camera. It moves constantly, it adjusts its exposure locally as it moves, and — this is the important part — your brain edits out what it already knows is there. You have not properly seen the cable behind the television for two years. Your visual system has decided it is not information and it stopped sending it to you. A camera has no such filter. It flattens the room to one plane, fixes one exposure for the whole frame, and reports back every single thing in it.

So the photograph is a free diagnosis. Use it that way.

How to use it

Photograph the room, then look at the photograph rather than the room. The first three things that annoy you in the picture are the three errors you have stopped being able to see.

Two refinements that make it sharper

  • Turn the photograph upside down. This is an old drawing-class trick and it works for exactly the same reason: inverted, the image stops being a room you recognise and becomes a pattern of shapes and tones. Errors of proportion and balance leap out. It is startling how well this works on a wall of pictures.
  • Turn it black and white. With colour removed, what is left is the distribution of light and dark, and light and dark is what gives a room depth. A photograph that goes to uniform mid-grey is a photograph of a room lit from one height.

Keep those photographs. When you have finished correcting the room, take the same photograph from the same spot at the same hour. It is the only honest before-and-after there is, and it will show you things you did not know you had fixed.

The most expensive page to ignore

The order of operations

Corrections have to be made in a particular order, because each one changes the answer to the next.

This is the page that saves you money. Not because anything on it is difficult, but because the natural order — the order in which people actually do it — is almost exactly backwards, and doing it backwards means paying twice.

Here is the order. It goes from the thing that nothing depends on, to the thing that depends on everything.

  1. Clear. Take out of the room everything that is broken, everything you do not like, and everything that is there because it has always been there. Do not replace any of it yet. An emptier room is easier to read, and a surprising number of rooms are substantially fixed at this stage. It costs nothing.
  2. Light. Lamps, bulbs, heights, and what you can stop blocking. This comes before colour and it is not negotiable, for the reason on the next page. It is usually the cheapest real change in the room and almost always the largest.
  3. Height. Curtain pole up, pictures to 145, shelves and mirrors adjusted. Nail holes and an afternoon. Do this before you paint, because you will be filling holes anyway and because the new heights change where the eye goes.
  4. Texture. The missing families. Fabric, wood, clay, something living. Usually one object, usually cheap, and it is what makes a corrected room feel lived in rather than merely tidy.
  5. Colour. Walls, ceiling, woodwork. Last, always — and only now, when you know what light the room will actually be living in and what else is already in it. By the time you arrive here, a surprising number of rooms no longer need painting at all.

The expensive mistake

Buying furniture at any point in this list. Furniture is not a stage. It comes after all five, if it comes at all, and most of the rooms I have corrected needed none.

Why colour comes fourth and not first

Everybody starts with paint. It is the wrong place to start, and here is the reason in one sentence: a colour is not a property of the paint. It is what happens when that paint meets the light in your room.

Change the light and you have changed the colour. Not your perception of it — the actual wavelengths coming off that wall into your eye. The same tin of paint on a north-facing wall and a south-facing wall is two different colours, and anybody who has painted both will tell you so.

So when you choose a colour before you have settled the light, you are answering a question whose terms have not been set yet. Then you fix the lighting afterwards, and the colour you chose turns out to be wrong, and you paint again. I have watched people do this three times in the same room.

The two things that decide a colour before you have chosen it

  • Which way the window faces. North light in the northern hemisphere never carries direct sun; it is cool and steady all day, and it will push a warm grey towards blue. South light is warm and strong and will push a cool white towards yellow. This is the first page of Part Two and it is the single most useful fact in the book.
  • What your bulbs are. A bulb has a colour temperature printed on the box and most people have never looked at it. Twenty-seven hundred kelvin is warm domestic light; four to five thousand is the light shops and offices are lit with. Put a colour chosen under one of those under the other and it is unrecognisable.

From the notebook

“You are not choosing a colour. You are choosing what the light will do to it.” — my father was a house painter and he mixed his own colours, and this is the only thing he ever said about paint that I have never had cause to revise.

There is one more reason to leave paint until fourth, and it is the practical one. Paint is the most disruptive and least reversible thing on the list. You have to empty the room, cover the floor, and lose two days. Lamps take twenty minutes and can be undone. Curtain poles take an afternoon. It is simply better sense to exhaust the cheap reversible moves first — and a great many rooms never need the paint at all once the first three stages are done.

What is deliberately not on the list

No colour fan deck — you will be given the six whites that matter and the reason each one fails, and a fan of two thousand chips makes the decision harder rather than easier. No laser measure; a tape is more accurate at room distances and never needs a battery. No design software. No mood-board app. No subscription to anything.

The one thing worth borrowing rather than buying

A stepladder. You will need one for perhaps two afternoons in the whole of this book, and then it will live in a cupboard for a decade. Ask a neighbour. It is also how you find out whether your neighbour has a drill.

And the masking tape, because I mean it

Before you put a hole in anything, cut a piece of tape the width of the object and stick it on the wall at the height you are considering. Leave it there for a day. Walk past it. Sit down and look at it. Almost every hanging error in this book would be prevented by that one habit, and the tape costs less than the filler you would otherwise need.

What this book will not do

I would rather tell you now than have you find out two hundred pages from here.

It will not make a small flat into a large one

There are real techniques in here for making a small room read as larger and more deliberate, and they work. None of them adds a square metre. If your flat is too small for your life, that is a different problem and no book solves it.

It will not help you with damp, mould, lead or asbestos

These come up in old buildings and they are not decorating problems. Damp has a cause and until the cause is found nothing you put on that wall will stay there. Paint in buildings from before the late 1970s may contain lead, and disturbing it by sanding is the dangerous part. Some materials in older buildings contain asbestos and must not be drilled or cut. If you suspect any of these, the correct next step is a surveyor or a licensed specialist, not a paintbrush. I will say so again each time it comes up.

It will not do electrical, gas, plumbing or structural work

Nothing in this book asks you to. Where a correction would — moving a ceiling fitting, adding a socket, taking out anything that holds the building up — you are told to stop and call somebody licensed. There is more good work available to you without touching any of that than you will get through in a year.

It cannot get you permission

If you rent, some of this is not available to you, and Part Six is written entirely for that situation — every correction in it needs no drill, no paint and nobody’s consent. If your building is listed or protected, check before you change a window, a door or anything on the outside.

It is not against spending money

This is the one people get wrong about me. I am against wasted money, which is a completely different position. There are things in this book I will tell you to spend on, because the cheap version is a false economy and you will buy it twice:

  • A good lamp. It will outlive the room.
  • Lined curtains, in enough fabric. The single largest visible difference money makes.
  • A sofa whose cover can be taken off, or which can be reupholstered.
  • Decent bulbs, which cost very little and change everything.

Everything else in the ordinary decorating budget is negotiable, and a good deal of it is listed on page 189 under the heading of what not to buy.

Six things you have been told

That are wrong — part one

One · “Paint a small room white to make it look bigger”

This is the most repeated piece of advice in the subject and it is the one I disagree with most sharply. A small room painted flat white does not read as bigger. It reads as small and unfinished, because white shows you exactly where the walls are and there is nothing else for the eye to do. Meanwhile every mark, every radiator, every cable is on full display against it.

A small room painted a deep, quiet colour reads as deliberate. The corners soften, the walls stop announcing their distance, and the room feels like a decision rather than a shortage. This is my position and not a physical law, so I have marked it as mine — but Part Three gives you the whole argument and the cases where white genuinely is the right answer.

Two · “Get the biggest light you can for a dark room”

A dark room is rarely short of lumens. It is short of directions. One very bright ceiling fitting in a dark room produces a bright ceiling and a dim room, because all it can do is illuminate the plane it is attached to and the tops of things. Three modest lamps at three heights will make that room feel twice as bright as one powerful pendant, using less electricity.

Three · “Push the furniture against the walls to make space”

In a small room this is often exactly wrong. Furniture flat against every wall leaves a void in the middle, and a void does not read as space — it reads as a waiting room. Pulling a sofa five or ten centimetres off the wall, and letting one chair sit at an angle, gives the room an inside. The floor you lose is floor nobody was standing on.

Why these persist

Not one of the three is a conspiracy. They are all true of showrooms, which are lit from above, painted pale so the stock reads, and arranged around a central aisle. Advice written for a shop floor got repeated until it sounded like advice for a home.

That are wrong — part two

Four · “An accent wall will transform the room”

One wall in a different colour does not transform a room. It divides it. The eye reads the coloured wall as nearer than the others, so a room with one dark wall and three pale ones feels shorter in that direction, not deeper. If the wall you chose is the one with the window in it, you have also just put your only source of light in front of your darkest surface, which is the least useful arrangement available.

There is a version of this that works, and it is not an accent wall — it is taking one colour all the way round the room and onto the ceiling, so the room reads as a single volume. Page 62.

Five · “Mirrors double the light”

A mirror reflects what is in front of it. That is the whole mechanism. A mirror opposite a window reflects the window and genuinely helps, because it puts a second bright rectangle into the room. A mirror opposite a blank wall reflects a blank wall, adds nothing, and has cost you the wall it is hanging on. Before you hang a mirror, stand where it will go and look at what you would be looking at.

Six · “You need a feature, a focal point, a statement piece”

A room needs somewhere for the eye to rest, which is not the same thing and is usually cheaper. A window with the curtains hung properly is a focal point. A fireplace is a focal point. A bookcase with actual books in it is a focal point. The word statement is doing commercial work in that sentence, and the thing being stated is usually a price.

Carry this one forward

Almost every piece of decorating advice you have absorbed was written to sell something. Ask what each rule would have you buy, and a surprising number of them dissolve.

That is the end of Part One. You know the four errors, you know how to find them, and you know the order in which to correct them. Everything after this is detail, and the detail starts where it has to start.
