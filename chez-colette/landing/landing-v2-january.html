A room is not decorated. A room is corrected.

I left the trade because it only knows one verb. Buy. A client would ask me why her sitting room felt wrong, and the profession had trained me to answer with a shopping list.

What is actually wrong

The four errors

I have stood in several hundred ordinary rooms. Almost none of them were short of objects. Nearly all of them were suffering the same small set of nameable errors, and most cost nothing at all to correct.

1

The room is lit from one height

One light in the middle of the ceiling, and that is all there is. Every object is lit from the same angle, so nothing has any depth, and everyone in the room looks tired.

2

Everything is hung too low

Pictures, curtain pole, mirror, pendant. All of it sitting lower than it should, dragging the room towards the floor and making the ceiling feel closer than it is.

3

One texture, repeated

The error that makes a room full of perfectly nice things feel like a hotel lobby. Stand in the doorway and look at nothing but surface.

4

Nobody has ever looked at the ceiling

In French we have a name for it that English does not: le cinquième mur, the fifth wall. It is the one surface in most homes that no one has ever made a decision about.

Most people find three of the four. That is the ordinary result, and it is not a disaster. It is why the room has been resisting you.

The author

A word from Colette

I grew up in a building that was never meant for people. It was built for looms.

The Croix-Rousse is the hill above Lyon where the silk weavers worked. Their looms stood nearly four metres tall, so the buildings went up with four-metre ceilings and enormous north windows, because north light does not move, and does not glare, and you can match a thread by it all day. The weavers are long gone. Ordinary families live in their workrooms now, and mine was one of them. So I was raised being told which windows were the good ones.

My grandmother made curtains. Not as a hobby: as a trade, for forty-one years, in other people’s apartments all over that city. She kept a cloth-bound notebook of every window she ever measured: the drop, the width, the fullness, the colour she had mixed to go with it, and the name of the family. Near the end of her life she made me promise I would write it all down properly before it was lost.

This is me keeping that promise, with fourteen years of my own work added to hers. Those fourteen years were not spent in beautiful houses. They were spent in small flats, rented flats, ground-floor rooms that never saw the sun.

Your taste was never the problem. Nobody ever showed you what to look at.

The most expensive page to ignore

Corrections have an order

Each one changes the answer to the next. The natural order, the order people actually use, is almost exactly backwards, and doing it backwards means paying twice.

Usually the largest changeis also the cheapest

A colour is not a property of the paint. It is what happens when paint meets the light in your room, which is why colour comes last and not first. Where a price is mentioned anywhere in this book it is an order of magnitude, not a quotation.

Questions, answered plainly

Is a paper book coming in the post?

No, and I say it plainly so nobody is surprised. This is a digital book: a PDF of 204 pages, and an EPUB for phones and e-readers. Nothing ships. It is on your phone about thirty seconds after you pay, and the link in your email opens it again any time you like. If you want paper, you print it yourself. The audit page and the measurement tables are made for exactly that.

Do I have to buy things to use this book?

The opposite. That is the whole argument. Most of the corrections in here cost nothing and some of them take twenty minutes. One or two are worth real money, and when we get to those I tell you plainly which they are. There is an entire reference section at the back called What Not to Buy.

I rent. Can I actually change anything?

Yes, and Part Six is devoted entirely to it: the corrections that need no permission and leave no mark. Much of my fourteen years was spent in small flats, rented flats, ground-floor rooms that never saw the sun, and apartments a landlord had ruined with the cheapest white paint and the wrong lights. That is the work I know. Read your tenancy agreement before you make a permanent change to anything.

Is this just full of photographs of beautiful houses?

No. This book is drawn, not photographed. Every diagram is a line drawing made for the page. A photograph of somebody else’s beautiful room teaches you nothing about your own, and if you are looking for a book about châteaux, this is not it.

Metric or imperial?

Both, every time a measurement matters. The book was written in centimetres, because that is how I measure, and every figure carries its inches and feet beside it.
