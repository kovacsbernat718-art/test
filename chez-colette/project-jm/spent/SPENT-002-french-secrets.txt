Go and look at the chair in your home that nobody sits in. There's a throw folded over the arm that has never been unfolded, and by Thursday there will be a coat on it. Now walk round behind it. A doorway. A hallway. The route to the kitchen. That chair is the first of five reasons your home looks perfectly nice and never quite feels good. Not one of them is fixed by buying anything.

And at some point you quietly concluded that some people simply have a gift for homes, and you were not one of them.

A French decree of two thousand and two defines a decent rented home: one main room of at least nine square metres, just under a hundred square feet, with a ceiling of two point two metres, about seven feet two, or else twenty cubic metres of space, about seven hundred cubic feet. It can measure a room to the centimetre. It cannot tell you whether anyone will want to sit in it.

A room is not only looked at. It is sat in and listened to, and a photograph shows you neither. You were not careless, ma chère, and you were not tasteless. You were handed pictures and never shown what to look for.

The fourth reason takes two minutes, costs nothing, and it is the one everyone skips. And the fifth is a dressing gown from seventeen sixty-nine that explains the last good thing you bought.

My name is Colette, and what I share here is how to read a room. I grew up in the Croix-Rousse in Lyon, in silk weavers' rooms four metres high, over thirteen feet, built for Jacquard looms and north light, not for people. My grandmother was a furnishing seamstress here for forty-one years. I spent fourteen years decorating small, dark, rented flats. Nothing I show you today is for sale.

Stay for the part about why your curtains look like nothing after dark, because it is the thing nobody tells you.

Before the method, one minute on why. Because in this house we do not move furniture without knowing what we are doing. Before you notice a single cushion, your body asks a room four questions. Is my back safe? What will I hear? Where can my eyes rest? How far does this go? The trade learned the answers by watching people, long before science named them.

Start with the back. My grandmother always knew which chair a family loved, because it was the one she was re-covering, worn through at the arms. Almost always it had a wall behind it and a view of the door.

In the flats I was hired to fix, the sofa faces the television with the hallway behind it, and the desk faces a wall with the whole room at your back. The sitting room has become a pièce de passage, a room you only walk through. For fourteen years I watched what happens next. Nobody is frightened. They shift, find reasons to stand up, and drift to the one corner with a wall behind it. A seat is chosen by its back, not by its cushion.

So ask of the sofa, the desk and the bed: is there something solid behind me, and can I see the door? If not, turn the desk, or stand something low and solid behind the sofa, a sideboard or a long table. Ten minutes, nothing to buy.

Quickly, while I have you. My grandmother kept a notebook for forty-one years, every window she measured, every colour she mixed, every drop and every width, in her own hand. She made me promise I would write it out properly before it was lost. So I did. Your back is safe. Now listen, because the second thing your body checks is not something it can see.

In the flats my grandmother worked in, every curtain was lined and every room had books. Now yours. A laminate floor, a glass coffee table, a bare window with a roller blind. Clap once and very often a thin, bright ringing comes back.

A hard, flat surface throws sound back almost whole. A soft, heavy one keeps some of it. An uneven one, books at different depths, breaks it up. In a room of hard, parallel surfaces, a single footstep comes back several times, from several directions. The name they gave this later is sensory gating. The nervous system filters out sound it can predict, which is why a ticking clock disappears after a few minutes in the same room. Reflections from everywhere at once are harder to filter, so the room never quite goes quiet. Every bare surface answers back.

My grandmother made curtains in this city for forty-one years, and she had a rule about this. A curtain that is not lined is only half made.

So clap. Close every curtain and clap again. Then fill your emptiest shelf with books, at different depths. Five minutes, nothing to buy.

That quiets the room for your ears. But your eyes have been working all evening too, and what tires them is the surfaces.

Almost nothing in an old French room is perfectly flat. The parquet has grain and eighty years of feet on it, and the curtain is linen. Now yours. Grey cupboard doors, a floor printed to look like oak, a polyester throw, all under one flat light in the middle of the ceiling.

Real material never repeats, so the eye can wander across it and keep finding small differences. My husband is a joiner, and he taught me to look for the same knot twice. On most printed floors you'll find it again not far along, because it is a handful of photographs of wood, printed over and over, and your eye notices before you do. The eye rests on what it cannot finish reading.

And here is the part nobody tells you. Texture is not in the material. It is in the light. Linen is a surface of tiny ridges, one for every thread. Light that strikes it head-on hides each ridge's shadow behind the ridge, and the linen reads flat as paper. Light skimming across it from a lamp close beside it throws every shadow out where you can see it, and the weave appears. One light in the middle of the ceiling falls on most things from above and in front, and skims across very little. That is why your curtains look like nothing after dark, and why, in this house, light always comes before texture. That lamp has a chapter to itself, page thirty, Table Height, The One That Does The Work, because it is the light that shows you what your things are made of.

Tonight, switch off the ceiling light and stand one lamp close beside the curtain. Five minutes, nothing to buy.

Your eyes can rest now. But they also need somewhere to go, and in most flats the furniture is in the way.

Where I learned my trade, the sofa stood off the wall, by a metre or by a hand. Now yours. Sofa, bookcase, bed and television unit all pressed to the walls, and a bare square of floor in the middle. It looks like moving day, the evening the removal men left.

This is the one everyone skips, because it sounds too small to matter. When a sofa touches the wall, the eye reads the two as one flat surface, as if the sofa were painted on. Pull it away and a dark seam of shadow appears. A shadow is how the eye knows one thing stands in front of another, and the moment it knows that, the wall moves back. The room is not a centimetre bigger. It reads deeper. It's the same shadow as the one in the linen. A shadow is the cheapest depth you will ever buy.

My rule, and it is mine, not a law of physics, is a hand's width, about eight centimetres, three inches. Pull the sofa out by that much. Two minutes, nothing to buy.

So the room is safe, quiet, restful and deep. Then you buy one good thing, and all of it starts to look wrong.

The French rooms I love most were never bought in a weekend. They were gathered. A table from one grandmother, a lamp from the year somebody married, a mirror nobody remembers buying. Now yours. You finally buy a solid oak dining table, and within a week the chairs around it look like plastic.

Denis Diderot wrote an essay about exactly this in seventeen sixty-nine. Given an elegant scarlet dressing gown, he found the study he had loved suddenly shabby around it. So he replaced the straw chair, then the desk, then the prints, and ended in debt. He had been the master of his old gown, he wrote, and had become the slave of the new one.

The eye never judges an object alone, only against its neighbour. In a room bought all at once, a single better thing raises the standard and everything else fails it together. A gathered room has no single standard, so the new thing joins the others instead of judging them. A matched room is always one purchase away from shabby.

So before the next good thing arrives, break the set. Bring in one object from another room, a chair from the kitchen, a picture from the bedroom. Fifteen minutes, nothing to buy.

None of this was ever secret. My grandmother knew all five of these things and never read a word of science. And one more, not on the list, for staying. Most homes have one piece that everything else is arranged around. The heavy armchair from your mother's flat, say. You keep it for what it cost, or for whose it was, and you buy cushions to apologise for it. My test, and it's mine: if you passed it tomorrow on the pavement with a sign saying take me, would you carry it up your stairs? If not, it isn't furniture any more. It is a promise you are keeping to somebody who never asked you to. And if you would, but it still looks wrong, it needs a new cover or a different room, not replacing.
