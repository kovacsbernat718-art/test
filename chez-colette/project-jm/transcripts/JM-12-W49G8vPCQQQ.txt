Most people open a cabinet one day and realize they have been keeping something for 30 years that they never once used. In an American house, that moment usually shows up when somebody is standing in a garage with too many trash bags, and by then it is too late. Japanese homes tend to avoid that moment altogether, and it has very little to do with discipline or being a naturally tidy person. Japan has simple rules about what comes into a house, what stays, and what goes, and those rules get taught early and used for a lifetime, so the pile never builds up in the first place. So, I want to go through 11 Japanese decluttering lessons that almost everyone learns far too late. And number 11 is the one that causes family arguments, so stay with me until the end because that is the one you cannot go back and fix. The first lesson is to stop saving your best things for later, and you already know exactly which things I mean. There is a set of China in your cabinet right now that has come out four times since the wedding it came from, a sweater in your closet with the tag still on it, and a bottle of perfume that has sat so long it has turned. Japanese people have a phrase for this, ichigo ichie, and it simply means one time, one meeting. What it tells you is that today only happens once, and it is never coming back. So, the special occasion you have been waiting for is the day you are already in. When somebody knocks on the door in Japan, they get the good cup, even if it is just a neighbor stopping by with no warning, because the household is not holding it back for a perfect occasion or better visitor because that never comes. In Japan, they use all the stuff they own in the present moment. Now, think about how you ended up doing the opposite, because you were sold two of everything and then told when you were allowed to use each one. You bought the everyday plates and the good plates, the everyday towels and the guest towels, the cheap glasses and the ones that live on the high shelf. You paid for both sets and then you spent 40 years using the worst half while the better half sat in a cabinet collecting dust and cluttering up your house. So, eat dinner off the good plates tonight and I mean tonight and not Sunday because Sunday has a way of turning into another 30 years. The second lesson is that your house was never too small and I understand that one is annoying to hear when you were standing in a hallway trying to get past a stack of boxes. In 1979, a European described Japanese homes as rabbit hutches and the phrase usagi goya stuck in Japan for decades afterward because people were genuinely stung by it. Set the insult aside and look at what it actually tells you because a family living in a third of your square footage does not have your clutter problem. They never did. The size of the building was never the thing creating the situation in your hallway. What Japanese homes mostly do not have is a basement, an attic, or a garage. And that one structural fact changes everything about how a household behaves. When there is no place to put off a decision, the decision gets made at the front door immediately by whoever is holding the object. Your house gave you three enormous rooms where nothing ever has to be decided. So, for 40 years you have been moving things into them instead of choosing and none of that is a character flaw. It is just what happens when a building offers you somewhere to hide from a question. Go count the rooms you genuinely live in during a normal week and most people find that number is three, maybe four, no matter how large the house is. Everything else is being heated, insured, cleaned, and paid for so that it can hold objects. If you want to test what you actually need, take the things you are unsure about, put them in one room, shut the door, and leave it alone for a month. If you never once went in there for anything, you have your answer. And the answer is that you were storing a feeling rather than a possession. That test costs you nothing, and that is true of every rule on this list, which is exactly why I gathered all of them together in one place. There are plenty more habits like it, small changes that keep a home calm and organized without you spending a cent, and they are all in my new book over at the JapaneseMethod.com. Now, the reason that closed room works so well is that nothing inside it was asking you for anything while the door was shut. And that brings us to the third lesson, which is that everything you own is asking you for something every single day, whether you touch it or not. There is a Japanese word for this, tiir, which literally means putting your hands in, and it describes the ongoing care an object requires of you for as long as it stays in your house. The interesting part is the direction of that word, because it describes the relationship from the object side. The knife needs sharpening. The garden needs tending. The wool needs airing out. You agreed to all of that at the register without anybody mentioning it to you. Open one ordinary drawer in your kitchen and count what is inside it. And most drawers come back somewhere between 40 and 70 items. Every one of those is a small standing decision that has to be dusted, sorted, moved when you were looking for something else, and eventually dealt with by somebody. Multiply that across a house, and you land at a number in the thousands, and then the tiredness you feel on a Saturday morning starts to make a lot more sense. You are not tired because you are getting older. You are tired because several thousand objects are all making a small request at the same time, and your brain has been answering them one by one for years. That is also why the pile of socks matters more than it looks like it should, because you own 22 pairs and you wear the same six. The other 16 are not neutral and they are not free, since they get washed, folded, put away, and pushed aside every single time you reach for the six you like. So, pick out the five things in your house that ask the most of you and be honest about whether each one is earning that. The fourth lesson is about the question you ask yourself while you are holding something, because the question most people use has no correct answer and that is exactly why they keep everything. Asking whether you might need this someday sends your mind out into a future that does not exist yet, where every object is potentially useful. So, the answer always comes back yes and the object always goes back on the shelf. You can be doing this for eight hours and end the day with a sore back and a house that looks identical. Japan gave the world a different question through one specific method and the word at the center of it is tokimeku, which describes the small flutter you feel in your chest. In English, that got translated as sparking joy and the translation is the reason the method falls apart for a lot of people over 60. A woman holding her late husband's toolbox does not feel joy and she does not feel nothing either. She feels about six things at once and not one of them is a clean yes or no. The original word is describing a physical reaction in the body rather than happiness and when your body has nothing clear to say, you need a question the calendar can answer instead. So, ask when you last put your hands on this thing and be specific about it, because a year is a year and there is no arguing with it. Then, ask the follow-up that does most of the work, which is whether you would feel disappointed or relieved if this item disappeared overnight through no fault of your own. Relief is information. Relief means that object has been asking you for something and giving you very little back and you have been paying that bill for years without opening the envelope. The fifth lesson is that you are storing other people's lives in your house, and they are storing yours in theirs. Japanese children grow up hearing one instruction more than almost any other, which is meiwaku o kakenai, do not become somebody else's burden. The word meiwaku covers the trouble you cause another person just by existing near them carelessly, and it is taken extremely seriously from about the age of four onward. Read that word in the direction nobody wants to read it. Because the boxes of your things sitting in your daughter's garage are meiwaku, and her boxes in your spare room are the same thing pointed back at you. Everybody in that arrangement is being polite, and everybody is losing space over it. I have been guilty of this myself, since I left several boxes at my mother's house for years and told myself they were important to me. She asked about them every single time I visited in a very nice way, which is its own kind of pressure. Then I finally asked myself the question that ended it, which was that if these things were not valuable enough to keep in my own house, there was no honest reason for them to be in hers. Helping somebody out for a few months while they move, or while their life is upside down, is a generous thing to do, and I am not telling you to stop. What you need to add is a date, said out loud, kindly, one time, with a plan attached to it. Without a date, it is not help anymore. It is just a slow transfer of the problem. And the person who ends up moving those boxes is usually the one who least wanted them there. The sixth lesson is that almost none of it is worth selling. And this one stops people dead when they hear how Japan handles it. When a Japanese family wants to get rid of a sofa, they pay the city to come take it away. You buy a sticker at the convenience store, you fill it out, you book a collection date, and you put the sofa outside on the right morning. The category even has its own name, sodai gomi, meaning oversized refuse. And the word got so familiar in the 1980s that people started using it as a joke about retired husbands sitting around the house all day. Nobody in that system is pretending they will recoup anything, and that honesty is a large part of why their houses empty out in a weekend while yours takes 4 years. Here in the States, we tell ourselves a different story, which is that all of this has value, and we simply have not gotten around to listing it. So, you photograph the dresser, you write the description, you pick a price, and then you spend 3 weeks talking to people who want it for half of that, plus two people who arrange a time and never show up. You paid for storage, you paid with your evenings, and the dresser is still in the basement. Selling is worth your time on a short list of things, and it is shorter than you want it to be. Real wood furniture, collectibles, working power tools, and electronics from the last 5 years or so. That is close to the whole list. The pine dresser is not on it, and neither is anything you have already photographed twice. For everything else, pick a number that feels right to you and use it as a rule rather than a suggestion. Anything you believe is worth more than that number, you list. Anything below it, you donate on the same day you decide without a second look. Then give the listings a deadline of 2 weeks, because an item that has not sold in 2 weeks is not going to sell at the price you have in your head, and you already know that. The seventh lesson is that letting go gets much easier when the thing becomes somebody's gain, and there is a Japanese custom built entirely around this called osusowake. It means sharing out a portion of what you were given. So, when somebody receives a box of fruit from their hometown, they pass some of it along to a neighbor without any ceremony about it. There is no giver and no receiver in that exchange, since the whole thing is understood as one item continuing along a line it was already traveling. You are not losing the object, you are moving it further down that line. This changes the feeling completely when it comes to the harder items, especially the ones connected to people. A sewing machine that goes to your granddaughter who actually sews is not gone. And the good tools that go to the young man next door who is fixing up his first house are not gone either. You will see them again and you will hear about them, which is more than you can say for anything in a box in the attic. It works on a house, too, and I have watched it happen more than once. A couple who raised four children in the same place for 38 years came very close to calling off their move. Not because of the money and not because of the paperwork, but because they could not stand the idea of the house belonging to strangers. The buyers asked to come by early to learn how everything worked. The heating, the sprinklers, the trick with the back door, and everyone sat at the kitchen table for 2 hours. The couple wrote to me afterward and said that meeting the buyers and seeing how excited they were made the whole thing possible. And the wife told me she finally stopped feeling like she was abandoning the place. The eighth lesson is that you should never own more than you can personally clean. And the clearest example of this is sitting in every school in Japan. Japanese schools employ almost no janitors because the students clean the classrooms themselves along with the hallways and the bathrooms every single day for 12 years straight. There is a set time in the schedule for it called souji no jikan and everybody does it including the teachers. A whole country grows up with a cloth in hand learning one lesson very early, which is that owning something and cleaning it are the same activity and always have been. Nobody taught you that and there is no reason you should have figured it out on your own. So, your house quietly filled up past the point your body can service it, and you have been carrying the difference ever since in the form of guilt on Saturday mornings. Walk through your rooms and count the surfaces you genuinely wipe down in a normal week, and then count the surfaces that exist. The gap between those two numbers is the part of your house that is technically yours and functionally abandoned. The right amount to own is the amount you can maintain on a bad day, when your back hurts, and you slept poorly, and you have somewhere to be at 2:00 in the afternoon. Most people set their limit based on their best possible day, which almost never happens. So, the house is permanently running at a level nobody can sustain. So, take one shelf tonight, take everything off it, and leave that shelf empty for a while, since a shelf with three things on it gets wiped down, and a shelf with 30 things on it gets skipped for 2 years. The ninth lesson is that half of what you were keeping belongs to somebody you used to be. There is a word for what is happening here, shuchaku, which comes out of Buddhist teaching and means clinging. And the teaching is very precise about where the problem sits. I did this with painting supplies for about 9 years. I used to paint on weekends, and I was reasonably good at it. Then life changed, and I stopped. But I kept every brush, three unopened canvases, and a wooden box of paints that had gone solid. Every time I saw that box, I felt slightly bad about myself, which is a strange thing to pay rent on. Throwing it out did not erase the years I spent painting, because those years are not in the box, and they never were. Go look in the closet for the clothes cut for a life you no longer live. The business suits with 30 years of work in the shoulders, the size you used to be, and the sewing machine from the year you were going to become a person who sews. The career those suits belonged to was already finished before you opened the closet door, and the shelf of business books is the same story, since every one of them was useful once. And what you actually want to read about now is gardening or travel or your grandchildren. The hardest version of this is a room belonging to somebody who died, kept exactly the way they left it. And I want to be careful here, because that room is doing something for you. Keep the things that give you comfort when you hold them, and I mean really hold them, and let go of the things that only produce the ache without the comfort. Take a photograph of anything you are unsure about, because the memory was never stored in the object, and a photograph holds it perfectly well. You are allowed to become somebody else at 73. And for most people, that permission turns out to be the entire thing they were waiting for. The 10th lesson is the one that almost nobody says out loud, and it is that the window for doing any of this yourself closes, and nobody sends you a notice about it. Every other lesson on this list is about what to let go of. And this one is about when your hands and your knees are still your own to use. Japan treats this as a normal stage of adult life, rather than a defeat. And they have a whole category of home modification for it that gets planned and subsidized through their care system, where they remove thresholds, widen doorways, and add rails. The first thing that comes out during that work is almost never a doorstep, since the first thing to go is whatever is sitting on the floor of the hallway. For anybody past 65, a fall at home is the single event most likely to end independent living. And most of those falls happen over something lying on the floor of a room that person has walked through 10,000 times. You know where everything is, so you step around it without thinking, which works perfectly until the night you get up at 2:00 in the morning and do not turn the light on. The boxes you can move at 68 are boxes you cannot move at 81. And the distance between those two ages is one bad stair or one hospital stay. This is also the version of too late that cannot be undone by trying harder later. Everything else on this list you can get to next year, but this one runs on a schedule your body sets without telling you. So, tonight, walk the route you take from your bed to the bathroom and walk it the way you walk it in the dark and then clear that route completely. Nothing on the floor, nothing narrowing the doorway, nothing with a cord across it, and do not worry about the rest of the house yet because that one path matters more than every other lesson on this list combined. The 11th lesson is that if you do not deal with your possessions, your family will deal with them and it will cost them far more than any of it is worth. Japan has an entire industry for this called ihen seiri, made up of companies that go into the homes of people who have died and clear out everything left behind. And that industry exists because the volume of unsorted belongings became more than families could handle on their own. What Japanese people started doing in response is the useful part. They buy a notebook. It is called an ending note, an ending note, and you can buy one in an ordinary stationery store alongside the calendars and the pens. It is not a will and there is no lawyer and no cost beyond the notebook itself. You write down what you own that has value, what you want done with it, who gets what, and any wishes you have about the rest in your own handwriting, and you tell somebody where it is. Japanese people buy that notebook specifically so that their children never have to hire the company. The reason this is the lesson that splits families is that the arguments are almost never about money. They are about a ring with no note attached to it and a chair that two people both remember sitting in with the same person. When your voice is not in the room, every object turns into a negotiation between people who are grieving, jet-lagged, and running out of time. And the calmest family in the world can come apart under those conditions. I have watched it happen inside my own family, and the thing people fought over was not expensive at all. My grandmother handled this better than anybody I have known. She called everyone over to the house while she was still perfectly well, went through the rooms with us, and told us out loud who was getting what and why and where each thing had come from in the first place. It took one Saturday afternoon, and there was nothing left to argue about afterward, because she had already answered every question anybody would have thought to ask. What she gave us was not really the objects at all. It was a day with no decisions left in it during a week when none of us would have been in any condition to make them. When something comes up that none of these 11 lessons obviously covers, there are three questions that will get you to an answer on almost anything. The first one is whether you are keeping this because you want it or because deciding about it is harder than finding a spot for it. The second is whether you could get another one tomorrow if you truly needed it, and what that would honestly cost you. The third is whether this belongs to the life you are living now or to a version of yourself that is already finished. Please do not try to do all 11 of these this weekend, and I mean that, because turning it into one enormous exhausting weekend is exactly what causes people to give up and never come back to it. Do the first one tonight, which just means eating your dinner off the good plates, and then walk your hallway before bed and pick up whatever is on the floor. That is a real start, and it takes about 15 minutes. Everybody learns these 11 lessons eventually, and the only thing that changes from one house to the next is whether you learn them while you are holding the object yourself or whether your daughter learns them while she is holding it for you.
