Before the method, one minute on why. Because in this house we do not move furniture without knowing what we are doing.

A room is not a picture. You do not stand in the doorway and look at it. You sit down in it, at one particular height, facing one particular direction, for three hours at a time, usually in the evening, usually under electric light. Almost everything that makes a room feel good is about what happens at that height, in that chair, at that hour. And almost everything you have been shown about decorating was photographed from the doorway, standing up, in the middle of the day. That gap is the whole problem. You have been given instructions for the picture and then asked why the room does not work.

Number one. Your back.

Look again at that chair nobody uses. In a French apartment, a seat is placed so the person in it has something solid behind their shoulders and can see the door. Not for superstition. Because you cannot settle in a chair when part of your attention is being spent behind you, and you will not notice you are spending it. You will simply find that you prefer the other chair, and you will assume it is more comfortable. It probably is not.

Now here is what is actually in most rooms. Every piece of furniture pushed flat against the wall, a clear square of carpet in the middle, and one seat placed with its back to the doorway because that is where it fitted. That is not a sitting room, ma chère. That is a waiting area. A doctor's waiting area is arranged in exactly that shape and for exactly that reason — it is designed so nobody settles.

So pull the seating in off the wall. Five centimetres, fifteen, two inches to six. Not into the middle of the room — just off the wall, enough that the room closes around the seats instead of hugging the edges. Then turn the odd chair so it faces the seating rather than the television, and check that everyone can see the door.

My rule, and it is mine, not a law of physics: no two people should sit more than two point four metres apart, about eight feet. Past that, everybody raises their voice very slightly, all evening, and nobody ever says so.

Four minutes. It costs nothing. That one is on page one hundred, and it is called Pull It Off The Wall, because it is the correction people refuse to believe until they do it.

Number two. The one good thing that ruins everything else.

You save up. You buy the sofa, the proper one, the one you have wanted for two years. It is delivered, it is beautiful, and within about a week the coffee table beside it looks cheap, the rug looks tired, the curtains look like curtains that came with the flat. Nothing else changed. You are not imagining it and you are not being greedy.

This is old, and it is French, which is why I am fond of it. In seventeen sixty-nine, Denis Diderot wrote a short essay about being given a fine new dressing gown. He put it on, and his old study — the one he had been perfectly happy in — became shabby around him. So he replaced the chair. Then the desk, then the prints on the wall, and he wrote that he had been the master of his old dressing gown and had become the slave of the new one. He ended in debt over a gown.

The mechanism is simple. A room reads by comparison, not by absolutes. Nothing in it is expensive or cheap on its own; everything is expensive or cheap next to the thing beside it. So a single upgrade does not raise the room. It lowers everything you did not upgrade.

My grandmother's rule was this: buy in the order of what is worn out, never in the order of what is wanted. And here is the correction, which is free. When the new thing arrives and the room turns on you, do not buy the next thing. Wait three weeks. Almost every time, what you were reacting to was the newness, not the quality — and in three weeks the sofa stops shouting and the room comes back.

Nothing to buy, three weeks to wait. That one has a chapter to itself, on page one hundred and fourteen, called Nothing That Came In A Set.

That is what happens when you buy. Now here is what happens when you do not buy anything at all, and the room still puts you on edge.

Number three. What the room sounds like.

You have been in this room. It photographs beautifully. Pale, calm, everything in its place. And after twenty minutes in it you want to leave, and you cannot say why, because there is nothing to point at.

Go and stand in the middle of your room and clap once, hard. Listen to what happens after the clap. If there is a short bright ring afterwards, a little tail of sound off the walls, you have found it. Hard floor, flat plaster, glass, a low table and not much else — the sound has nothing to land in, so it bounces, and every footstep, every chair leg, every plate set down is slightly sharper than it should be. You are not hearing a room. You are being mildly startled by one, three hundred times an evening.

The trade has known the fix for as long as there have been curtains. Soft, heavy and vertical. A lined curtain, hung wide and dropped to the floor, is the largest piece of soft material in most homes and it does more to quiet a room than anything else you own. Then a rug with some weight. Then a bookcase with actual books in it, which is the most effective sound-absorbing object most people already have and do not know it.

A room you can hear yourself think in has soft things at three heights — floor, wall and window — exactly like light.

You can test this tonight for nothing. Hang a blanket over the back of the sofa, close the curtains, and clap again. That is your answer. The curtain chapter is page one hundred and sixteen, and it is there because the curtain is doing four jobs and most people buy it for one.

Sound handled. Now the one nobody expects, which is the reason a room you styled carefully stops speaking to you.

Number four. The room that was finished all at once.

You did it properly. One weekend, one shop, or one very good afternoon on the internet. Everything arrived, everything matched, and for about a month it was exactly right. And now you walk past it and feel nothing at all, and you assume you have got bored, or that you are shallow.

You are not. A room assembled in one transaction has nothing left to reveal. Every object in it was chosen by one person, in one mood, in one week, so there is no second layer — nothing that arrived from somewhere else, nothing you have to explain, nothing with a mark on it. Your eye reads the whole thing in four seconds and then has no further business there.

Now think about the homes that made your shoulders come down. I will wager every one of them contained something obviously older than the rest. A chair that did not match. A jug that was clearly somebody's grandmother's.

My rule, and again it is mine: every room needs one thing that is older than everything else in it, and one thing that cannot be bought new. Not antiques. A wooden board, a chipped plate you actually use, a photograph that is not framed to match.

And there is a reason it has to be genuinely older and not merely styled to look it. Wear is not a finish. It is a record of use, and it falls in the places a hand actually goes — the arm of a chair, the edge of a board, the rim of a jug. A manufactured version puts the wear everywhere evenly, which is the one place real wear never is, and your eye catches that in about a second without ever telling you what it caught.

That takes a week of looking, in your own cupboards, and it costs nothing at all.

That is what your eye does over months. This next one is what it is doing every single evening, and it is the one I was called out for more than any other.

Number five. One light, at one height.

Stand in your sitting room this evening with the lights as you normally have them, and find the shadow of something solid — a jug, a lamp base, the arm of a chair. If it has one hard shadow, or no shadow at all, your room is lit from one place, and that place is almost certainly the ceiling.

An overhead light is the worst position available in a home. It is above every face in the room, so it puts a shadow in every eye socket, and it lights the tops of things — the top of the table, the top of your head, the top of the sofa back. Everything you actually look at is underlit, and everybody in the room looks tired. That is not the room being ugly. That is the room being lit like a corridor.

Light has to arrive at three heights. Overhead, which you switch off. Table height, beside where people sit, which is the one that does the work. And floor level, low in a corner, behind something — which almost nobody has, and which is the single fastest change in this entire video.

And read the bulbs. Twenty-seven hundred kelvin, and nothing above three thousand, anywhere you sit. Above that you are living under shop lighting, and a shop is lit so you will look at objects, not so you will stay.

Move one lamp onto the floor tonight and switch the big light off. Twenty minutes, no money. The numbers are collected on page thirty-three, and the bulb you want is on page thirty-four, because it is the cheapest correction in the whole book and people still buy the wrong one.

Light is the correction. This next one is the obstruction — the thing in the room that no amount of correcting will fix, because it should not be there.

Number six. The thing you are keeping out of guilt.

Look around and find it. You already know what it is. The enormous armchair that cost a great deal eight years ago. The dark carved table you inherited, that fits nothing you own now and that you cannot picture ever liking. The lamp from somebody who is no longer here.

You keep it, and the reasons all sound responsible. It is still perfectly good. It was expensive. It was hers.

Here is what it is actually doing. It is setting the scale of the room, because it is the largest or the heaviest thing in it, and everything else is being arranged around an object you do not like. You are decorating in service of a mistake. And every evening, a small part of you registers it and looks away, which is a thing you have been doing for so long you no longer notice you are doing it.

So: the money is gone. It went eight years ago, and keeping the chair does not bring any of it back. It only charges you rent in floor space and in attention. And an inherited thing is not the person. If you loved her, keep the small object — the sewing box, the photograph, the jug. You do not have to keep the sideboard to keep your mother.

The assignment is one afternoon. Take it out of the room, do not sell it yet, put it somewhere else and live for two weeks without it. If you do not miss it, you have your answer, and you have it for free.

That is the obstruction gone. Now the last one, which is what most people do to a small room and it is exactly backwards.

Number seven. The empty middle.

A room feels small, so you push everything to the walls, because clearing the floor must make the room bigger. It is the most logical thing in the world and it is wrong every time.

An empty middle does not read as space. It reads as a room that has been cleared for something — a dance, a viewing, a removal van. The eye has nothing to stop on, so it goes straight to the walls and measures them, and measuring the walls is exactly what you did not want it to do.

What actually makes a small room feel larger is a clear route through it and depth in front of it. Leave one walking lane, at least seventy-five centimetres, about thirty inches, from one side to the other, and keep it truly clear. Then let the furniture sit off the walls in a group with a rug under it — and the rug must be big enough that the front legs of every seat stand on it, or the whole arrangement floats and reads as temporary.

And leave one corner where nothing happens. Not storage, not a plant, not a floor lamp. One empty corner. A room with nowhere for the eye to rest is a room you cannot rest in either.

Half an hour, and it costs you nothing but your afternoon. Page one hundred and forty-seven is the whole diagnosis for a sitting room, and this is fault one on it.

Now let me say the quiet part about who profits from you not knowing this.

Think about where you have learned what a good room looks like. Not from a room. From a photograph of a room. And now think about how that photograph was made.
