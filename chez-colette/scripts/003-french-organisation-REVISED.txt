There is a basket of clean laundry in your home, and you have been getting dressed out of it since Sunday. You are not behind on the washing. It is done, and folded. The clothes have nowhere to go, because the place they are meant to go is full of clothes you cannot wear this month. That basket is not the problem. It is the first of nine small French rules your home breaks every day, and between them they have cost you every Saturday you spent tidying a house that looked the same by Wednesday.

And at some point you made your peace with it. Some people are tidy, you thought, and I'm just not one of them.

Now, one fact. Between eighteen fifty-three and eighteen seventy, Paris was rebuilt under a prefect called Georges-Eugène Haussmann, who wrote the dimensions into law. Six floors, no more, and apartment ceilings of two point eight to three point two metres, about nine to ten and a half feet. They were not generous. They were tall, and everything a family owned went up the wall. For years I worked in studios of twenty-eight square metres, about three hundred square feet. Some were calm. Some looked like your basket. And the calm ones did not belong to calmer people.

So hear this first, ma chère. You are not lazy, and you are not messy. Your rooms tell your hands to put things down in all the wrong places, and nobody ever taught you to hear them.

Nine rules, then. Number two is about a mirror, and why the one in your hall shows you your mess twice. Number five is the one everybody skips, and it's why the other eight stop working by spring. And number seven will change what you think a dining table is for.

My name is Colette, and what I share here is how to read a room. I grew up in the Croix-Rousse in Lyon, in a building made for the canuts, the silk weavers, with ceilings four metres high, about thirteen feet, because the looms were. My grandmother spent forty-one years as a couturière d'ameublement, a furnishing seamstress, inside hundreds of other people's homes. I spent fourteen years decorating the small, rented, badly renovated ones. One honest word about the title. Nobody in France ever wrote these down as nine rules. She insisted on them, and I have only numbered them. And not one fix in this video is for sale.

Stay to the end for the one I did not number. It is a hook, and it takes less than a minute.

One minute before the first rule, because a rule you do not understand is one you abandon inside a fortnight. Your hands do not decide where things go. The room does. A hand carrying something puts it down at hand height, on something flat, in whatever light there is. I have looked at a great many piles, and that is where they form. So we are not organising your things. We are correcting what your room tells your hands, in the usual order. Light, then height, then texture, then colour.

The first rule is the window. In a French flat, the window usually opens inward, like a door, so anything standing on the sill gets knocked over the first time you open it. Now look at yours. If it slides, nothing ever forced it clear, so it became a shelf. A plant in its plastic pot. A candle burnt down to the tin. The post. A charger. In my trade we learn that the eye goes first to the brightest part of a room, and in daylight that is the window. Anything in it is lit from behind and turns into a flat dark shape. Whatever stands in the window is the loudest thing in the room. My grandmother's rule was that a window is for light, not for keeping. Clear one sill tonight. Two minutes, and it costs nothing. And once the light is in, one object in your home exists to catch it, and yours is probably facing the wrong way.

Quickly, while I have you. My grandmother kept a notebook for forty-one years, every window she measured, every colour she mixed, every drop and every width, in her own hand. She made me promise I would write it out properly before it was lost. So I did. Every rule and every measurement in one place, in a book called Colette's Home Bible, and the link is in the description below. Now. Back to the room. That object is your mirror, and it has been working against you.

In the old flats of my city, the big mirror hangs over the fireplace or between two windows, where it has daylight to catch and throw back into the room. That is the second rule. Now stand in your hall and look at what your mirror faces. Coats on the hooks, three deep. The shoes by the door. A bag nobody has unpacked since Sunday. Whatever is in front of it, it doubles, so the pile by your door becomes two piles, and the second one is at eye height. A mirror doubles whatever it faces, so choose what it faces. Turn it, move it, or lean it where it catches a window, a lamp, or a picture you love. Five minutes, nothing to buy. So much for the light. Now look up, at the one place in your home nobody looks, which is why things live there for years.

The third rule is the tops of things. The French for the height of a room is hauteur sous plafond, ceiling height, and it is the measurement Haussmann wrote into the city. In the flats I grew up in, nothing was kept on top of anything. Now look at yours. The wardrobe, with two suitcases, a shoebox of cables and a lampshade still in its plastic. My rule, and it is mine, not a law of physics: the eye takes the top of your tallest furniture as the top of the room. Whatever you store on top of a wardrobe, you take off the height of the room. So it comes down, and it goes behind a door, or it goes. Ten minutes, a step stool, nothing to buy. And behind which door? One you already have, hiding space because of the order your clothes hang in.

My grandmother made curtains in this city for forty-one years, and she had a rule about this. Clothes hang by their length, longest at one end, shortest at the other. That is the fourth rule. Now open your wardrobe. A long coat, a shirt, a dress, a jacket, another coat. Every long thing drops a column of fabric nearly to the floor, so the space under the short things is chopped into narrow gaps you cannot use. Sort by length and those gaps join into one clear space under the short end, often tall enough for a small chest of drawers or all your shoes. Hang by the length, and the wardrobe hands you back a shelf. You know this one already. It is the same rule as the curtains. The drop decides everything. Fifteen minutes, and it costs nothing. It will also show you how much of that rail you can't wear for months.

Which is the fifth rule, and the one everybody skips. In the homes I grew up around, the wardrobe held one season at a time. Twice a year came le grand ménage, the deep seasonal clean, and the clothes changed over with it. Winter went away, folded and clean, and summer came out. Now look at your rail. Swimsuits beside a wool coat. Sandals under a ski jacket. All of it so tight you force a hand in. A wardrobe holding the whole year has the wrong half of it in your way every morning. If you can't wear it this month, it doesn't live where you dress. No cupboard to spare? That is page one hundred and forty, Storage, Without Building Anything, because the flats I worked in rarely had one either. This is the one that makes the other eight hold, because it takes a whole season off what they have to carry. One afternoon, nothing to buy.

And what you put that season in matters as much as where, because most people reach for plastic, and my grandmother would have stopped them at the door. The sixth rule was hers: cloth must breathe. Wool and linen went away clean, in cotton bags or an old sheet, in a cupboard with a little air. Now think of your closet shelf. A plastic tub with a clip lid, packed with sweaters. A duvet in a vacuum bag, squeezed flat as a board. Plastic does keep the dust out. But if there was any damp in that wool the day you shut the lid, it has nowhere to go until you open it again, months later. Plastic keeps the dust out and the damp in. An old pillowcase keeps the dust off and lets the air through. She kept her sheets that way too, each set folded inside its own pillowcase, so a whole bed came out at once. One evening, and you already own the pillowcases.

So the cupboards can breathe. Now the biggest surface you own, and the seventh rule. In the flats I grew up in, the table was laid for dinner every evening. Plates, glasses, a jug of water, a cloth on Sundays. So every evening, whether anybody meant to tidy or not, it was cleared to the wood. Now picture yours. A laptop and its charger. The post, with the bills at the bottom. A school project drying on newspaper. A pharmacy bag, still stapled shut. It has become the place things go to wait. Here is the whole secret. A surface cleared every day, for a reason that has nothing to do with tidying, can never hold more than a day. A table that is laid every evening can never become a desk. No dining table at all? Then it is whatever you eat at. Lay it tonight, even for one. Five minutes, and you own everything you need. Then look at the sofa, the one piece of furniture in your home you have to unpack before you can use it.

In the sitting rooms I worked in here, and this is the eighth rule, you could sit down without moving anything first. Two cushions, perhaps three, and a throw over one arm. Now look at yours. Seven cushions in a row like a jury. A knitted throw, a fringed throw, and the fake fur one somebody gave you. Every evening they go on the floor so you can sit, and every morning they go back. An object that moves twice a day is out of place half the time. If you have to move it to sit down, it is not decoration. It is a chore you bought. Put all but two in a cupboard for a week. Five minutes, and it costs nothing. In fourteen years I never once saw anyone ask for the seventh one back. Which two to keep, and why, is on page one hundred and seventeen, Cushions And Throws, because the answer is almost never the newest two. And with the sofa clear, you will see the last thing, which I kept until now on purpose.

Colour comes last, because by then a great many rooms no longer need it. The ninth rule. In the flats I worked on, when a room needed paint at all, the storage went the colour of the wall behind it. Now look at yours. A white drawer unit against a green wall. A dark bookcase against cream. A black metal shelf against white. The eye goes to edges, the lines where one colour stops and the next begins. A cupboard in another colour draws a rectangle round itself and says, here is where the things are. In the wall's colour it has no edges, and the eye slides past. A cupboard the colour of the wall is a wall. In any other colour, it is furniture. And match the sheen as well, because a shine catches light the wall does not, and brings the edge straight back. One piece of your own, one weekend, the smallest tin they sell.

Now the part that costs me something to say. If four people live in two rooms, there is more life than cupboard, and no rule I have ever learned changes that arithmetic. And here is where money wins. My husband is a menuisier-ébéniste, a joiner and cabinetmaker, and he will tell you a cupboard built to measure, floor to ceiling, uses space no ready-made piece can reach. If you own your home, it is money well spent. Two warnings, ma chère, to protect your home. Anything tall and heavy gets fixed to the wall, because with its drawers out it can tip forward, and if you rent, that is a permission worth asking for. Never push a wardrobe tight against an outside wall; leave a few centimetres, an inch or two, for air. And black spots or a damp smell behind one? Don't paint over it. Call someone who can find where the water is coming from.

And now the part that nobody selling you anything will ever say out loud. Walk into any shop that sells storage and count. Clear boxes with clip lids, in every size. Boxes on wheels for under the bed. Bags you flatten with a vacuum cleaner. Drawer dividers, hanging pockets, baskets, and labels for the baskets. Every one of them is the same purchase. A place to put a thing you already own. Not one of them takes anything out of your home. Every one of them brings something in.

And watch what the first one does. Three matching boxes on one shelf, and the shelf beside it suddenly looks shabby, so you buy for that shelf too, and then for the cupboard, and by then you are not organising, you are matching. In fourteen years I never once saw the boxes stop at one shelf. The first box is never the last, and that is not a fault in the design. It is the design.

Nobody decided this in a room somewhere, ma chère. It is quieter and sadder than that. A shop sells what people walk in asking for, and people walk in believing their problem is a shortage of boxes. There is no money in a bare windowsill and a wardrobe hung by length. There is a great deal of money in a new box for every problem, bought again each time the problem grows. What your grandmother knew simply stopped being handed down, because nobody who sells storage earns a thing from teaching it. Nobody kept it from you. It simply stopped being worth anybody's while to say.

Now, the one I did not number, because it is not a rule your home ever had. My grandmother kept a single hook on the back of her bedroom door. Whatever she had worn once aired on it overnight, and by morning it was in the wardrobe or in the wash. So look at the end of your bed. That heap is not laziness, ma chère. A thing worn once is too clean for the wash and not clean enough for the wardrobe, so nobody ever gave it a place, and it takes the nearest flat thing that will hold it. Every pile in your home is a category nobody built a place for. So, one hook on the back of the door, the kind that needs no drill. One, because one is exactly the size that category should be.

Nine rules, and four sentences to keep, in this order. Nothing stands in front of the light. Height is used behind a door, never on top. Cloth breathes, and nothing you eat at or sit on is ever buried. Storage wears the colour of the wall. Light, then height, then texture, then colour.
